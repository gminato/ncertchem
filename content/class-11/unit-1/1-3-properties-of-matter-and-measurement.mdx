## Physical and chemical properties

Every substance has its own set of characteristic properties. Some are **physical properties**: colour, odour, melting point, boiling point and density. You can observe or measure them without changing what the substance is. Others are **chemical properties**: how a substance burns, or whether it reacts with an acid or a base. Burning, for example, turns methane into carbon dioxide and water: $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$. You only learn those by making the substance change into something else.

## Measurement and the SI system

A measured quantity is always a number followed by a unit. A wall that is 3.5 m long has the number 3.5 and the unit metre. Early on, scientists used two separate systems, the English system and the metric system. The metric system was based on tens, so it was easier to use, and it became the basis for a single international standard. That standard is the **International System of Units**, abbreviated **SI**, which was established by the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1960.

SI has seven **base quantities**, each with its own base unit. Every other physical quantity, such as speed, volume or density, is derived from these seven.

- Length: metre (m)
- Mass: kilogram (kg)
- Time: second (s)
- Electric current: ampere (A)
- Thermodynamic temperature: kelvin (K)
- Amount of substance: mole (mol)
- Luminous intensity: candela (cd)

The mole deserves attention in this course. One mole contains exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities. That fixed number is the Avogadro constant, expressed in mol⁻¹.

SI also uses prefixes to name multiples and submultiples of a unit. A kilometre is $10^{3}$ metres, a centimetre is $10^{-2}$ metres, and a milligram is $10^{-3}$ grams. The prefixes you will meet most often in chemistry are kilo (k), milli (m), micro (µ) and nano (n).

## Mass and weight

Mass and weight are different quantities, and chemistry depends on keeping them apart. **Mass** is the amount of matter in a body. **Weight** is the force that gravity exerts on that body. Mass is the same wherever you go. Weight changes with the strength of gravity.

A laboratory balance measures mass accurately, and the gram is the unit you will use most, because reactions use small amounts of chemicals. The Scrolly below follows one 60 kg block from an Earth balance to a Moon spring scale.

<Scrolly animation="mass-weight">
<Step>
Start on Earth with a 60 kg block on a beam balance. The balance compares the block with standard masses. Both pans feel the same gravity, so the beam stays level and the balance reads **60 kg**. That reading is the mass, the amount of matter in the block.
</Step>
<Step>
Carry the same block to the Moon and hang it from a spring scale. The spring now shows a smaller pull, which is the weight, the gravitational force on the block. Its weight is **97.2 N**. The block has lost no matter, so its mass is still 60 kg. Only the gravity changed.
</Step>
<Step>
Weight is mass times gravitational acceleration: $W = m \times g$. With $g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ on Earth, the block weighs 588 N. With $g = 1.62\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ on the Moon, it weighs 97.2 N. Mass is the constant, and weight follows $g$. A balance can therefore report the same mass on any planet.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Volume

**Volume** is the space a substance occupies. Its SI unit is the cubic metre, but that is far too large for a laboratory. Chemists usually use cubic centimetres (cm³) or cubic decimetres (dm³). For liquids and solutions, the common unit is the **litre** (L), which is not an SI unit.

$$
1\ \text{L} = 1000\ \text{mL}, \qquad 1000\ \text{cm}^{3} = 1\ \text{dm}^{3}
$$

You measure liquid volumes with graduated cylinders, burettes and pipettes. A volumetric flask is the tool for preparing a solution of a known volume.

## Density

Mass and volume together give **density**, the mass per unit volume of a substance:

$$
\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}
$$

The SI unit of density is kg m⁻³, which is a large unit for everyday use. Chemists usually quote density in g cm⁻³. A density of 1 g cm⁻³ equals 1000 kg m⁻³, because one gram is $10^{-3}$ kg and one cubic centimetre is $10^{-6}$ m³. A high density means the particles are packed more closely.

## Temperature

Three scales are in common use: degree Celsius (°C), degree Fahrenheit (°F) and kelvin (K). The kelvin is the SI unit. The Celsius scale puts the freezing point of water at 0 °C and its boiling point at 100 °C. On the Fahrenheit scale those two points are 32 °F and 212 °F.

The scales are related by two simple formulas:

$$
^{\circ}\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}\,{}^{\circ}\text{C} + 32 \qquad\qquad \text{K} = {}^{\circ}\text{C} + 273.15
$$

Kelvin is shifted so that its zero lies at the lowest possible temperature. A temperature in °C can be negative, but a temperature in kelvin cannot. Watch the three thermometers below as one liquid column moves through the ice point, body temperature, boiling point, and the one place where the two Celsius and Fahrenheit numbers agree.

<Scrolly animation="thermometer-trio">
<Step>
Begin at the ice point of water, where it freezes: **0 °C**. Read the same height on the other scales and you get **273.15 K** and **32 °F**. Kelvin's numbers are always positive, because its zero is the lowest temperature possible.
</Step>
<Step>
Body temperature, about **37 °C**, is **310.15 K** or **98.6 °F**. The liquid stands at the same height on all three thermometers. Only the numbers change. A rise of one degree Celsius is also a rise of one kelvin, but it is 1.8 degrees Fahrenheit.
</Step>
<Step>
Water boils at **100 °C**, which is **373.15 K** or **212 °F**. The span from freezing to boiling is 100 Celsius degrees, but 180 Fahrenheit degrees, because one Fahrenheit degree is only 5/9 of a Celsius degree.
</Step>
<Step>
At **−40 °C** the two scales read the same number, **−40 °F**. This is the only temperature where they agree. Below it the Fahrenheit reading is lower than the Celsius one. The kelvin reading is 233.15 K, and the lowest possible kelvin reading is 0 K, which is −273.15 °C.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- A physical property is measured without changing the substance; a chemical property needs a chemical change.
- Every measurement is a number and a unit. SI has seven base quantities, and every other quantity is derived from them.
- Mass is the amount of matter and does not change; weight is $m \times g$ and changes with gravity.
- Density is mass divided by volume. The SI unit is kg m⁻³, and 1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³.
- $K = {}^{\circ}\text{C} + 273.15$ and ${}^{\circ}\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}\,{}^{\circ}\text{C} + 32$. The Celsius and Fahrenheit scales agree at −40.
</KeyIdeas>
