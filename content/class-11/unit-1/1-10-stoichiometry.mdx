<Hook>
Stir a spoon of baking soda into a glass of lemon juice and it fizzes hard, then gradually goes quiet. One ingredient has run out. Could you have predicted, before stirring, which one would run out and how much gas would be made?
</Hook>

The word **stoichiometry** comes from two Greek words: *stoicheion*, meaning element, and *metron*, meaning measure. It is the part of chemistry that measures how much of each substance is used and made in a reaction. Everything starts from one source of information: the balanced chemical equation.

<Scrolly animation="reaction-reading">
<Step>
Read the equation $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ as a recipe for molecules. One molecule of methane meets two molecules of oxygen and gives one molecule of carbon dioxide and two of water. The numbers in front of the formulas are **coefficients**, and they count molecules taking part.
</Step>
<Step>
A single molecule is far too small to count, so chemists count in **moles**. One mole is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles. The coefficients are the same whether you count molecules or moles, so the equation also reads as $1\ \text{mol}$ of methane with $2\ \text{mol}$ of oxygen giving $1\ \text{mol}$ of carbon dioxide and $2\ \text{mol}$ of water.
</Step>
<Step>
Each mole has a mass, its molar mass in grams per mole, found from the atomic masses. Methane is $16.04\ \text{g}$ per mole and oxygen $32.00\ \text{g}$ per mole. So the reactants give $16.04\ \text{g}$ of CH$_4$ and $64.00\ \text{g}$ of O$_2$, and the products give $44.01\ \text{g}$ of CO$_2$ and $36.03\ \text{g}$ of H$_2$O.
</Step>
<Step>
Add up each side and you get the same answer: $80.04\ \text{g}$ in and $80.04\ \text{g}$ out. This is the law of conservation of mass written in grams. Every stoichiometric calculation follows the same path: use the coefficients to get moles, then the molar mass to get grams.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Balancing a chemical equation

A balanced equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, because atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical change. You change coefficients to achieve this, never subscripts. Changing a subscript would change the substance itself.

For many equations, trial and error works. Take the combustion of ethane, $\ce{C2H6}$, in air. A workable order is to balance carbon first, then hydrogen, and finally oxygen, which often appears on both sides of the arrow:

$$
\ce{C2H6 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \ce{2C2H6 + 7O2 -> 4CO2 + 6H2O}
$$

Count again: 4 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms and 14 oxygen atoms on each side. Balanced.

## 1.10.1 Limiting reagent

Reactions in the laboratory rarely start with exactly the ratio the equation asks for. Usually one reactant is in excess, and the other is used up first. The reactant that runs out first is the **limiting reagent**, and it fixes how much product can form. Once it is gone the reaction stops, however much of the other reactant remains.

Take $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$ with 6 mol of hydrogen and 2 mol of oxygen. The equation needs two hydrogen molecules for every oxygen molecule, so 6 mol of hydrogen could react with 3 mol of oxygen, but only 2 mol is present. Oxygen limits the reaction. Its 2 mol makes 4 mol of water, which is $4 \times 18.02 = 72.06\ \text{g}$, and it uses up 4 mol of hydrogen. That leaves 2 mol of hydrogen unreacted.

The method is always the same: divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient, and the smallest result is the limiting reagent. Each such value is the number of times the equation can run, and the smallest one sets the limit.

## 1.10.2 Reactions in solutions

Most reactions in the laboratory are carried out in solutions, so you also need a way to say how much solute is dissolved in a given amount of solution. There are four common measures.

**Mass per cent** (w/w %) is the mass of solute as a percentage of the mass of solution:

$$
\text{mass \%} = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100
$$

Dissolve 5 g of common salt in 95 g of water and the solution is $5\%$ by mass, because the solution weighs 100 g.

**Mole fraction** is the share of one component in the total moles. If a substance A dissolves in B, and their amounts are $n_\text{A}$ and $n_\text{B}$, then

$$
x_\text{A} = \frac{n_\text{A}}{n_\text{A} + n_\text{B}}, \qquad x_\text{B} = \frac{n_\text{B}}{n_\text{A} + n_\text{B}}, \qquad x_\text{A} + x_\text{B} = 1
$$

One mole of solute dissolved in nine moles of solvent gives $x_\text{A} = 0.1$.

**Molarity**, written $M$, is moles of solute per litre of solution. It is the most widely used measure in the laboratory:

$$
M = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{volume of solution in litres}}
$$

To dilute a stock solution, the amount of solute stays the same while the volume grows. That gives the familiar relation $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$. Solid sodium hydroxide, with molar mass $40.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, gives a 2.0 M solution when $20\ \text{g}$ of it is dissolved to make $250\ \text{mL}$.

<Check n={3} numeric answer={2} tolerance={0.05} unit="M">
Calculate the molarity of a solution made by dissolving 20 g of NaOH (molar mass 40.0 g mol⁻¹) to make 250 mL of solution.
</Check>

Molarity has one weakness. Volume depends on temperature, so a molar solution is only exactly molar at the temperature at which it was measured.

**Molality**, written $m$, is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. It uses the mass of the solvent rather than the volume of the solution:

$$
m = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{mass of solvent in kg}}
$$

<Scrolly animation="concentration-vessels">
<Step>
Molarity uses volume. Start with 0.500 mol of sodium chloride. Dissolve it in 1.00 L of solution and the molarity is $0.50\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. Put the same solute into a half-litre and the molarity doubles to $1.00\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, though the number of moles has not changed.
</Step>
<Step>
Halve the volume again, to $0.250\ \text{L}$, and the molarity becomes $2.00\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. The solute is the same. Only the volume holding it has changed, and molarity follows the volume.
</Step>
<Step>
Molality ignores volume and counts the solvent by mass. Dissolve 0.500 mol of sodium chloride in $1.000\ \text{kg}$ of water and the molality is $0.500\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$. This value depends only on two masses, so it does not change with the state of the liquid.
</Step>
<Step>
Heat the solution from $25\ °\text{C}$ to $80\ °\text{C}$. Water expands as it warms, so the solution's volume grows from about $1.009\ \text{L}$ to $1.035\ \text{L}$, taking its density as $1.02\ \text{g mL}^{-1}$ at $25\ °\text{C}$, an assumed value. Molarity falls from $0.496$ to $0.483\ \text{M}$. Molality stays at $0.500\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$, because no mass has changed.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Molality is therefore the measure to use when the temperature may change, for example in experiments run over a range of temperatures. Molarity is the measure to use when you are dispensing a known volume at a fixed temperature.

<KeyIdeas>
- A balanced equation gives the coefficients as molecules, moles and, through molar masses, grams, and mass is conserved: $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ turns $80.04\ \text{g}$ of reactants into $80.04\ \text{g}$ of products.
- Balancing changes coefficients only, so that each element has the same number of atoms on both sides.
- The limiting reagent is the reactant with the smallest value of moles divided by coefficient; it sets the amount of product.
- Molarity is moles per litre of solution and changes with temperature; molality is moles per kilogram of solvent and does not.
- Mass per cent and mole fraction are other ways to express composition, and dilution keeps the moles of solute constant: $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$.
</KeyIdeas>
