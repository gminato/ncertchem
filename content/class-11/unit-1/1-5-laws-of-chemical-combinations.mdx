<Hook>
Light a diya inside a sealed glass jar. The flame sputters out, the wick chars and the glass goes sooty. Put the jar on a kitchen scale before and after, and the reading does not change. Where did the oil go?
</Hook>

Chemists began to ask questions like this one in the eighteenth century. Their careful measurements led to a handful of simple rules, the **laws of chemical combination**. Each one describes what mass or volume does when elements join to make compounds. Together they are the experimental foundation for the atomic theory in Section 1.6.

Start with the oldest of them, which needs nothing more than a balance and a sealed flask.

<Scrolly animation="mass-balance">
<Step>
Start with a flask sealed by a stopper. Inside it are hydrogen and oxygen molecules in the proportions the equation asks for: two $\ce{H2}$ molecules for every $\ce{O2}$. On the other pan, weights totalling 36.03 g balance the flask exactly. That is the mass of the reactants.
</Step>
<Step>
Now let the reaction run. The stopper keeps everything inside, so no gas can escape. The hydrogen and oxygen atoms regroup, and by the end the flask holds two molecules of water, $\ce{2H2O}$. Watch the atoms: each one that went in is still there, now bonded into water.
</Step>
<Step>
The beam has not moved. The products still weigh 36.03 g, so the change in mass is 0.00 g. In 1789, Antoine Lavoisier reached the same result from careful combustion experiments: in a physical or chemical change there is no net change in mass.
</Step>
<Step>
Count the atoms to see why. Before the reaction there are 4 H and 2 O. After it there are still 4 H and 2 O. Matter is neither created nor destroyed, so the total mass cannot change. This is the **Law of Conservation of Mass**.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

**Definite proportions.** Joseph Proust found that a compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass, whatever its source. Water from a mountain stream and water made in a laboratory are both about 11.2 % hydrogen and 88.8 % oxygen by mass. This is the **Law of Definite Proportions**.

**Multiple proportions.** Elements can sometimes combine in more than one way. Hydrogen and oxygen form water, $\ce{H2O}$, and hydrogen peroxide, $\ce{H2O2}$. Take the same mass of hydrogen in each: for every 2 g of it, water takes up 16 g of oxygen and hydrogen peroxide takes up 32 g. The two oxygen masses are in the ratio 1 : 2, a small whole number. John Dalton put this forward in 1803 as the **Law of Multiple Proportions**.

**Gaseous volumes.** Gases behave in a different way. In 1808, Joseph Gay-Lussac found that when gases react, their volumes are in simple whole-number ratios, provided they are at the same temperature and pressure. Two volumes of hydrogen combine with one volume of oxygen to give two volumes of water vapour.

That observation is best seen by drawing the gases themselves.

<Scrolly animation="gay-lussac-avogadro">
<Step>
Three gases at the same temperature and pressure sit in cylinders. Two volumes of hydrogen and one volume of oxygen go in, and two volumes of water vapour come out. The cylinders are drawn to scale, so the heights show the volumes.
</Step>
<Step>
Measure the volumes and the ratio is 2 : 1 : 2. The same numbers are the coefficients of the balanced equation $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$. Gay-Lussac's ratios are whole numbers, and they follow the equation exactly.
</Step>
<Step>
Zoom in on one unit volume of each gas. Every box holds the same number of molecules, two in each. Two volumes of hydrogen hold four molecules, one volume of oxygen holds two, and two volumes of water vapour hold four. This is **Avogadro's law**: equal volumes, equal numbers of molecules.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the molecules come apart. Each $\ce{H2}$ splits into two hydrogen atoms, and each $\ce{O2}$ into two oxygen atoms. The eight H atoms and four O atoms are exactly the atoms that were in four $\ce{H2}$ and two $\ce{O2}$, so none has been lost.
</Step>
<Step>
The atoms regroup into four $\ce{H2O}$ molecules, two in each box. Four $\ce{H2}$ and two $\ce{O2}$ give four $\ce{H2O}$: the 2 : 1 : 2 ratio survives with nothing left over. Avogadro's idea explains the ratios only because hydrogen and oxygen molecules contain two atoms each.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

**Avogadro's law.** In 1811, Amedeo Avogadro proposed that equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. He also saw that the molecules of hydrogen and oxygen must be diatomic, $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O2}$, for the volume ratios to work. Dalton's theory explained the laws of mass but not the laws of gaseous volumes, which is where Avogadro's idea fills the gap.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Conservation of mass:** in any physical or chemical change, the total mass is unchanged, so matter is neither created nor destroyed.
- **Definite proportions:** a compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass.
- **Multiple proportions:** when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a small whole-number ratio, such as 1 : 2 for $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{H2O2}$.
- **Gaseous volumes:** gases react in simple whole-number volume ratios, which are the coefficients of the balanced equation.
- **Avogadro's law:** equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure hold equal numbers of molecules. This works because $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ are diatomic.
</KeyIdeas>
