<Hook>
Your stomach holds a strong acid that helps digest your lunch. The same kind of chemistry makes the fertiliser that grew the rice on that plate. What links a stomach to a farm?
</Hook>

Chemistry is rarely a subject on its own. Physics, biology and geology all lean on it, and many of the things you use each day exist because someone worked out how to make them. The answer to the Hook is the same idea that runs through this whole unit: chemistry looks at what things are made of, and at what happens when their particles change.

<Scrolly animation="everyday-chemistry">
<Step>
Start with the stomach. The acid there is hydrochloric acid, $\mathrm{HCl}$, a molecule of one hydrogen atom and one chlorine atom. It helps break down food, and it kills many of the microbes that arrive with it. Medicines work at the same level: a drug acts because its molecules fit a particular target in the body, and chemists design that fit one molecule at a time.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, the farm. Most nitrogen fertilisers are made from ammonia, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, a molecule of one nitrogen atom and three hydrogen atoms. Nitrogen gas makes up most of the air, but its two atoms are held together so tightly that plants cannot use it directly. An industrial catalyst helps split that bond, so nitrogen can join hydrogen and form ammonia.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, the atmosphere. Carbon dioxide, $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and methane, $\mathrm{CH_4}$, are greenhouse gases. Their molecules absorb the infrared heat that the Earth radiates, which warms the air. Their levels are shaped by chemical reactions too, some that produce them and some that remove them. Understanding those reactions is part of the work of environmental chemists.
</Step>
<Step>
The same thread runs through all three. In the ammonia reaction, one nitrogen molecule and three hydrogen molecules regroup into two ammonia molecules: $\mathrm{N_2 + 3\,H_2 \rightarrow 2\,NH_3}$. Count the atoms on each side and you get two nitrogen and six hydrogen, unchanged. Chemistry studies matter and its changes at the level of atoms and molecules: which atoms are present, how they are joined, and what a rearrangement makes.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Chemistry matters beyond the laboratory, too. Its industries make fertilisers, drugs, soaps, dyes, polymers, metals and alloys, and they support jobs and national economies. Developing new materials, from superconducting ceramics to conducting polymers, depends on the same understanding of particles and their bonds.

The unit's first question is therefore a broad one: what is matter, and how do we describe it? The next Section starts there.

<KeyIdeas>
- Chemistry is central to medicine, food, materials, the environment and the economy.
- Chemistry studies matter and its changes at the level of atoms and molecules.
- In a chemical change, atoms are rearranged into new molecules. None are created or destroyed.
</KeyIdeas>
