Freeze a glass of water and you get ice. Boil it and you get steam. Ice, water and steam are one substance, $\ce{H2O}$, in three states. Yet a glass of nimbu-pani, which looks just as clear, is not a single substance at all. What separates the two?
</Hook>

Matter is whatever has mass and takes up room. Everything you can see, weigh or touch is made of it, from a notebook to the air in the room. Before we can sort matter into categories, it helps to recall the three physical states it can take and how they differ.

## 1.2.1 States of matter

Matter appears as a solid, a liquid or a gas. The difference lies in how closely the particles are packed and how freely they move. In a solid, the particles sit in an orderly arrangement and can only vibrate about their positions. In a liquid they are still close together, but they slide past one another. In a gas they are far apart and move quickly in every direction.

These arrangements show up as properties. A solid keeps both its volume and its shape. A liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of whatever holds it. A gas has neither a fixed volume nor a fixed shape, so it spreads to fill the container it is in.

The states change into one another when temperature or pressure changes. Heating a solid usually turns it into a liquid, and heating the liquid further turns it into a gas. Cooling runs the same steps in reverse. The simulation below lets you move water through all three states, from −50 °C to 150 °C at 1 atm.

## 1.2.2 Classification of matter

Look at matter at the bulk level and it falls into two groups: mixtures and pure substances. A pure substance has a fixed composition, and every particle in it is the same kind of chemical unit. A mixture contains two or more pure substances, mixed in any ratio, so its composition varies from sample to sample.

The Scrolly below takes six kinds of sample, one at a time. Each Step adds one branch to the tree, so you can see where every sample sits.

<Scrolly animation="matter-classification">
<Step>
Here is a sample that holds several kinds of particle at once: water molecules, oxygen molecules and sodium atoms. Before we sort it, notice the key question. Matter is classified by which kinds of particle it contains, and in what proportions. A pure substance has one kind of chemical unit throughout, and a mixture has more than one.
</Step>
<Step>
Now an element made of single atoms. Sodium, $\ce{Na}$, is a metal, and every particle in it is one sodium atom. A pure substance that consists of only one kind of atom is an element. Some elements, such as sodium or copper, are made of atoms.
</Step>
<Step>
Some elements are made of molecules instead. Oxygen gas is made of molecules, $\ce{O2}$, in which two oxygen atoms share a bond. Hydrogen and nitrogen gases behave the same way. The element is still pure, because every particle is the same, but its particles are molecules, not single atoms.
</Step>
<Step>
A compound is a pure substance made of two or more elements in a fixed ratio. Water, $\ce{H2O}$, has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom, and that ratio never changes. Its properties differ from those of its elements: hydrogen and oxygen are gases, while water is a liquid. Its constituents cannot be separated by simple physical methods.
</Step>
<Step>
Now a mixture with even composition. Air is mostly nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$, with about one part in five oxygen, $\ce{O2}$. The two gases are spread evenly, so a sample from one corner matches a sample from another. A mixture of this kind is called homogeneous.
</Step>
<Step>
Last, a mixture with uneven composition. Iron filings and sulphur can be mixed by hand, but they stay as separate patches of iron and sulphur. The composition differs from place to place, so the mixture is heterogeneous. Its parts can be separated by physical methods, such as picking out the iron with a magnet.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Mixtures can therefore be separated back into their components, using physical methods such as hand-picking, filtration, crystallisation and distillation. A compound cannot be split that way, because its elements are held together by chemical bonds.

<KeyIdeas>
- Solids have a definite volume and shape. Liquids have a definite volume only. Gases have neither a definite volume nor a definite shape.
- Heating moves matter from solid to liquid to gas. Cooling moves it back.
- A pure substance has a fixed composition. An element has one kind of atom, which may join into molecules. A compound has two or more elements in a fixed ratio.
- A mixture has a variable composition. A homogeneous mixture is uniform throughout, and a heterogeneous mixture is not.
- The parts of a mixture can be separated by physical methods, but the elements of a compound cannot.
</KeyIdeas>
