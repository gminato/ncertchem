## Reaction enthalpy

A chemical reaction turns reactants into products, and the enthalpy change that goes with it is the **reaction enthalpy**, written $\Delta_r H$. It is the sum of the enthalpies of the products minus the sum of the enthalpies of the reactants, each weighted by its stoichiometric coefficient:

$$
\Delta_r H = \sum_i a_i H_m(\text{products}) - \sum_i b_i H_m(\text{reactants})
$$

Here $a_i$ and $b_i$ are the coefficients in the balanced equation and $H_m$ is the molar enthalpy.

### 5.4.1 Standard enthalpy of reaction

An enthalpy depends on the conditions, so chemists fix a set of **standard conditions**. The **standard state** of a substance at a given temperature is its pure form at **1 bar**. Liquid ethanol at 298 K is pure liquid ethanol at 1 bar, and solid iron at 500 K is pure iron at 1 bar. This is the convention used here. It is 1 bar, not the older "STP" of 1 atm and 273 K. Data are usually given at 298 K.

When every substance is in its standard state, the enthalpy change is the **standard reaction enthalpy**, written with a superscript: $\Delta_r H^\circ$.

### 5.4.2 Enthalpy changes during phase transformations

Phase changes carry energy too. Ice needs heat to melt, and the temperature stays at 273 K while it does. The enthalpy change for melting one mole of a solid in its standard state is the **standard enthalpy of fusion**, $\Delta_{\text{fus}}H^\circ$. Melting is endothermic, so every enthalpy of fusion is positive, and freezing gives the same amount of heat back. The **standard enthalpy of vaporisation**, $\Delta_{\text{vap}}H^\circ$, is the same idea for boiling at the boiling point. Table 5.1 lists both for some substances.

| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| N₂ | 63.15 | 0.72 | 77.35 | 5.59 |
| NH₃ | 195.4 | 5.65 | 239.73 | 23.35 |
| HCl | 159.0 | 1.992 | 188.0 | 16.15 |
| CO | 68.0 | 6.836 | 82.0 | 6.04 |
| CH₃COCH₃ | 177.8 | 5.72 | 329.4 | 29.1 |
| CCl₄ | 250.16 | 2.5 | 349.69 | 30.0 |
| H₂O | 273.15 | 6.01 | 373.15 | 40.79 |
| NaCl | 1081.0 | 28.8 | 1665.0 | 170.0 |
| C₆H₆ | 278.65 | 9.83 | 353.25 | 30.8 |

*Table 5.1, NCERT Class 11 Part I, Unit 5. The values are the standard enthalpy changes at the melting and boiling points.*

The size of the change follows the strength of the attractions between the molecules. Water's hydrogen bonds hold it tightly in the liquid, so vaporising a mole of water takes more heat than vaporising a mole of acetone, which has weaker dipole-dipole attractions. Take the table's 40.79 kJ mol⁻¹ for water at its boiling point. Boiling two moles of water at 373.15 K needs $2 \times 40.79 = 81.6$ kJ.

### 5.4.3 Standard enthalpy of formation

The **standard enthalpy of formation**, $\Delta_f H^\circ$, is the standard enthalpy change for making one mole of a compound from its elements, each in its reference state. The reference state of an element is its most stable form at 298 K and 1 bar. The reference state of hydrogen is $\text{H}_2$ gas, and for oxygen, carbon and sulphur it is $\text{O}_2$ gas, graphite and rhombic sulphur. By convention, $\Delta_f H^\circ$ is **zero for an element in its reference state**, because nothing has been made.

Some formation equations are:

$$
\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}, \qquad \Delta_f H^\circ = -285.83\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\ce{C(graphite) + 2H2(g) -> CH4(g)}, \qquad \Delta_f H^\circ = -74.81\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

A reaction is not always a formation. Calcium carbonate made from calcium oxide and carbon dioxide has not been made from its elements, so its enthalpy change is not a formation enthalpy. Equations must also be written for one mole of the product. The reaction $\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{Br}_2\text{(l)} \to 2\,\text{HBr(g)}$ makes two moles of HBr, so its enthalpy change is $2\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{HBr})$. Halving every coefficient gives the formation equation for one mole, $\tfrac12\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \tfrac12\text{Br}_2\text{(l)} \to \text{HBr(g)}$, with $\Delta_f H^\circ = -36.40$ kJ mol⁻¹. Doubling that gives $-72.8$ kJ mol⁻¹ for the reaction as written. A value of $-178.3$ kJ mol⁻¹ sometimes printed for this reaction is wrong: that figure belongs to a different reaction.

*Table 5.2, selected rows, at 298 K, from NCERT Class 11 Part I, Unit 5.*

The formation values give a reaction enthalpy without any new measurement. The general rule is

$$
\Delta_r H^\circ = \sum_i a_i\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum_i b_i\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{reactants})
$$

For example, $\text{Mg(OH)}_2\text{(s)} \to \text{MgO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(g)}$ has

$$
\Delta_r H^\circ = \big[-601.7 + (-241.82)\big] - (-924.54) = +81.0\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

The reaction takes in heat, so it is endothermic and needs heating to decompose the hydroxide.

### 5.4.4 Thermochemical equations

A balanced equation together with its $\Delta_r H$ is a **thermochemical equation**. The physical state of each substance must be written, and allotropes named, because the enthalpy depends on them. The methanol combustion below is one example:

$$
\text{CH}_3\text{OH(l)} + \tfrac32\,\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{CO}_2\text{(g)} + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}, \qquad \Delta_r H^\circ = -726.3\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

Three conventions apply to every thermochemical equation.

1. The coefficients refer to moles, never molecules, of each substance. The value of $\Delta_r H$ refers to the amounts that the equation states. If you write the equation with 2 mol of methanol, the enthalpy is doubled.
2. Enthalpy is extensive. Doubling every coefficient doubles $\Delta_r H$, and halving them halves it.
3. Reversing the equation reverses the sign of $\Delta_r H$. The heat absorbed in one direction is the heat released in the other.

### 5.4.5 Hess's law of constant heat summation

Enthalpy is a state function. The change from reactants to products depends only on where the system starts and where it ends, not on the route between them. So a reaction that goes in several steps has the same overall enthalpy change as the sum of its steps, at the same temperature. This is **Hess's law**.

Some reactions cannot be measured directly. Carbon burns to carbon monoxide, but CO₂ always forms as well, so the heat for $\text{C(s)} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2\text{(g)} \to \text{CO(g)}$ cannot be read from a calorimeter. <KeyIdeas>
- Standard states are pure forms at 1 bar. The standard enthalpy of reaction, $\Delta_r H^\circ$, is the enthalpy change with every substance in its standard state.
- The standard enthalpy of formation is zero for an element in its reference state, and every formation value in Table 5.2 is for one mole of compound.
- $\Delta_r H^\circ = \sum a_i\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum b_i\,\Delta_f H^\circ(\text{reactants})$. For $\text{Mg(OH)}_2 \to \text{MgO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ this gives $+81.0$ kJ mol⁻¹.
- Doubling a thermochemical equation doubles its ΔrH, and reversing it changes the sign. Each stated ΔrH refers to the amounts in the equation as written.
- Hess's law follows from enthalpy being a state function: a route in several steps has the same overall ΔrH as the sum of its steps. The carbon route gives $\Delta_r H_2 = -393.5 - (-283.0) = -110.5$ kJ mol⁻¹.
</KeyIdeas>
