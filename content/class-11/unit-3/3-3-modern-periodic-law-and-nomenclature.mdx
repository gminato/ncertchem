<Hook>
Iodine has an atomic mass of 126.9 u and tellurium has 127.6 u, so by mass tellurium should come after iodine. Yet in the table tellurium comes first, and iodine follows it. What decides the order, if not the mass?
</Hook>

## The modern periodic law (3.3)

Mendeleev arranged the elements by atomic mass, and his arrangement worked very well, but some neighbours did not match the order of their masses. The reason lay hidden in the atom. In 1913 Henry Moseley studied the X-rays that different elements give out, and he found a regular pattern in them. Once the pattern is plotted, it puts the elements in an order that matches their atomic numbers.

<Scrolly animation="moseley-plot">
<Step>
In 1913, Henry Moseley studied the characteristic X-rays that elements emit. He plotted the square root of the X-ray frequency against atomic number, $Z$, rather than against atomic mass. The axes are drawn here, and the plot starts empty. Notice that the horizontal axis is the atomic number.
</Step>
<Step>
The points now appear, one for each element from aluminium ($Z = 13$) to zinc ($Z = 30$). These values are not Moseley's measurements. They come from a standard approximation for the K-alpha lines, $\nu = \tfrac{3}{4}R_\infty c\,(Z - 1)^2$, which has the same straight-line shape that his data showed.
</Step>
<Step>
The points fall on a straight line. In this approximation, $\sqrt{\nu}$ rises by the same amount for each step up in $Z$, so the line has a constant slope. The square root of the frequency is therefore linear in the atomic number, and the atomic number sets the order.
</Step>
<Step>
Extend the line back and it meets the axis at $Z = 1$, where $\sqrt{\nu} = 0$. Atomic number is the more fundamental property, and it replaces atomic mass in the law. The **modern periodic law** says that the properties of the elements repeat in a regular way as their atomic numbers increase.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Mendeleev's law was built on atomic mass, so the modern law changes the basis but keeps the idea. The reason for the periodicity is now clear. An atom's number of protons equals its number of electrons, and the electrons set the chemistry. A property that repeats with the electron arrangement must repeat with the atomic number. Tellurium at $Z = 52$ and iodine at $Z = 53$ are in the right order, even though iodine is the lighter of the two.

## The long form of the periodic table (3.3)

The long form is the layout most used today, and it is the one this site follows. Its horizontal rows are called **periods**, and its vertical columns are **groups**. Elements in one group have similar outer electronic configurations. The period number matches the highest principal quantum number, $n$, of the elements in that row. IUPAC now numbers the groups 1 to 18, which replaces the older IA to VIIA, VIII, IB to VIIB and 0 scheme.

There are seven periods. The first has two elements, and the next six have 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements. The sixth and seventh periods would each hold 32 elements in theory, but their 14 lanthanoids and 14 actinoids are placed in two separate rows below the main table. Those two rows keep the main table's structure and keep elements with similar properties in the same column.

<Scrolly animation="periodic-long-form">
<Step>
Here is the long form, with its seven rows and eighteen columns. The lanthanoids and actinoids sit in two detached rows below. Each cell carries the atomic number and the symbol, and every cell stays dimmed until its period is reached. Nothing is counted yet, so the running total is zero.
</Step>
<Step>
The first period contains hydrogen and helium, two elements, because the 1s subshell holds two electrons. The second period fills 2s and then 2p, giving eight elements from lithium to neon. The running total is now ten. Each period's length is set by the number of orbitals that its subshells contain.
</Step>
<Step>
The third period fills 3s and 3p, with eight elements from sodium to argon. The fourth period starts at potassium in 4s, but the 3d subshell fills before 4p. That gives eighteen elements from potassium to krypton, and the running total reaches thirty-six. Scandium to zinc is the first transition series.
</Step>
<Step>
The fifth period works the same way, with 4d between 5s and 5p, so it holds eighteen elements from rubidium to xenon. The sixth period fills 6s, then 4f, then 5d and 6p. That packs in thirty-two elements: eighteen in the main table and the fourteen lanthanoids below it. The total is eighty-six.
</Step>
<Step>
The seventh period has the same pattern: 7s, then 5f, 6d and 7p. It holds thirty-two elements, eighteen in the main table and fourteen actinoids below, and it ends at the element with $Z = 118$. The running total of 118 is the full set of elements known so far.
</Step>
<Step>
Now read the columns. Group 1 holds the alkali metals and group 18 holds the noble gases. Each group shares an outer electronic configuration, and the sweep shows the columns from left to right. Elements in one column behave alike, which is the reason the long form is organised this way.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Naming elements above 100 (3.4)

Element names have traditionally belonged to their discoverers, and the IUPAC then ratifies them. That worked well until the heaviest elements arrived. Those elements are so unstable that only a few atoms are made at a time, and both American and Soviet scientists claimed credit for element 104. The Americans named it Rutherfordium, and the Soviets named it Kurchatovium. To prevent such disputes, IUPAC recommends a systematic name, built from the atomic number, until a discovery is proved and a name is officially recognised.

The systematic name uses one root for each digit of $Z$, and it ends in "ium". Table 3.4 gives the roots. Each digit has a name and a short abbreviation, so the symbol is made from the first letters of the roots.

One rule of elision applies. A root that ends in "i", such as "bi" or "tri", keeps its "i", so the "i" of "ium" is dropped. Element 142, for example, gives "un" + "quad" + "bi" + "ium", which becomes unquadbium. Once a discovery is confirmed, the temporary name is replaced by a permanent one, which is often chosen to honour a scientist or a place. Element 101 is systematically unnilunium and officially mendelevium, and element 118 is ununoctium and oganesson. Table 3.5 lists these pairs for every element from 101 to 118. The table prints two systematic names with doubled consonants, Ununnillium and Unununnium. The rule gives ununnilium and unununium, which is what this site uses.

<KeyIdeas>
- Moseley's X-ray work showed that atomic number, not atomic mass, orders the elements, and the modern periodic law follows from it.
- The long form has seven periods, which are the rows, and eighteen groups, which are the columns. Period number equals the highest principal quantum number.
- Period lengths are 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32, and the lanthanoids and actinoids sit in two separate rows below.
- Elements above 100 get a systematic name built from one root per digit of $Z$, with "ium" added (Table 3.4).
- In a systematic name, a root ending in "i", such as "bi" or "tri", elides the "i" of "ium".
</KeyIdeas>
