</Hook>

## Ionization enthalpy (3.7.1(c))

Ionization enthalpy measures how tightly an atom holds on to its electrons. It is the energy needed to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state. For the first electron the change is written as

$$\ce{X(g) -> X^+(g) + e^-}$$

and the value, $\Delta_i H$, is given in kJ mol⁻¹. Pulling an electron away from a neutral atom always costs energy, so $\Delta_i H$ is always positive.

Removing a second electron from the ion $\ce{X+}$ costs more, because the ion is positive and holds what is left more firmly. The third is higher again. When a book says "ionization enthalpy" without a qualifier, it means the first.

Plot the first ionization enthalpies against atomic number and two patterns stand out. The peaks are at the noble gases, which have closed shells, and the troughs are at the alkali metals. In between, the value generally rises across a period and falls down a group. Two forces explain this. The nucleus pulls on each electron, and the inner electrons screen some of that pull. The electron that is removed feels an effective nuclear charge, $Z_{\text{eff}}$, that is smaller than the true nuclear charge.

Slater's rules, a textbook estimate that NCERT does not use, put $Z_{\text{eff}}$ for the outer electron of sodium at about 2.2 and for chlorine at about 6.1. Across a period, electrons enter the same shell and screen each other only a little, so $Z_{\text{eff}}$ climbs. Down a group, each new shell adds screening that outweighs the extra charge.

Two dips break the rise in period 2. Both have their origin in the 2p subshell, and the Scrolly below shows them one at a time.

## Electron gain enthalpy (3.7.1(d))

Ionization enthalpy describes losing an electron. Electron gain enthalpy describes taking one. It is the enthalpy change when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom to form a negative ion:

$$\ce{X(g) + e^- -> X^-(g)}$$

The symbol is $\Delta_{eg} H$, and the unit is kJ mol⁻¹. NCERT's sign convention is that a negative value means energy is released. Many books define the electron affinity with the opposite sign, so check which convention your book uses before you compare numbers. This Section uses NCERT's.

NCERT's Table 3.7 gives values for the main group elements. Group 1 runs from hydrogen to caesium, and the others are:

- Group 16: O −141, S −200, Se −195, Te −190, Po −174 kJ mol⁻¹.
- Group 17: F −328, Cl −349, Br −325, I −295, At −270 kJ mol⁻¹.
- Group 18: He +48, Ne +116, Ar +96, Kr +96, Xe +77, Rn +68 kJ mol⁻¹.

The trend across a period is that the gain becomes more negative, since the atom is smaller and the added electron is pulled in more strongly. Down a group it becomes less negative, because the added electron lands further from the nucleus. The noble gases have positive values, because the added electron must start a new, higher shell, and that is an unstable arrangement.

Two exceptions come from repulsion inside the small second shell. Fluorine's value, −328, is less negative than chlorine's, −349, and oxygen's, −141, is less negative than sulphur's, −200. An electron added to fluorine or oxygen enters the crowded n = 2 shell and meets strong repulsion. An electron added to chlorine or sulphur enters the larger n = 3 shell, where the repulsion is much weaker.

Oxygen's second electron gain enthalpy is positive. The oxide ion $\ce{O-}$ is already negatively charged, so it repels the electron being added, and energy must be supplied to force it in.

## Electronegativity (3.7.1(e))

Electronegativity is a qualitative idea. It describes how strongly an atom in a compound pulls on shared electrons, and it cannot be measured directly. Several numerical scales exist. The Pauling scale is the most widely used. In 1922 Linus Pauling arbitrarily assigned fluorine the value 4.0, and the other values are set relative to it.

NCERT's Table 3.8 gives the Pauling values for period 2, from lithium to fluorine, as 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5 and 4.0. For period 3, from sodium to chlorine, they are 0.9, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.1, 2.5 and 3.0. Down group 1 they fall from 1.0 for lithium to 0.7 for caesium, and down group 17 from 4.0 for fluorine to 2.2 for astatine.

The trend follows the atomic radius. Across a period the radius shrinks, and the outer electrons feel a stronger pull, so the electronegativity rises. Down a group the radius grows and the pull weakens, so electronegativity falls. Non-metals are electronegative and tend to gain electrons, and metals are electropositive and tend to lose them. The increase in electronegativity across a period is therefore a move towards non-metallic character, and the decrease down a group is a move towards metallic character.

<KeyIdeas>
- The first ionization enthalpy, $\Delta_i H$, is the energy to remove one electron from an isolated gaseous atom, in kJ mol⁻¹. It rises across a period and falls down a group.
- The dips at boron (after beryllium) and oxygen (after nitrogen) come from the 2p subshell: a 2p electron is screened by the 2s pair, and the paired electrons in oxygen's 2p repel each other.
- Electron gain enthalpy, $\Delta_{eg} H$, is negative when energy is released. It becomes more negative across a period and less negative down a group. Fluorine and oxygen are the exceptions, because their added electron enters the small second shell.
- Noble gases have positive electron gain enthalpies, because the added electron must enter a new, higher shell.
- Pauling electronegativity, with fluorine at 4.0, rises across a period and falls down a group. High values go with non-metallic character.
</KeyIdeas>
