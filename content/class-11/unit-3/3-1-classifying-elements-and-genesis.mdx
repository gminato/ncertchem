## 3.1 Why classify the elements?

Every substance you have met is built from elements. For a long time, the list grew faster than anyone could hold it in their head. In 1800 only thirty-one elements were known; by 1865 the number had more than doubled, to sixty-three. Studying each element and each of its compounds one at a time became impossible.

So chemists looked for a system: a way to group elements so that what is known about one tells you something about its neighbours. A good classification does two jobs. It tidies what has already been observed, and it predicts what has not yet been found.

## 3.2 The first patterns

The search began with small groups of similar elements, and then with the whole list at once.

### Döbereiner's triads

By 1829 the German chemist Johann Döbereiner had noticed that some elements came in sets of three that behaved alike. In each set, the middle element looked like a blend of the two outer ones, and its atomic weight sat close to their average.

<Scrolly animation="history-triads">
<Step>
Start with lithium, sodium and potassium. Their atomic weights are $7$, $23$ and $39$. Average the outer two, lithium and potassium, and you get $\dfrac{7 + 39}{2} = 23$. That is exactly the weight of sodium, the middle element. The dashed line marks that mean on the number line.
</Step>
<Step>
Sodium's dot now slides into place at its own weight. In this triad the match is exact. Döbereiner also found that the middle element's properties lay in between those of the other two, which is the second half of his observation.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, calcium, strontium and barium. The outer average is $\dfrac{40 + 137}{2} = 88.5$. Strontium's dot starts at that mean and then moves to its own weight, $88$. The two numbers are close to, not equal to, each other, and that near-match is what made the pattern look real.
</Step>
<Step>
Last, chlorine, bromine and iodine. The outer average is $\dfrac{35.5 + 127}{2} = 81.25$, and bromine's weight is $80$. Again the middle element sits close to the mean. Döbereiner found several such sets, but the rule covered only a few elements, so it was set aside as coincidence.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Döbereiner's Law of Triads worked for only a handful of elements, so most chemists dismissed it. The next attempt was a geometric one. In 1862 the French geologist A.E.B. de Chancourtois arranged the known elements by increasing atomic weight and drew them on a cylinder, to display the periodic recurrence of properties. That also drew little attention.

### Newlands' law of octaves

In 1865 the English chemist John Alexander Newlands took the idea further. He lined up the elements by atomic weight and noticed that every eighth element behaved like the first, in the way that every eighth note of a musical scale repeats the first one an octave higher.

<Scrolly animation="history-octaves">
<Step>
Newlands' table starts with lithium, the first note, which the musical picture calls do. The seven notes of the first row run from lithium to fluorine, so each column shares a note name.
</Step>
<Step>
Fill the first row with the first seven elements, lithium to fluorine. The atomic weights rise steadily: $7$, $9$, $11$, $12$, $14$, $16$, $19$. Each row has seven places, and each place is one note of the octave.
</Step>
<Step>
The eighth element, sodium, starts the second row, directly beneath lithium. In Newlands' pattern it shares lithium's properties. The arc joins the two, the way the eighth note of a scale echoes the first.
</Step>
<Step>
The row pattern continues through potassium and calcium. The pattern seemed to hold only up to calcium. Beyond it, the spacing of eight stopped matching the chemistry, and the dashed cell marks the place where the pattern gave out.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Newlands' law was not widely accepted in his time, but the Royal Society later recognised his work with the Davy Medal in 1887. Its failure beyond calcium pointed to a deeper rule that the octave picture could not express.

### Lothar Meyer's atomic-volume curve

Working in Germany, the chemist Lothar Meyer tested the same idea with physical properties rather than with a rule about counting. He plotted atomic volume, along with melting point and boiling point, against atomic weight. The curves were not straight. They rose, fell and rose again, with repeats at regular intervals.

Here is a plot of molar volume, which is atomic mass divided by density, for the solid elements. The peaks fall at the alkali metals, and the curve falls away between them.

<Scrolly animation="history-meyer">
<Step>
The curve starts with lithium, a light, low-density metal with a small volume. Its molar volume is about $13\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. Beryllium, boron and carbon, which follow, have smaller values still, so the curve dips.
</Step>
<Step>
Sodium's molar volume is much larger, near $24\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. The curve rises sharply at sodium, the second alkali metal, and that is the first peak after the dip.
</Step>
<Step>
Potassium comes next, with a larger volume again. Between the peaks, the curve falls away towards the transition metals and then climbs back. The repeat is what Meyer was looking for.
</Step>
<Step>
Rubidium and caesium complete the picture: each alkali metal is a peak, and the curve falls between them. Meyer reached a periodic table of this sort by 1868, though his paper was published after Mendeleev's.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Meyer and Mendeleev both reached their ideas in 1869, working separately. The graph uses PubChem densities, so its values can differ slightly from Meyer's own measurements.

## Mendeleev's periodic law

Dmitri Mendeleev went further than any of his predecessors. He used more properties than atomic weight alone, including the empirical formulas of compounds, and he stated the result as a law: the properties of the elements vary periodically with their atomic weights. Döbereiner had started the study of such relationships, but Mendeleev was the first to publish a law of this kind.

He arranged the elements in horizontal rows and vertical columns, in order of increasing atomic weight, so that elements with similar properties fell into the same column, which he called a group. Where the order of weights and the order of properties disagreed, he trusted the properties. Iodine, with a smaller atomic weight than tellurium, went into group VII with the halogens, and he suspected the weight measurement was wrong.

The gaps are the most famous part of his table. Mendeleev left empty places where no known element fitted, and he predicted that they would be filled. The gaps under aluminium and silicon became eka-aluminium and eka-silicon. Gallium and germanium were discovered later, and NCERT's Table 3.3 sets his predictions beside the values found.

<Scrolly animation="history-mendeleev-gaps">
<Step>
The chart shows only elements that were known in 1869, in the long-form layout of groups and periods. Scandium, gallium and germanium are not yet known, and the noble gases were not part of his table.
</Step>
<Step>
The dashed cells are the gaps. Gallium sits below aluminium, in group 13, and germanium below silicon, in group 14. Mendeleev named these missing elements eka-aluminium and eka-silicon, after the aluminium and silicon above each gap.
</Step>
<Step>
Tellurium and iodine are the tension in his table. Tellurium is heavier, yet iodine goes into group 17 with the halogens. The swap is written in the cells: the properties decided the order, and the atomic weight took second place.
</Step>
<Step>
Mendeleev predicted the atomic weight, density and oxide formula of each gap. For eka-aluminium he gave $68$ and $5.9\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$, and for eka-silicon $72$ and $5.5\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$. Gallium was later found at $70$ and $5.94$, and germanium at $72.6$ and $5.36$.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Table 3.3 in NCERT sets his predictions beside the values found later. The predicted oxide formulas follow from each element's group: a group-13 element forms $\ce{E2O3}$, and a group-14 element forms $\ce{EO2}$. Gallium's measured oxide is $\ce{Ga2O3}$ and germanium's is $\ce{GeO2}$, so the valence rule held.

<KeyIdeas>
- Döbereiner's triads, Newlands' octaves and Meyer's curve were the first patterns. Each was partly right, and each failed for some elements.
- Mendeleev's periodic law: the properties of the elements vary periodically with their atomic weights. He used compound formulas as well as weights, and he trusted properties where the weight order disagreed.
- He left gaps for eka-aluminium and eka-silicon and predicted their atomic weights and densities, which were then found as gallium and germanium.
- A prediction is judged by its error. Mendeleev's eka-aluminium atomic weight was 2.86 % from the value later found.
</KeyIdeas>
