## Valence: the number of bonds an element makes

The **valence** of a representative element is usually equal to the number of electrons in its outermost orbitals. It can also equal eight minus that number. The two rules are a guide, not a law. but transition elements, which show variable valence, break the pattern. Chemists now often say **oxidation state** where they once said valence. The oxidation state of an element in a compound is the charge its atom would carry if the bonding electrons went wholly to the more electronegative partner.

Because valence follows the group, you can predict the formulas of hydrides and oxides from the group number. Each member of a group forms compounds of the same kind:

- **Group 1:** valence 1. Hydrides are $\ce{LiH}$, $\ce{NaH}$ and $\ce{KH}$. Oxides are $\ce{Li2O}$, $\ce{Na2O}$ and $\ce{K2O}$.
- **Group 2:** valence 2. The hydride is $\ce{CaH2}$. Oxides include $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{CaO}$, $\ce{SrO}$ and $\ce{BaO}$.
- **Group 13:** valence 3. Hydrides include $\ce{AlH3}$ and $\ce{B2H6}$. Oxides include $\ce{Al2O3}$, $\ce{B2O3}$ and $\ce{Ga2O3}$.
- **Group 14:** valence 4. Hydrides are $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{SiH4}$, $\ce{GeH4}$ and $\ce{SnH4}$. Oxides include $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{SiO2}$ and $\ce{SnO2}$.
- **Group 15:** valence 3 or 5. Hydrides are $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{PH3}$ and $\ce{AsH3}$. Oxides include $\ce{N2O3}$, $\ce{N2O5}$, $\ce{P4O6}$ and $\ce{P4O10}$.
- **Group 16:** valence 2 or 6. Hydrides are $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{H2S}$, $\ce{H2Se}$ and $\ce{H2Te}$. Oxides include $\ce{SO3}$, $\ce{SeO3}$ and $\ce{TeO3}$.
- **Group 17:** valence 1 or 7. Hydrides are $\ce{HF}$, $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HBr}$ and $\ce{HI}$. The highest oxide is $\ce{Cl2O7}$.

### Predicting a formula

To write the formula of a compound from two elements, take the valence of each. The atoms combine in the ratio that cancels both valences, just as in the crossing rule for ionic charges.

Take magnesium and nitrogen. Magnesium is in group 2, so its valence is 2. Nitrogen is in group 15, so its valence is 3 or 5. Magnesium nitride uses the form with valence 3. Three magnesium atoms supply $3 \times 2 = 6$ units of positive charge, and two nitrogen atoms take $2 \times 3 = 6$, so the formula is $\ce{Mg3N2}$.

Now try calcium and chlorine. Calcium has valence 2 and chlorine has valence 1, so one calcium atom needs two chlorines: $\ce{CaCl2}$.

## Anomalous behaviour of the second period

The first member of each group in the s- and p-blocks is different from the members below it. This is most marked for the second period, from lithium to fluorine. Three features explain it: the atoms are small, their charge-to-radius ratio is large, and their electronegativity is high.

There is a fourth reason, and it is about orbitals. The second-period atoms have only four valence orbitals, the 2s and the three 2p orbitals. The heavier members of each group also have 3d orbitals, so they can expand their valence shell to hold more than four pairs of electrons. Boron is limited to four bonds, as in the ion $\ce{BF4-}$. Aluminium can go further and forms $\ce{AlF6^3-}$. The **maximum covalency** of a second-period element is therefore 4.

Two more consequences follow. Lithium and beryllium form compounds with a strongly covalent character, unlike the other alkali and alkaline earth metals. The first member of the p-block also forms $p\pi$–$p\pi$ multiple bonds to itself and to other second-period elements, as in $\ce{C=C}$, $\ce{C#C}$, $\ce{N#N}$ and $\ce{C=O}$, which heavier members rarely manage.

### Diagonal relationships

The first element of a group often resembles the second element of the next group, one period down. Lithium resembles magnesium, beryllium resembles aluminium, and boron resembles silicon. This is the **diagonal relationship**. The size data from NCERT show why the pairs are alike: lithium has a metallic radius of 152 pm and an ionic radius of 76 pm, while magnesium has 160 pm and 72 pm. Their radii are close, which is one reason for their similar chemistry.

## Periodic trends and chemical reactivity

Atomic and ionic radii fall across a period, so ionization enthalpies generally rise and electron gain enthalpies become more negative. The element at the far left has the lowest ionization enthalpy, and the element at the far right, the halogen, has the most negative electron gain enthalpy. Noble gases, with their filled shells, have positive values.

These trends make the two ends of a period the most reactive. The most reactive metal is the alkali metal on the far left, which loses its one electron to form a cation. The most reactive non-metal is the halogen on the far right, which gains an electron to form an anion. The centre of the period is the least reactive in this sense. A low ionization enthalpy makes an element a good **reducing agent**, because it gives up electrons easily. A very negative electron gain enthalpy makes an element a good **oxidising agent**, because it takes electrons readily.

Metallic character is highest on the left of a period and falls to the right, while non-metallic character rises. Metallic character also increases down a group, since the outer electrons sit further from the nucleus and are held less tightly.

### Oxides across a period

Oxygen shows the trend clearly. The elements at the two ends of a period combine readily with oxygen. The normal oxide of the element on the far left is the most basic, as in $\ce{Na2O}$. The normal oxide of the element on the far right is the most acidic, as in $\ce{Cl2O7}$. Oxides from the centre of the period are **amphoteric**, such as $\ce{Al2O3}$, or **neutral**, such as CO, NO and N₂O.

An amphoteric oxide acts as an acid towards a base and as a base towards an acid. A neutral oxide has neither acidic nor basic properties. Sodium oxide and chlorine heptoxide show the two ends in water:

$$
\ce{Na2O + H2O -> 2NaOH}
\qquad
\ce{Cl2O7 + H2O -> 2HClO4}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- The valence of a representative element is usually its number of valence electrons, or eight minus that number, so the formulas of its hydrides and oxides follow the group.
- The first member of each second-period group has only 2s and 2p valence orbitals, so its maximum covalency is 4, and it resembles the element one period down and one column to the right (a diagonal pair).
- Ionization enthalpy rises and electron gain enthalpy becomes more negative across a period, so the two ends of a period are the most reactive.
- Oxide character runs from basic (left, $\ce{Na2O}$) through amphoteric ($\ce{Al2O3}$) to acidic (right, $\ce{Cl2O7}$), and neutral oxides ($\ce{CO}$, $\ce{NO}$, $\ce{N2O}$) have neither character.
- Low ionization enthalpy makes a good reducing agent, and a very negative electron gain enthalpy makes a good oxidising agent.
</KeyIdeas>
