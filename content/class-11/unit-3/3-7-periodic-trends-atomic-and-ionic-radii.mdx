A pinch of namak is sodium and chlorine that have struck a bargain: sodium hands an electron over to chlorine. The sodium that ends up in your food is a smaller particle than the soft, silvery metal it came from, while the chlorine that took the electron has swollen. Why would losing an electron shrink an atom, and gaining one make it grow?
</Hook>

## Why periodic trends exist

The Periodic Table is arranged by atomic number, and when you read it row by row and column by column, properties repeat with a rhythm. Reactivity, size, and how tightly an atom holds its electrons all change in patterns you can predict. The reason is the electronic configuration. Every element in a column has the same number of valence electrons, and every step along a row adds one proton and one electron to the same outer region, so the rhythm of configurations is the rhythm of properties.

In this Section we take the first pattern, size, and explain it in terms of two quantities: the **nuclear charge** that the outer electrons feel, and the **principal quantum number** $n$ of the shell they occupy. Size is also the first place where the question "how big is an atom?" needs care, because an atom has no sharp edge.

## Atomic radius

### What "size" means

The electron cloud around an atom fades out gradually, so it has no hard surface to measure to. Chemists get around this by measuring the distance between two atoms that are bonded together, and dividing by two. For a non-metal, the distance between the two nuclei of a covalent bond gives the **covalent radius**. For a metal, the distance between the centres of two neighbouring metal atoms in the crystal gives the **metallic radius**. NCERT uses the single term "atomic radius" for both, and so will this Section.

<Scrolly animation="radii-definitions">
<Step>
Take two chlorine atoms bonded together as Cl₂. The distance between their nuclei is 198 pm, the bond length. Half of that is 99 pm, and that half is the covalent radius of chlorine. Each atom is treated as a ball whose radius is half the distance to its partner.
</Step>
<Step>
For a metal the same idea uses the crystal. In solid copper, neighbouring atoms are 256 pm apart, so half of that, 128 pm, is the metallic radius of copper. The picture is a row of touching spheres, and each radius is half of the gap between the centres.
</Step>
<Step>
Both pictures are read the same way. The atomic radius is always half a distance that was measured between two nuclei, so it is a convention, not a sharp boundary. That is why the numbers that follow should be compared with each other, not treated as exact sizes of single isolated atoms.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

### Across a period: the radius shrinks

Read the atomic radii of period 2 from lithium to fluorine and they fall steadily, from 152 pm to 64 pm. Every one of these atoms has its outer electrons in the same shell, $n = 2$. What changes is the nuclear charge. Moving one step right adds a proton to the nucleus and an electron to the same shell, and the electrons do not block the extra pull from the nucleus very well, so each outer electron is held more tightly.

The working quantity is the **effective nuclear charge**, $Z_\text{eff}$: the charge an outer electron feels after the inner electrons have partly screened the nucleus. The Slater estimate below gives it for the period 2 elements. NCERT discusses this idea qualitatively, and the numbers here are a textbook estimate used to explain the trend.

<Scrolly animation="radii-period-two">
<Step>
Start with lithium. Its outer electron sits in the $n = 2$ shell, and the atom is 152 pm across. Lithium has only one outer electron and only a small effective nuclear charge, so that electron is held loosely and the atom is large.
</Step>
<Step>
Now move to fluorine, seven places along. Its radius is 64 pm, less than half that of lithium. Nothing has moved to a new shell. Fluorine simply has more protons pulling on the same outer shell, and its outer electrons sit much closer to the nucleus.
</Step>
<Step>
The whole row shows the same message. The Slater estimate of $Z_\text{eff}$ rises from about 1.30 for lithium to about 5.20 for fluorine. Within one shell, a larger effective nuclear charge means a smaller atom. The noble gases are not included here, because NCERT compares them with van der Waals radii instead.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

### Down a group: the radius grows

Now go down group 1, the alkali metals. The trend reverses. Lithium is 152 pm, sodium 186 pm, potassium 231 pm, rubidium 244 pm and caesium 262 pm. Each step down the group adds a complete new shell. The outer electron of caesium lives in the $n = 6$ shell, far from the nucleus, and the inner shells that lie between it and the nucleus screen most of the pull. Adding shells therefore wins over the extra protons, and the atom grows.

<Scrolly animation="radii-group-one">
<Step>
Lithium and sodium are the first two rungs. Lithium is 152 pm and sodium 186 pm. Sodium has one more shell, $n = 3$ against $n = 2$, so its outer electron is farther out, even though it has eight more protons.
</Step>
<Step>
Potassium, rubidium and caesium carry the same story further. Potassium is 231 pm, rubidium 244 pm and caesium 262 pm. The radius climbs a little at each step rather than falling, because each step adds a shell of its own.
</Step>
<Step>
Count the shells under each element, and the pattern is clear. The valence shell moves from $n = 2$ for lithium to $n = 6$ for caesium, and the radius follows the shell, not the number of protons.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Ionic radius

### Cations shrink and anions grow

When an atom loses an electron it becomes a cation, and when it gains one it becomes an anion. The nuclear charge stays the same in both cases, but the number of electrons changes. A cation has fewer electrons than its parent atom for the same pull from the nucleus, so the remaining electrons are drawn in. An anion has one or more extra electrons, which repel each other and spread out, so the ion expands.

Ionic radii are estimated from the distances between ions in ionic crystals. In general they follow the same trends as atomic radii. The sodium and fluorine pairs show the two directions most clearly. The ionic radii on this page are Pauling's crystal radii, one scale for every ion, and they match the values NCERT prints for F⁻ and Na⁺.

<Scrolly animation="radii-ionic">
<Step>
The sodium atom has a radius of 186 pm, and the sodium ion, Na⁺, has a radius of 95 pm. The ion has lost its outer shell of electrons, so the nucleus now holds the remaining electrons much more tightly. The cation is smaller than its parent atom.
</Step>
<Step>
Fluorine goes the other way. The fluorine atom is 64 pm, and the fluoride ion, F⁻, is 136 pm. The extra electron repels the others, and the electron cloud swells. The anion is larger than its parent atom, which is the reverse of the cation case.
</Step>
<Step>
Ions that share the same number of electrons are called isoelectronic. The 10-electron set O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ has the same electron cloud in each case. Only the nuclear charge changes, from 8 to 12 protons, and the radius falls steadily as the nucleus pulls harder.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Atomic radius is half a distance between nuclei: the covalent radius for a bond, or the metallic radius for neighbours in a metal. It is a convention, so compare values rather than treat them as exact sizes.
- Across a period the radius falls, because the outer shell stays the same while the effective nuclear charge $Z_\text{eff}$ rises.
- Down a group the radius rises, because each step adds a shell, and the inner shells screen the nuclear pull on the outer electrons.
- A cation is smaller than its parent atom, and an anion is larger, because the number of electrons changes while the nuclear charge does not.
- Among isoelectronic species, the one with the larger nuclear charge is the smaller one: O²⁻ is larger than F⁻, and F⁻ is larger than Na⁺.
</KeyIdeas>
