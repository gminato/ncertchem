<Hook>
Methane is the main gas in biogas, the fuel from a village gobar-gas plant. Carbon's ground state has only two unpaired electrons, yet $\ce{CH4}$ has four identical C–H bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. Where do the other two bonds come from?
</Hook>

Linus Pauling introduced hybridisation to explain the characteristic shapes of polyatomic molecules such as $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. A simple overlap of pure atomic orbitals cannot give those angles, so Pauling proposed a new set of orbitals that are mixtures of the old ones. Hybridisation is a description of the bonding. It does not cause the shape. VSEPR predicts the shape from the electron pairs, and hybridisation describes the bonds in that shape, using orbitals that point the same way.

## The main features of hybridisation

Hybridisation is the process of mixing atomic orbitals of slightly different energies. The result is a new set of orbitals of equal energy and shape, which then form the bonds. Four features follow from that.

- The number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals that were mixed.
- All the hybrids have the same energy and the same shape.
- Hybrid orbitals form stronger bonds than the pure atomic orbitals they come from.
- The hybrids point in directions that keep the electron pairs as far apart as possible, so the type of hybridisation indicates the geometry of the molecule.

Four conditions apply. Only orbitals of the valence shell hybridise. The orbitals taking part should have almost equal energy. Promotion of an electron is not an essential condition before hybridisation, though the examples below use it to create the unpaired electrons a molecule needs. And it is not necessary for the orbitals to be half-filled: filled valence orbitals can take part as well.

## 4.6.1 Types of hybridisation

The hybridisations below involve $s$ and $p$ orbitals of the valence shell. Each one is named by the orbitals that mix, and the Scrolly that follows shows the shape each one describes.

<Step>
Start with the simplest case. One $s$ orbital and one $p$ orbital mix to give two sp hybrids that point in opposite directions, $180°$ apart. In $\ce{BeCl2}$ each hybrid overlaps with a chlorine orbital, and the molecule is linear. The hybrids are on the left, and the shape they describe is on the right.
</Step>
<Step>
Mix one $s$ orbital with two $p$ orbitals and you get three $\text{sp}^2$ hybrids, lying in one plane at $120°$. $\ce{BCl3}$ uses them for its three B–Cl bonds, so the molecule is trigonal planar. That is the shape VSEPR predicts for three bonding pairs, and the hybrids describe the bonds in it.
</Step>
<Step>
One $s$ and three $p$ orbitals give four $\text{sp}^3$ hybrids. Each has $25\%$ $s$ character and $75\%$ $p$ character, and each points to a corner of a tetrahedron. $\ce{CH4}$ uses all four for its C–H bonds, so every H–C–H angle is $109.5°$.
</Step>
<Step>
Water also has four $\text{sp}^3$ hybrid orbitals, but only two of them form bonds. The other two hold lone pairs, which repel the bonding pairs more strongly. The bond angle closes from $109.5°$ to $104.5°$, and the molecule is bent. The four hybrids keep their tetrahedral directions, and the lone pairs take two of them.
</Step>
<Step>
$\text{PCl}_5$ needs five orbitals, and phosphorus has them: one $3s$, three $3p$ and one $3d$. Promotion gives five unpaired electrons, and five $\text{sp}^3\text{d}$ hybrids point to the corners of a trigonal bipyramid. Three bonds lie in the equatorial plane at $120°$, and two point axially, at $90°$ to that plane.
</Step>
<Step>
$\text{SF}_6$ uses six orbitals: one $3s$, three $3p$ and two $3d$. Six $\text{sp}^3\text{d}^2$ hybrids point to the corners of a regular octahedron, so all the bond angles are $90°$ and the six fluorine atoms are equivalent. The hybrids describe the bonds, and the octahedral shape is what VSEPR predicts for six bonding pairs.
</Step>

### sp hybridisation

One $s$ and one $p$ orbital mix to form two equivalent sp hybrid orbitals. Each one has $50\%$ $s$ character and $50\%$ $p$ character. A molecule whose central atom is sp hybridised and bonded directly to two other atoms is linear. This is also called diagonal hybridisation.

The two sp hybrids point in opposite directions along the axis. Each has a large positive lobe and a very small negative lobe, and that shape gives more effective overlap and so stronger bonds.

Beryllium in $\ce{BeCl2}$ is the example. The ground state is $1s^2 2s^2$, which has no unpaired electrons, so one $2s$ electron is promoted to an empty $2p$ orbital. The $2s$ and one $2p$ orbital then hybridise into two sp orbitals, $180°$ apart. Each overlaps axially with a half-filled orbital of a chlorine atom, and the two Be–Cl bonds are $\sigma$ bonds in a straight line.

### sp² hybridisation

One $s$ and two $p$ orbitals mix to form three equivalent $\text{sp}^2$ hybrid orbitals, which lie in a trigonal planar arrangement. Take boron in $\ce{BCl3}$. Its ground state is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^1$, so one $2s$ electron is promoted to the empty $2p$ orbital. Boron then has three unpaired electrons, which hybridise into three $\text{sp}^2$ orbitals. Each overlaps with a chlorine orbital, and the Cl–B–Cl angle is $120°$.

### sp³ hybridisation

One $s$ and three $p$ orbitals mix to give four equivalent $\text{sp}^3$ hybrid orbitals, each with $25\%$ $s$ and $75\%$ $p$ character. They point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron, with an angle of $109.5°$ between any two.

Carbon in $\ce{CH4}$ is the textbook case. Its ground state is $2s^2 2p^2$, which has two unpaired electrons. Promotion gives $2s^1 2p^3$ with four unpaired electrons, and the four $\text{sp}^3$ hybrids each overlap with the $1s$ orbital of a hydrogen atom. The result is four identical $\sigma$ bonds at $109.5°$.

Nitrogen in $\ce{NH3}$ has the ground state $2s^2 2p^3$, with three unpaired electrons, and no promotion is needed. Three of its four $\text{sp}^3$ hybrids overlap with hydrogen orbitals, and the fourth holds a lone pair. A lone pair repels a bonding pair more strongly than two bonding pairs repel each other, so the H–N–H angle closes from $109.5°$ to $107°$ and the molecule is pyramidal.

Oxygen in $\ce{H2O}$ is similar. Its four $\text{sp}^3$ hybrids include two bonding orbitals and two holding lone pairs. The tetrahedral arrangement is still there, but the two lone pairs push the bonds closer together, and the H–O–H angle becomes $104.5°$. The molecule is angular, or V-shaped.

## 4.6.2 Other examples: ethane, ethene and ethyne

The same ideas explain the carbon–carbon compounds, and they show the difference between $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds in practice.

**Ethane, $\ce{C2H6}$.** Both carbons are sp³ hybridised. One sp³ hybrid from each carbon overlaps end on to form a $\text{C–C}$ $\sigma$ bond, which is an sp³–sp³ bond. The other three hybrids of each carbon form $\text{sp}^3\text{–s}$ $\sigma$ bonds with hydrogen. The C–C bond is $154\ \text{pm}$ long, and each C–H bond is $109\ \text{pm}$. There are seven $\sigma$ bonds in all.

**Ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$.** Each carbon is sp² hybridised. One sp² hybrid from each carbon overlaps head on to form the C–C $\sigma$ bond, and the other two sp² hybrids of each carbon form $\text{sp}^2\text{–s}$ $\sigma$ bonds with two hydrogen atoms. That leaves one unhybridised $2p_z$ orbital on each carbon, perpendicular to the plane of the molecule. These two $p$ orbitals overlap side on, above and below the plane, and they form a weak $\pi$ bond.

The double bond therefore has one $\sigma$ bond and one $\pi$ bond. NCERT gives the C=C length as $134\ \text{pm}$ in its text, with C–H bonds of $108\ \text{pm}$, an H–C–H angle of $117.6°$ and an H–C–C angle of $121°$. The whole molecule has five $\sigma$ bonds and one $\pi$ bond.

**Ethyne, $\ce{C2H2}$.** Both carbons are sp hybridised, so each has two unhybridised $p$ orbitals. One sp hybrid from each carbon forms the C–C $\sigma$ bond, and the other sp hybrid of each carbon overlaps with the $1s$ orbital of a hydrogen atom. Each of the two unhybridised $p$ orbitals on one carbon overlaps sideways with the matching orbital on the other carbon, and that makes two $\pi$ bonds. The triple bond is one $\sigma$ bond and two $\pi$ bonds. The molecule is linear, and its C≡C bond is $120\ \text{pm}$ long in NCERT's table of average bond lengths. The whole molecule has three $\sigma$ bonds and two $\pi$ bonds.

## 4.6.3 Hybridisation of elements involving d orbitals

The third-period elements have $d$ orbitals in addition to $s$ and $p$ orbitals. Their energies are comparable to those of the $3s$ and $3p$ orbitals, and to those of the $4s$ and $4p$ orbitals. So hybridisation that mixes $s$, $p$ and $d$ orbitals is possible for these elements. Mixing orbitals from different shells, such as $3p$ with $4s$, is ruled out, because their energies differ too much.

**sp³d hybridisation in $\text{PCl}_5$.** The ground state of phosphorus is $3s^2 3p^3$. In the excited state, one $3s$ electron is promoted to a $3d$ orbital, so that five orbitals each hold one electron. The five $\text{sp}^3\text{d}$ hybrids point to the corners of a trigonal bipyramid, and each overlaps with a singly occupied orbital of a chlorine atom.

Not all five bonds in $\text{PCl}_5$ are equivalent. Three bonds lie in one plane, the equatorial plane, at $120°$ to each other. The other two, the axial bonds, lie one above and one below that plane, at $90°$ to it. The axial bond pairs feel more repulsion from the equatorial pairs, so the axial bonds are slightly longer and weaker than the equatorial ones. That makes $\text{PCl}_5$ more reactive.

**sp³d² hybridisation in $\text{SF}_6$.** Sulphur has the ground state $3s^2 3p^4$. In the excited state, the six orbitals $3s$, three $3p$ and two $3d$ each hold one electron. These six orbitals form six $\text{sp}^3\text{d}^2$ hybrids, which point to the corners of a regular octahedron. Each overlaps with a fluorine orbital, so the molecule has a regular octahedral geometry.

NCERT's summary of the other $d$-orbital hybridisations includes square planar $\text{dsp}^2$, as in $[\text{Ni(CN)}_4]^{2-}$; square pyramidal $\text{sp}^3\text{d}^2$, as in $\text{BrF}_5$; and octahedral $\text{sp}^3\text{d}^2$ or $\text{d}^2\text{sp}^3$, as in $\text{SF}_6$ and $[\text{CrF}_6]^{3-}$.

<KeyIdeas>
- Hybridisation mixes the orbitals of one atom into an equal set of hybrids. The number of hybrids equals the number of orbitals mixed.
- sp hybrids are linear (50% $s$), sp² hybrids are trigonal planar at $120°$, and sp³ hybrids are tetrahedral at $109.5°$ (25% $s$).
- Lone pairs in hybrid orbitals close the bond angles: $107°$ in $\ce{NH3}$ and $104.5°$ in $\ce{H2O}$.
- In a multiple bond, the $\sigma$ bonds come from hybrids and the $\pi$ bonds from unhybridised $p$ orbitals: ethene has 5 σ and 1 π, and ethyne has 3 σ and 2 π.
- sp³d (as in $\text{PCl}_5$, trigonal bipyramidal) and sp³d² (as in $\text{SF}_6$, octahedral) use $d$ orbitals of the third period. The axial bonds of $\text{PCl}_5$ are slightly longer and weaker.
</KeyIdeas>
