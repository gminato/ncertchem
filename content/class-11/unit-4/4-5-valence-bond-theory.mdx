<Hook>
Lewis dots explain why two hydrogen atoms share an electron pair. They do not explain why that pair settles at one exact distance, or why the bonds of a methane molecule point in four particular directions. Something more is needed. What is missing?
</Hook>

Valence bond theory was introduced by Heitler and London and developed further by Pauling and others. It starts from atomic orbitals and asks how they combine when two atoms approach. Lewis structures tell you which atoms are bonded. Valence bond theory tells you why the bond forms, how long it is and which way it points. Its full mathematical treatment is beyond this course, so we use a qualitative picture built on overlap.

## How a hydrogen molecule forms

Take two hydrogen atoms, A and B, each with one electron in a 1s orbital. Far apart, they do not interact. As they approach, new forces appear. Each nucleus attracts the other atom's electron, and the two electrons repel each other, and the two nuclei repel each other too. Attraction tends to pull the atoms together; repulsion tends to push them apart.

<Step>
Start with two hydrogen atoms far apart. Each has one electron in a 1s orbital, and at this distance they hardly feel one another. The energy is close to zero, so the curve starts at the zero line and the overlap picture above shows two separate spheres.
</Step>
<Step>
Bring them closer and the attractions win. Each nucleus pulls on the other atom's electron, and these pulls outweigh the repulsion between the nuclei and between the electrons. The net force draws the atoms together, the orbitals begin to overlap, and the energy falls steadily as the atoms approach.
</Step>
<Step>
At $74\ \text{pm}$ the attractions and repulsions balance, and the energy reaches its minimum of $-435.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. This is the bond length of the hydrogen molecule, and the depth of the well is its bond enthalpy. Breaking the bond needs $435.8\ \text{kJ}$ per mole of $\ce{H2}$, as $\ce{H2(g) -> H(g) + H(g)}$. The atoms are now bonded.
</Step>
<Step>
Push the nuclei closer than $74\ \text{pm}$ and repulsion takes over. The energy climbs steeply, as the curve shows. The minimum is the most stable state of $\ce{H2}$, so the bond length is set by the place where the curve bottoms out, not by where the atoms happen to be.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

The energy released when the bond forms is the **bond enthalpy**. The hydrogen molecule is more stable than two separate atoms by that amount, and it takes the same amount of energy to pull one mole of $\ce{H2}$ apart. The curve above is a smooth model that reproduces the minimum at the measured bond length and bond enthalpy.

## 4.5.1 The orbital overlap concept

The minimum in the curve is the point where the atomic orbitals partly interpenetrate. This shared region is called **overlap**. The extent of overlap decides the strength of the covalent bond, and in general, greater overlap gives a stronger bond. A covalent bond therefore forms when two half-filled orbitals with opposite spins overlap, and the two electrons pair in the region they share.

## 4.5.2 Directional properties of bonds

The hydrogen molecule is a simple case because each atom contributes an $s$ orbital, and a sphere looks the same from every side. Overlap along the line joining the nuclei is the only choice, so the bond has no direction to explain.

Polyatomic molecules are different. Methane has four bonds at $109.5°$ to one another, ammonia is pyramidal and water is angular. Their geometry matters as much as their bonds. Valence bond theory explains these directional properties through the overlap of orbitals that point in particular directions, and through hybridisation, which the next Section develops.

## 4.5.3 Overlapping of atomic orbitals

Orbitals have a phase, shown as a sign on each lobe of a $p$ orbital. The sign is not an electric charge. It is the sign of the wave function in that lobe. When two orbitals overlap, the overlap is positive if the lobes that meet have the same sign, negative if they have opposite signs, and zero if the contributions cancel. Bonding needs the lobes that face each other to have the same sign, so that the overlap is positive.

<Scrolly animation="vbt-overlap">
<Step>
The simplest overlap is head on: two $s$ orbitals meet along the line joining their nuclei. Both lobes are positive, so the overlap is positive and the orbitals reinforce each other. This is a $\sigma$ bond, and the overlap is strongest along the axis.
</Step>
<Step>
Two $p$ orbitals can also meet head on, lobe to lobe, along the same axis. Lobes of the same sign face each other here as well, so the overlap is positive and this is again a $\sigma$ bond. A $p$ orbital points along one direction, so this overlap also fixes the direction of the bond.
</Step>
<Step>
Now turn one $p$ orbital around so that opposite lobes face each other. The product of the signs is negative, so the overlap is negative. Opposite signs do not bond. Atoms do not form a stable bond through a negative overlap, which is why the phase of the lobes must match.
</Step>
<Step>
Parallel $p$ orbitals can also overlap side by side, with their axes perpendicular to the line joining the nuclei. The lobes above the axis overlap with each other, and the lobes below the axis overlap with each other. This is a $\pi$ bond, and it is weaker because the overlap is smaller.
</Step>
<Step>
When two $p$ orbitals sit at right angles, one lobe of each overlaps with a lobe of opposite sign, and the net overlap is zero. No bond forms from this pair, so a sensible picture of bonding needs both the right sign and the right direction.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## 4.5.4 Types of overlapping and the nature of covalent bonds

Covalent bonds fall into two types, depending on how their orbitals overlap.

**Sigma ($\sigma$) bonds.** A $\sigma$ bond forms by end-to-end, head-on overlap of bonding orbitals along the internuclear axis. It is also called axial overlap. Three combinations of orbitals give $\sigma$ bonds:

- $s$–$s$ overlap, where two half-filled $s$ orbitals overlap along the axis, as in $\ce{H2}$.
- $s$–$p$ overlap, where a half-filled $s$ orbital of one atom overlaps with a half-filled $p$ orbital of another.
- $p$–$p$ overlap, where half-filled $p$ orbitals of two approaching atoms overlap along the axis.

**Pi ($\pi$) bonds.** A $\pi$ bond forms by sideways overlap. The orbitals keep their axes parallel to each other and perpendicular to the internuclear axis. The overlap produces two saucer-shaped electron clouds, one above and one below the plane of the bonded atoms. A $\pi$ bond is never formed alone: it always comes with a $\sigma$ bond between the same two atoms.

Ethyne shows both kinds in one molecule. Each carbon has two unhybridised $p$ orbitals, and they can overlap sideways in two perpendicular planes. Seen down the $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$ axis, which points at you, the two $\pi$ bonds sit at right angles to each other.

<Scrolly animation="vbt-ethyne-pi">
<Step>
The first bond between the two carbons is a $\sigma$ bond. The sp hybrids of each carbon overlap end on along the $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$ axis, so the bond is a disc of electron density on the axis, and it sits at the centre of this end-on view.
</Step>
<Step>
Each carbon still has two unhybridised $p$ orbitals, and they lie at right angles to the axis. The $p_y$ orbitals overlap side on, above and below the axis. Those lobes form the first $\pi$ bond, with its coral lobe above and its teal lobe below.
</Step>
<Step>
The $p_z$ orbitals form the second $\pi$ bond. They lie at right angles to the first, so this cloud sits to the left and right of the axis. The two $\pi$ bonds are perpendicular to each other and to the $\sigma$ bond.
</Step>
<Step>
Together, one $\sigma$ bond and two $\pi$ bonds make the triple bond of ethyne. The $\pi$ clouds are weaker than the $\sigma$ bond, but the three bonds together hold the two carbons far more tightly than a single bond would.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## 4.5.5 Strength of σ and π bonds

The strength of a bond depends on how far its orbitals overlap. A $\sigma$ bond overlaps to a larger extent, because the orbitals meet end on, and so it is the stronger of the two. A $\pi$ bond overlaps sideways, to a smaller extent, and it is weaker. In a multiple bond, the $\sigma$ bond is always there, and the $\pi$ bond or bonds are added to it.

The consequence is that the single bond between two carbons, $\text{C–C}$, is the longest of the carbon–carbon bonds. Adding a $\pi$ bond shortens it: the average lengths in Table 4.2 are $154\ \text{pm}$ for $\text{C–C}$, $133\ \text{pm}$ for $\text{C=C}$ and $120\ \text{pm}$ for $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$.

<KeyIdeas>
- A covalent bond forms when half-filled orbitals with opposite spins overlap, and the energy of H₂ is lowest at the bond length, $74\ \text{pm}$, where the bond enthalpy is $435.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
- The overlap of two lobes is positive when they have the same sign, negative when they have opposite signs, and zero when their contributions cancel.
- A $\sigma$ bond forms by head-on overlap along the internuclear axis, from $s$–$s$, $s$–$p$ or $p$–$p$ combinations.
- A $\pi$ bond forms by sideways overlap of parallel $p$ orbitals, above and below the plane. It always follows a $\sigma$ bond.
- The $\sigma$ bond is stronger than the $\pi$ bond, because it overlaps to a larger extent.
</KeyIdeas>
