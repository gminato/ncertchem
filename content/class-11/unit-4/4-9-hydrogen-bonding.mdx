<Hook>
Hydrogen sulphide, H₂S, has a molar mass of about 34 g mol⁻¹, nearly twice that of water. Yet it boils at about −60 °C, while water boils at 100 °C. Water's molecules are light, yet they hold together far more strongly than H₂S molecules do. What is holding them together?
</Hook>

## What a hydrogen bond is

Nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine are highly electronegative elements. When one of them is covalently bonded to hydrogen, the shared electrons move towards it. The hydrogen is left partly positive and the heavier atom partly negative. That partly positive hydrogen can then be attracted to a second atom of nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine, in another molecule or in the same one.

NCERT defines the hydrogen bond as the attractive force that binds a hydrogen atom of one molecule to an electronegative atom (F, O or N) of another molecule. It is drawn as a dotted line, and a solid line stands for the covalent bond. The hydrogen bond acts as a bridge: one atom is held by a covalent bond and the other by a hydrogen bond.

The next Scrolly shows the mechanism with two molecules, HF and water, and the intramolecular case in o-nitrophenol.

## 4.9.1 Cause of the hydrogen bond

When hydrogen is bonded to a strongly electronegative element X, the shared electron pair moves away from the hydrogen towards X. The hydrogen atom becomes electropositive relative to X, and it carries a fractional positive charge, written δ⁺. X acquires a fractional negative charge, δ⁻. The molecule is polar, and the opposite charges of neighbouring molecules attract each other electrostatically.

NCERT says that the hydrogen bond is weaker than a covalent bond. It gives no numerical range for its strength, but it does say the strength depends on the physical state: it is greatest in the solid and least in the gas. For scale, the H–H covalent bond in this unit has an enthalpy of 435.8 kJ mol⁻¹ (Section 4.3.3), and a hydrogen bond is a small fraction of that.

<Step>
In one H–F bond, the shared electrons sit closer to fluorine. The shaded cloud shows the shift. Hydrogen is left partly positive, marked δ⁺, and fluorine partly negative, marked δ⁻. This polar bond is the cause of everything that follows.
</Step>
<Step>
Now bring a second HF molecule near the first. The δ⁺ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ⁻ fluorine of the next, and the dotted hydrogen bonds link the molecules into a zig-zag chain. Each hydrogen bond runs between two different molecules, so it is intermolecular.
</Step>
<Step>
Water works the same way. A hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the oxygen of another, and the dotted line marks that hydrogen bond. Each dotted line joins two water molecules, so again the bond is intermolecular, and it helps explain why water is a liquid at room temperature.
</Step>
<Step>
In o-nitrophenol, the hydrogen of the OH group is attracted to an oxygen of the nitro group in the same molecule. Because the OH and nitro groups sit next to each other on the ring, the contact closes a six-membered ring. A hydrogen bond between two atoms of one molecule is intramolecular, so it does not join the molecule to its neighbours.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## 4.9.2 Types of hydrogen bond

NCERT recognises two types.

**Intermolecular** hydrogen bonds form between two different molecules, whether they are of the same substance or of different substances. The HF chain, water and alcohols are all examples. Their strength is what lifts the boiling points of these liquids.

**Intramolecular** hydrogen bonds form when the hydrogen lies between two highly electronegative atoms within the same molecule. In o-nitrophenol the hydrogen sits between the two oxygen atoms, one on the OH group and one on the nitro group. It joins two groups within the same molecule, so it does not link that molecule to its neighbours.

## Boiling points and the anomaly of water

Hydrogen bonds matter most in how they change physical properties. The hydrides of groups 14 to 17 show the effect clearly. Among the heavier hydrides of each group, the boiling point rises steadily with period. The lightest hydride of each group, in period 2, should lie on the same straight line. Most do not. The next Scrolly extends each trend back to period 2 and compares it with the observed value.

The values come from the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics, and they are not from NCERT. They are normal boiling points, converted from °C to kelvin.

<Step>

<KeyIdeas>
- A hydrogen bond forms when H is covalently bonded to F, O or N and is attracted to a lone pair on another F, O or N. It is drawn as a dotted line.
- The bond forms because the shared electrons move towards the more electronegative atom, which leaves hydrogen δ⁺ and the partner δ⁻.
- A hydrogen bond is weaker than a covalent bond. Its strength is greatest in the solid and least in the gas.
- Intermolecular hydrogen bonds join different molecules, as in HF chains, water and alcohols. Intramolecular ones lie within one molecule, as in o-nitrophenol.
- Hydrogen bonds raise the boiling points of water, HF and NH₃ far above the trend of their groups. CH₄, with no hydrogen bond, follows its group.
</KeyIdeas>
