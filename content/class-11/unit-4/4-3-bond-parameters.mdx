<Hook>
Every breath is about four-fifths nitrogen, which hardly reacts with anything at room temperature. Oxygen, the other main gas in the air, feeds every fire. Both are two atoms joined in a pair. So why do they behave so differently? The answer lies in the measurable features of the bond: how long it is, how strong it is and how many pairs it shares.
</Hook>

## 4.3.1 Bond length

The **bond length** is the equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms. It is measured by spectroscopy and by diffraction of X-rays and electrons, and it is one of the first numbers a chemist records for a new molecule.

In a covalent bond, each atom contributes a share of that distance, and the share is called its **covalent radius**. It is roughly the radius of the atom's core, measured to the point where it meets the core of a partner. The covalent radius is half the distance between two identical atoms joined by a single bond, and the bond length between unlike atoms is the sum of their two radii:

$$
R = r_\text{A} + r_\text{B}
$$

Here $R$ is the bond length and $r_\text{A}$, $r_\text{B}$ are the covalent radii of the two atoms. Do not confuse the covalent radius with the **van der Waals radius**, which is half the distance between two identical atoms in neighbouring molecules of a solid. The van der Waals radius is the larger of the two.

<Step>
Start with one carbon atom, drawn to scale. Its **covalent radius** is the distance from its nucleus to the point where its core would touch a bonded partner. For carbon in a single bond this radius is $77\ \text{pm}$, the value in NCERT's covalent radius table.
</Step>
<Step>
Bring a second carbon atom up until the two cores touch. The distance between the nuclei is the **bond length**, and it is simply the sum of the two radii: $R = 77 + 77 = 154\ \text{pm}$. This matches the average C–C bond length in the table of average bond lengths exactly.
</Step>
<Step>
Now make the bond double. The carbon radius drops to $67\ \text{pm}$ for a double bond, because two shared pairs hold the atoms closer together. Add the two radii and you get $134\ \text{pm}$, within 1 pm of the $133\ \text{pm}$ printed for C=C in the table of average bond lengths.
</Step>
<Step>
A triple bond takes the radius to $60\ \text{pm}$, so the bond length is $120\ \text{pm}$, the value printed for C≡C. The sum of radii is a useful estimate, but it is only an estimate. H–Cl, for example, gives $37 + 99 = 136\ \text{pm}$, while the measured length is $127\ \text{pm}$.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Bond lengths are measured values, and the covalent radii are chosen so that they reproduce them as closely as they can. Two patterns come out of the tables. A bond between two atoms of the same kind shortens as the bond order rises, and a bond to a larger atom is longer.

## 4.3.2 Bond angle

The **bond angle** is the angle between the orbitals that hold the bonding pairs around a central atom in a molecule or complex ion. It is expressed in degrees and is determined experimentally, by spectroscopic methods. It tells you how the bonds are spread around the central atom, and so it is one of the clues to the shape of a molecule.

Water is the familiar example. Its two O–H bonds are arranged at an angle of $104.5^\circ$, and that angle is what makes water a bent molecule rather than a straight one.

## 4.3.3 Bond enthalpy

The **bond enthalpy** is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond between two atoms in the gas phase. The unit is $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. For hydrogen, breaking the H–H bond takes $435.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$:

$$
\ce{H2(g) -> H(g) + H(g)}, \qquad \Delta_\text{a}H = 435.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

Multiple bonds are stronger. Breaking the O=O bond of oxygen takes $498\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, and the N≡N bond of nitrogen takes $946.0\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. For a heteronuclear molecule, such as hydrogen chloride, the figure is $431.0\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. The bigger the bond enthalpy, the stronger the bond.

In a polyatomic molecule, a single bond enthalpy is harder to define, because each bond breaks at a different cost. Water shows this clearly. Its first O–H bond needs $502\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ to break, and the second, from the OH fragment, needs $427\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. The O–H bond is not the same bond the second time, because its chemical surroundings have changed. So the usual figure is an average: the total enthalpy for breaking all the bonds divided by the number of bonds broken.

$$
\text{average O–H} = \frac{502 + 427}{2} = 464.5\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

## 4.3.4 Bond order

In the Lewis description, the **bond order** is the number of bonds between two atoms. Equivalently, it is the number of shared electron pairs. A hydrogen molecule has one shared pair, so its bond order is 1. Oxygen has two shared pairs, so its bond order is 2, and nitrogen has three, so its bond order is 3. Carbon monoxide, with three shared pairs between carbon and oxygen, also has bond order 3.

Isoelectronic species have identical bond orders. For example, $\ce{F2}$ and $\ce{O2^2-}$ both have bond order 1, while $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{CO}$ and $\ce{NO+}$ all have bond order 3. Across this family, the general trend holds: as the bond order rises, the bond enthalpy rises and the bond length falls. The Scrolly below walks through the diatomics and the carbon series.

</Step>
<Step>
Oxygen shares two pairs, so its bond order is 2. Its bond is $121\ \text{pm}$ long and needs $498\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ to break. Lengths depend on the size of the atoms as well, so compare them only within one pair of atoms. Watch the bars: the enthalpy rises from hydrogen to oxygen, and the length rises too, because oxygen atoms are bigger.
</Step>
<Step>
Nitrogen shares three pairs, so its bond order is 3. Its bond is $109\ \text{pm}$ long, shorter than the bond in oxygen, and its enthalpy of $946.0\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ is the largest of the diatomics shown here. Triple bonds are among the hardest bonds to break in the common diatomic molecules.
</Step>
<Step>
Fluorine shares only one pair. Its bond is $144\ \text{pm}$ long and weak, about $159\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ from the bar chart of Fig. 4.21. A bond order of 1 in hydrogen and in fluorine gives very different strengths, so bond order alone does not fix a bond's strength.
</Step>
<Step>
Within one pair of atoms the rule is clean. Carbon's single, double and triple bonds measure $154$, $133$ and $120\ \text{pm}$. Each extra shared pair goes with a shorter bond, which is the general correlation: higher bond order, higher bond enthalpy, shorter bond.
</Step>

## 4.3.5 Resonance structures

Sometimes a single Lewis structure does not describe a molecule correctly. Ozone, $\ce{O3}$, is the standard example. Drawn with one double bond and one single bond, it has two unequal oxygen–oxygen bonds. Yet the experiment finds two equal bonds, each of which is between the lengths of a single and a double bond.

To handle this, chemists write several structures with similar energy, the same positions of the nuclei and the same numbers of bonding and non-bonding pairs, and call them **canonical forms**. Their combined description is the **resonance hybrid**. A double-headed arrow joins the forms, and the hybrid is the molecule as it really is.

The Scrolly below walks through ozone, from its two forms to its single averaged structure.

<Scrolly animation="resonance-ozone">
<Step>
Ozone can be drawn with a double bond on the left and a single bond on the right, with a positive charge on the central oxygen and a negative charge on the right-hand one. In this form the left bond is $121\ \text{pm}$, the normal double-bond length, and the right bond is $148\ \text{pm}$, the normal single-bond length.
</Step>
<Step>
Draw the mirror image, with the double bond on the right. The measured O–O bonds in ozone are both $128\ \text{pm}$. Each lies between the double and the single bond, so neither drawing fits the molecule on its own. Both drawings are correct Lewis structures, and that is exactly the difficulty.
</Step>
<Step>
Joined by a double-headed arrow, the two drawings are the **canonical forms**. They are not two molecules taking turns. The molecule does not spend part of its time in either form, and it does not flip between them the way keto and enol forms interconvert in tautomerism.
</Step>
<Step>
The **resonance hybrid** is the one real structure. Both bonds are the same length, $128\ \text{pm}$, and each has a bond order between 1 and 2. The dashed line in the drawing marks the part of the second pair that is spread over both bonds.
</Step>
<Step>
Average the two forms: each O–O bond has the bond orders $2$ and $1$ in turn, so $(2 + 1)/2 = 1.5$. That average is the bond order of the hybrid, and it sits between the two forms, just as the measured length does. Resonance also lowers the energy. The hybrid is more stable than either canonical form on its own.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Resonance is not limited to ozone. In the carbonate ion, $\ce{CO3^2-}$, one Lewis drawing with two single bonds and one double bond gives unequal carbon–oxygen bonds. The experiment finds three equal ones, so the carbonate ion is a hybrid of three canonical forms, each with the double bond on a different oxygen. The average bond order of each C–O bond is $4/3$, the bond order of the hybrid.

The carbon dioxide molecule shows the same thing with lengths instead. The measured C–O bond is $115\ \text{pm}$. A normal C=O double bond is $121\ \text{pm}$ and a C≡O triple bond is $110\ \text{pm}$, so the measured length lies between them, and no single structure can express that.

<Check n={3} numeric answer={1.33} tolerance={0.01} explanation="The three canonical forms of CO₃²⁻ put the double bond on each oxygen in turn, so each C–O bond takes the bond orders 2, 1 and 1 in one form. Averaging over the three forms gives (2 + 1 + 1) / 3 = 4/3, or 1.33 to two decimals.">
In the carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻, the three C–O bonds are equal. What is the average bond order of each one, to two decimal places?
</Check>

Resonance is easy to misread, so it is worth fixing the four points that students most often get wrong. The canonical forms have no real existence. The molecule does not spend part of its time in one form and part in another. There is no equilibrium between the forms, unlike the keto and enol forms of a tautomer. And the molecule has one structure, the resonance hybrid, which cannot be drawn as a single Lewis structure.

## 4.3.6 Polarity of bonds

A bond between two identical atoms, as in $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{N2}$ or $\ce{F2}$, shares its pair equally, and the bond is **non-polar**. When the two atoms differ, the shared pair is pulled towards the more electronegative one. In hydrogen fluoride, the pair sits closer to fluorine, and the bond is a **polar covalent bond**. Few bonds are perfectly one or the other, but this model is the one to start with.

Polarisation creates a **dipole moment**, $\mu$, which is the product of the magnitude of the charge and the distance between the centres of the positive and negative charges:

$$
\mu = Q \times r
$$

The dipole moment is measured in **debye**, D, which honours Peter Debye, whose work on X-ray diffraction and dipole moments earned him the Nobel Prize in 1936. The conversion is $1\ \text{D} = 3.33564 \times 10^{-30}\ \text{C m}$. A dipole moment is a vector, and chemists draw it as a crossed arrow on the Lewis structure: the cross sits on the positive end and the arrow head on the negative end. The usual physics convention runs the other way, with the head at the positive end, so in chemistry's drawing read the cross, not the head, as the positive end.

For a polyatomic molecule the picture has two parts. Each bond has its own **bond dipole**, and the molecule's dipole moment is the vector sum of all the bond dipoles and any lone-pair contributions. Water is the standard example. Its two O–H bonds are at $104.5^\circ$, and their sum gives $\mu = 1.85\ \text{D}$. The symmetric molecules cancel: the bond dipoles of $\ce{BeF2}$ are equal and opposite, and the three equal bond dipoles of planar $\ce{BF3}$ sum to zero. The two pyramidal molecules show why the bond dipoles are not the whole story. Fluorine is more electronegative than nitrogen, so each N–F bond points strongly towards fluorine. Yet NF₃ has a smaller dipole moment than NH₃, $0.23\ \text{D}$ against $1.47\ \text{D}$. The reason is the lone pair on nitrogen. In NH₃ the lone-pair dipole points the same way as the resultant of the three N–H bond dipoles, so the two add up. In NF₃ the lone-pair dipole points against the resultant of the N–F bond dipoles, and it cuts the total. The partial covalent character of ionic bonds has its own rules, which Fajans set out. A smaller cation and a larger anion make the bond more covalent. A greater charge on the cation does the same. And for two cations of the same size and charge, the one with a $(n-1)d^n ns^0$ configuration, typical of transition metals, polarises the anion more strongly than one with a noble-gas $ns^2 np^6$ configuration. The cation pulls the electron cloud of the anion towards itself, and that build-up of charge between the nuclei is exactly what happens in a covalent bond. The polarising power of the cation, the polarisability of the anion and the extent of its distortion together set the percentage of covalent character.

<KeyIdeas>
- Bond length is the distance between nuclei: the sum of the two covalent radii, for example $154\ \text{pm}$ for C–C, and the estimate is only approximate.
- Bond enthalpy is the energy to break one mole of a bond in the gas phase, in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. In water the two O–H steps differ, $502$ and $427\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, so the average is $464.5\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
- Bond order is the number of shared pairs. Higher bond order gives a shorter bond and a stronger one, within one pair of atoms.
- Resonance is needed when one Lewis structure fails. The canonical forms are not real, and the hybrid is the one structure: ozone's two equal bonds have bond order $1.5$ and length $128\ \text{pm}$.
- A dipole moment is the vector sum of the bond dipoles, measured in debye, with $1\ \text{D} = 3.33564 \times 10^{-30}\ \text{C m}$. Symmetry cancels it; a lone pair can add to it or cut it.
</KeyIdeas>
