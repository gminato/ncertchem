<Hook>
Forming a sodium ion from a sodium atom costs energy, and chlorine gives back only part of that when it picks up the electron. Yet salt is a stable crystal that lasts on the shelf for years. Where does the energy come back, and what holds the crystal together?
</Hook>

## Two steps before the bond

Kössel and Lewis's treatment of the ionic bond implies that the formation of an ionic compound depends on two things. The first is how easily positive and negative ions form from the neutral atoms. The second is how those ions are arranged in the solid, the crystal lattice. A compound will form only if both steps work out.

Forming a positive ion means removing electrons from the neutral atom, and forming a negative ion means adding electrons to it. Two energies describe these steps:

- The **ionisation enthalpy** is the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom in its ground state, $\text{M(g)} \rightarrow \text{M}^{+}\text{(g)} + \text{e}^{-}$. It is always positive, so ionisation always takes energy in.
- The **electron gain enthalpy**, $\Delta_{\text{eg}}H$, is the enthalpy change when a gaseous atom in its ground state gains an electron, $\text{X(g)} + \text{e}^{-} \rightarrow \text{X}^{-}\text{(g)}$. It can be negative (energy released) or positive (energy taken in). The **electron affinity** is the negative of the energy change accompanying electron gain.

Ionic bonds form most easily between elements with low ionisation enthalpies, which give up electrons readily, and elements with large negative electron gain enthalpies, which take them up. In practice, cations come from metals and anions from non-metals. The ammonium ion, NH₄⁺, is the exception: it is formed from non-metals and still acts as a cation.

An ionic solid is an orderly three-dimensional arrangement of cations and anions, held together by the Coulomb interactions between them. Sodium chloride crystallises in the rock-salt structure, in which each ion is surrounded by six ions of the opposite charge. The arrangement depends on the size of the ions and how they pack, not only on their charges.

### 4.2.1 Lattice enthalpy

The ionisation and electron gain steps for sodium chloride show the puzzle. The ionisation enthalpy of sodium is $+495.8$ kJ mol⁻¹, and the electron gain enthalpy of chlorine is $-348.7$ kJ mol⁻¹. Their sum, $+147.1$ kJ mol⁻¹, is positive, so forming the separate ions costs energy. Yet a crystal of sodium chloride is stable. The energy released when the ions come together into the solid, the **lattice enthalpy**, more than makes up for that cost.

The **lattice enthalpy** of an ionic solid is the energy needed to separate one mole of the solid completely into its gaseous ions. For sodium chloride, NCERT gives $+788$ kJ mol⁻¹. That is the energy to split one mole of solid NaCl into one mole of gaseous Na⁺ ions and one mole of gaseous Cl⁻ ions, each taken to an infinite distance from the others. Attraction between opposite charges and repulsion between like charges are both involved. Because the solid is three-dimensional, the value cannot be found from the forces between two ions alone: the geometry of the crystal has to be included.

The lattice enthalpy is therefore a better measure of the stability of an ionic compound than the octet of the separate ions. Put the steps in a cycle and the bookkeeping becomes clear. A cycle of this kind is called a Born–Haber cycle. NCERT does not draw it, but it is a standard way to combine the steps. Each step is written in the formation direction, from the elements to the solid. Separating the solid is the reverse of forming it, so the lattice formation step for NaCl is $-788$ kJ mol⁻¹, while NCERT's lattice enthalpy is $+788$ kJ mol⁻¹.

<Step>
Start from the elements in their standard states: sodium as a solid and chlorine as a gas. Both sit at zero. Every step that follows is measured from this level, in the direction that forms solid sodium chloride from them. Read this way, the final level is the enthalpy of formation, the quantity a chemist can look up or measure.
</Step>
<Step>
Sodium must first turn from a solid into gaseous atoms. This is sublimation, and it takes in about $+108$ kJ mol⁻¹, so the level climbs. The value is standard reference data, not one that NCERT prints in this Unit, so it is labelled as such. The ions are formed from gaseous sodium atoms, which is why this step is needed at all.
</Step>
<Step>
Chlorine gas must be split into single atoms. From half a mole of Cl₂ this is dissociation, which takes in about $+121$ kJ mol⁻¹ per chlorine atom. It is also standard data. After this step the level sits at about $+230$ kJ mol⁻¹, and every atom needed for the salt is in the gas phase, ready to become an ion.
</Step>
<Step>
Ionisation removes the outer electron from each gaseous sodium atom, taking in $+495.8$ kJ mol⁻¹. This is NCERT's value, and it is the largest uphill step in the cycle. The level now reaches $+725.5$ kJ mol⁻¹, its highest point. The energy has to be paid before the ions can exist at all, and the rest of the cycle has to make it back.
</Step>
<Step>
Electron gain adds one electron to each gaseous chlorine atom and releases $348.7$ kJ mol⁻¹, NCERT's value. The level comes down to $+376.8$ kJ mol⁻¹. That is still far above zero, so making the separate ions would cost energy overall. Sodium chloride is stable only because of the next step.
</Step>
<Step>
Lattice formation brings the gaseous Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions together into solid sodium chloride, releasing $788$ kJ mol⁻¹, NCERT's value. In this formation direction the step is $-788$ kJ mol⁻¹. The level drops to $-411.2$ kJ mol⁻¹, the standard enthalpy of formation of solid NaCl. The cycle closes there, and the lattice step is what makes the whole process downhill.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- An ionic bond forms when atoms lose and gain electrons. Ionisation enthalpy (always positive) and electron gain enthalpy (which can be either sign) set the cost of making the ions.
- Ionic compounds are cations from metals and anions from non-metals, arranged in a three-dimensional lattice held together by Coulomb interactions.
- For NaCl, the ion-forming steps cost +147.1 kJ mol⁻¹ in total, so the octet of the separate ions does not explain stability on its own.
- The lattice enthalpy is the energy to separate one mole of the solid into gaseous ions. NCERT gives +788 kJ mol⁻¹ for NaCl, and the formation-direction lattice step is therefore −788 kJ mol⁻¹.
- In a Born–Haber cycle the steps sum to the enthalpy of formation, which for solid NaCl is −411.2 kJ mol⁻¹ with standard data for the sublimation and dissociation steps.
</KeyIdeas>
