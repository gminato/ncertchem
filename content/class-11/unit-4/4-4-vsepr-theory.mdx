<Hook>
Carbon dioxide and water both have a central atom with two partners. Yet CO₂ is a straight line and H₂O is bent, like a boomerang. Both have the same count of partners, so what else is different?
</Hook>

Lewis structures tell you which atoms are bonded to which, but not how the atoms are arranged in space. The **valence shell electron pair repulsion** theory, or VSEPR, fills that gap. It gives a simple rule for predicting the shape of a covalent molecule from the number of electron pairs on its central atom.

<Scrolly animation="vsepr-repulsion">
<Step>
Picture four pairs of valence electrons crowded on one side of a central atom. Each pair is a cloud of negative charge, so neighbouring pairs push against each other. The arrangement is strained, and the pairs will keep moving until the push on each one is as gentle as it can be.
</Step>
<Step>
Let them spread out and four pairs settle into a tetrahedron. Each pair sits at the corner of a tetrahedron, and the angle between any two of them is $109.5°$. No other arrangement of four points on a sphere keeps them farther apart.
</Step>
<Step>
Two pairs are the simplest case. They stay as far apart as possible when they lie on opposite sides of the atom, in a straight line. The angle is $180°$, and the arrangement is **linear**, as in $\ce{BeCl2}$ and $\ce{HgCl2}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Three pairs spread into one flat triangle, with $120°$ between each pair of them. This is the **trigonal planar** arrangement, found in $\ce{BF3}$, where the central boron has no lone pair.
</Step>
<Step>
Five pairs cannot all be $90°$ apart, so two angles appear. Three pairs lie in one plane at $120°$ to each other, and two point straight up and down, at $90°$ to that plane. This is the **trigonal bipyramidal** arrangement, as in $\ce{PCl5}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Six pairs fit along the six directions of the axes: four around the equator at $90°$, and one above and one below. This is the **octahedral** arrangement, as in $\ce{SF6}$. These five arrangements, from linear to octahedral, are the base geometries for every molecule in this Section.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## The postulates of VSEPR

VSEPR is built on a few postulates, and every prediction below follows from them.

1. The shape of a molecule depends on the number of valence shell electron pairs around its central atom. Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count.
2. Electron pairs repel one another, because their electron clouds are negatively charged.
3. The pairs take up the positions that minimise this repulsion, and so maximise the distance between them.
4. The valence shell is treated as a sphere, with the electron pairs sitting on its surface at the greatest possible separation.
5. A multiple bond is treated as a single electron pair. The two or three pairs of a double or triple bond act as one super pair. So $\ce{CO2}$ has two electron domains around carbon, and each double bond counts once.
6. When a molecule can be written as several resonance structures, the model applies to any one of them.

The theory is also refined by the difference between lone pairs and bonding pairs. A lone pair belongs to the central atom alone, while a bonding pair is shared between two atoms. A lone pair therefore occupies more space, and the repulsions decrease in this order:

$$
\text{lone pair–lone pair} \;>\; \text{lone pair–bonding pair} \;>\; \text{bonding pair–bonding pair}
$$

This order explains almost every distortion from an ideal angle in the rest of the Section. A stronger repulsion pushes its neighbours closer together.

## Central atom with no lone pair

When the central atom carries no lone pair, the shape is the same as the arrangement of its bonded pairs. Table 4.6 lists the five base geometries.

| Electron pairs | Arrangement | Shape | Bond angles | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Linear | Linear | 180° | $\ce{BeCl2}$, $\ce{HgCl2}$ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | Trigonal planar | 120° | $\ce{BF3}$ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{NH4+}$ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° | $\ce{PCl5}$ |
| 6 | Octahedral | Octahedral | 90° | $\ce{SF6}$ |

The arrangement and the shape agree here because every pair is a bond. The bond angle in a tetrahedron is the same for every pair, so the only thing to learn is which arrangement goes with how many pairs.

## Central atom with one or more lone pairs

Once a lone pair is present, the arrangement of all the pairs and the shape of the atoms no longer match. The shape is described by the atoms alone, and the lone pairs are unseen. Table 4.7 gives the shapes of simple molecules and ions of this kind.

| Type | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Arrangement of pairs | Shape | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AX₂E | 2 | 1 | Trigonal planar | Bent | $\ce{SO2}$ |
| AX₃E | 3 | 1 | Tetrahedral | Trigonal pyramidal | $\ce{NH3}$ |
| AX₂E₂ | 2 | 2 | Tetrahedral | Bent | $\ce{H2O}$ |
| AX₄E | 4 | 1 | Trigonal bipyramidal | See-saw | $\ce{SF4}$ |
| AX₃E₂ | 3 | 2 | Trigonal bipyramidal | T-shape | $\ce{ClF3}$ |
| AX₅E | 5 | 1 | Octahedral | Square pyramidal | $\ce{BrF5}$ |
| AX₄E₂ | 4 | 2 | Octahedral | Square planar | $\ce{XeF4}$ |

In this notation X stands for a bonded atom and E for a lone pair. NCERT writes the same classes as AB, AB₂E and so on. For $\ce{SO2}$, each double bond counts as one electron pair, so the central sulphur has two bonding domains and one lone pair. The table gives the shape, not the arrangement of pairs.

### Why the bond angle closes

A lone pair pushes harder than a bonding pair does, so the bonds crowd together. Table 4.8 of NCERT gives the reason for each distortion:

- In $\ce{SO2}$, the lone pair–bonding pair repulsion is much stronger than the bonding pair–bonding pair repulsion. The angle falls from $120°$ to $119.5°$.
- In $\ce{NH3}$, the tetrahedral arrangement is broken by one lone pair. The bond angle falls from $109.5°$ to $107°$.
- In $\ce{H2O}$, two lone pairs repel each other more strongly than either repels a bond, so the angle falls from $109.5°$ to $104.5°$. The shape is bent.

<Scrolly animation="vsepr-lone-pairs">
<Step>
Start with $\ce{CH4}$. Carbon has four bonded pairs and no lone pair. They sit at the corners of a tetrahedron, and each H–C–H angle is $109.5°$. Every pair here is a bond shared between two atoms.
</Step>
<Step>
Now let one of the four pairs belong to nitrogen alone. $\ce{NH3}$ has three bonded pairs and one lone pair. The lone pair occupies more space than a bonding pair, so it pushes the three bonds harder. The shape is no longer tetrahedral; it is a trigonal pyramid.
</Step>
<Step>
The lone pair–bonding pair repulsion is stronger than the bonding pair–bonding pair repulsion, so the three N–H bonds are squeezed together. The bond angle falls from $109.5°$ to $107°$, and the lone pair stays close to the nitrogen.
</Step>
<Step>
Add a second lone pair and $\ce{H2O}$ has two bonded pairs and two lone pairs. The two lone pairs repel each other more than either repels a bond, so the H–O–H angle closes further, to $104.5°$. The shape is bent, the same bent shape as $\ce{SO2}$.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

### Where the lone pairs sit

A trigonal bipyramid has two kinds of position. Two **axial** positions sit on one axis, at $90°$ to the three equatorial positions. Three **equatorial** positions lie in one plane, at $120°$ to one another and $90°$ to the axial pair. A lone pair drawn in an axial position has three lone pair–bonding pair repulsions at $90°$. In an equatorial position it has only two. Each lone pair therefore prefers an equatorial place, because that place has fewer strong $90°$ repulsions.

This decides the shape of each member of the family. In $\ce{SF4}$, one lone pair takes an equatorial place, and the four bonds form a shape NCERT calls a see-saw, or a distorted tetrahedron. In $\ce{ClF3}$, two lone pairs take the two equatorial places, and the three bonds form a T. In $\ce{BrF5}$ the lone pair sits on the apex of a square pyramid, and in $\ce{XeF4}$ the two lone pairs sit on opposite sides of the octahedron, a square planar shape. Keeping the two lone pairs opposite each other puts them as far apart as possible.

## Shapes and the reasons behind them

The rule that the shape follows the electron-pair arrangement works best for the **p-block** elements, where the atoms are small and their valence shells are well defined. It also gives the right geometry when the energy difference between two possible structures is very small. The theory is still a model. NCERT notes that the theoretical basis of electron-pair repulsion and its effect on shape is not yet fully settled, so treat VSEPR as a reliable guide to predict shapes, and not as a complete explanation of the bonding.

<KeyIdeas>
- Electron pairs on a central atom repel and move as far apart as they can. The arrangement of all pairs is the electron-pair geometry: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal or octahedral.
- A multiple bond counts as one electron pair, and each lone pair also counts as one.
- Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, so the order is lone pair–lone pair, then lone pair–bonding pair, then bonding pair–bonding pair. This closes the bond angles: $109.5°$ falls to $107°$ in $\ce{NH3}$, and to $104.5°$ in $\ce{H2O}$.
- In a trigonal bipyramid, a lone pair goes to an equatorial position, because that avoids the extra $90°$ repulsions an axial lone pair would have.
- The shape describes only the atoms, while the arrangement describes all the pairs, lone pairs included.
</KeyIdeas>
