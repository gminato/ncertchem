<Hook>
A sealed bottle of water left on a warm table keeps the same water level for days. Yet water molecules are escaping into the air above it and falling back all the time. How can a level stay exactly where it is while the water is on the move? The same idea governs the chemical reactions in this Section.
</Hook>

## Reactions that run both ways

Many reactions do not run to completion. Put reactants in a closed vessel and they form products, but those products can also react to give the original reactants. Write such a change with a double arrow, $\ce{A + B <=> C + D}$, and read it as two reactions going on at once.

At the start only the reactants are present, so the forward reaction is fast and the reverse reaction cannot happen. As C and D build up, the reverse reaction speeds up, and the forward reaction slows because A and B are being used up. Sooner or later the two rates become equal. From then on the amounts of A, B, C and D no longer change. This is **chemical equilibrium**, and the word that matters most is *dynamic*. The reactions have not stopped. They cancel out.

## The isotope test

Chemists could not watch single molecules, so they tested the idea with isotopes. Ammonia is made from nitrogen and hydrogen, $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$. Run the synthesis under the same conditions, but use deuterium, D₂, in place of ordinary hydrogen. Both mixtures settle to equilibria of the same composition. The only difference is that the deuterium mixture holds D₂ and ND₃ where the other holds H₂ and NH₃.

Now mix the two equilibrium mixtures and leave them for a while. The total amount of ammonia is unchanged. A mass spectrometer, however, finds every form of the molecule: NH₃, NH₂D, NHD₂ and ND₃, together with H₂, HD and D₂. Hydrogen and deuterium atoms have changed partners. That can happen only if the forward and reverse reactions were still running in the mixture. A reaction that had simply stopped would have left the isotopes where they were.

The same holds when the reaction is approached from the other side. Start with H₂ and I₂ and hydrogen iodide forms. Start with HI alone and H₂ and I₂ appear. If the total number of H and I atoms is the same, both routes end at one equilibrium mixture. The scrolly below follows the first route, and the picture of the flask is the one to keep in mind for the rest of the Section.

<Step>
Begin with H₂ and I₂ alone, 0.020 mol L⁻¹ of each, and no HI. The forward rate is $k_f[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]$. With $k_f = 100\ \text{L mol}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, a rate constant chosen for this picture and not measured, the forward rate is $100 \times 0.020 \times 0.020$, which is 40 mmol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ on the bars. Nothing runs in reverse, because there is no HI to react.
</Step>
<Step>
Some H₂ and I₂ have reacted and HI has begun to form. The reverse rate, $k_r[\ce{HI}]^2$, is no longer zero. The forward bar has shrunk because the reactants are being used up, and the reverse bar has grown. The picture shows the moment [HI] reaches half its equilibrium value, about 0.3 s after the start. The reverse rate is then still a small fraction of the forward rate.
</Step>
<Step>
The two bars now match. The forward rate has fallen to about 2.05 mmol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, and the reverse rate has risen to the same value. This is equilibrium. Both reactions still run, and at this composition their rates are equal, so the amounts of H₂, I₂ and HI no longer change.
</Step>
<Step>
Plot the concentrations against time and the three curves level off at about 3 s. Every later point is the same, so the mixture looks still. Both reactions continue, each at 2.05 mmol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. Molecules of H₂, I₂ and HI are being made and broken at equal rates, which is the dynamic state.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the isotope test in numbers. Suppose the mixture holds equal numbers of H and D atoms, and they change partners at random. Then each N–H position in an ammonia molecule is D with probability one half. The four forms then appear in the ratio 1 : 3 : 3 : 1, that is NH₃ 12.5 %, NH₂D 37.5 %, NHD₂ 37.5 % and ND₃ 12.5 %. Deuterium has reached the ammonia, which is the dynamic proof in one picture.
</Step>

## Speed and direction

A rate depends on how often molecules meet, which depends on their amounts. In the model used here, the forward rate of H₂ + I₂ is $k_f[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]$ and the reverse rate is $k_r[\ce{HI}]^2$. The rate constants set how quickly the approach happens, but not where it ends. Doubling both of them makes the approach twice as quick and leaves the final mixture unchanged.

<KeyIdeas>
- At equilibrium the forward and reverse reactions both go on, at equal rates. Constant amounts are the result of that balance and do not mean the reactions have stopped.
- The isotope test shows the dynamic state directly: deuterium labels move between molecules inside the equilibrium mixture.
- Equilibrium can be reached from the reactants, from the products, or from a mixture of both, if the total number of atoms of each element is the same.
- In the model, the rates follow the mass-action laws: each concentration is raised to its stoichiometric coefficient, and the forward and reverse rates are equal at equilibrium.
- Starting amounts change the composition at equilibrium, but not the equilibrium constant, which changes only with temperature. Section 6.3 defines that constant.
</KeyIdeas>
