## 6.11 Ionization of acids and bases

Most chemistry in the kitchen, in the body and in the lab happens in water, so it matters how an acid or a base breaks up in water. A **strong acid** such as hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl}$, ionises almost completely. Nearly every molecule that dissolves gives a proton to water, and the solution is mostly $\ce{H3O+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$. A **weak acid** such as acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, ionises only in part, and most of its molecules stay whole.

Strength is also a question of who keeps the proton. A strong acid gives its proton away so readily that its conjugate base hardly takes one back: the chloride ion is a far weaker base than water. A weak acid holds on to its proton, so its conjugate base is comparatively strong. In every acid and conjugate base pair, the stronger acid has the weaker conjugate base, and the weaker acid has the stronger one.

The contrast shows up in numbers. A $0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ solution of a strong acid has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. A $0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ solution of acetic acid, with $K_a = 1.74 \times 10^{-5}$ from the table of weak acids, has $[\ce{H3O+}] = 1.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. Only about $1.3\ \%$ of its molecules have ionised. The full arithmetic for weak acids comes later in this unit; here the point is the gap between the two.

## 6.11.1 The ionic product of water

Water is unusual because it can act as an acid and as a base at the same time. One molecule gives a proton, and a second molecule takes it:

$$
\ce{H2O(l) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

The concentration of pure liquid water, about $55.55\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, is effectively constant, so it is folded into the constant. What remains is the **ionic product of water**:

$$
K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}]
$$

At $298\ \text{K}$ the ions in pure water are present at $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, so

$$
K_w = (1.0 \times 10^{-7})^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{mol}^2\,\text{L}^{-2}
$$

The fraction of water that has ionised is small. The ratio of dissociated to undissociated molecules is about $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$, so the equilibrium lies almost entirely on the side of intact water.

Kw is an equilibrium constant, so it changes with temperature. The ionisation of water absorbs heat, so warmer water ionises more and Kw rises. A standard estimate, which uses the enthalpy of ionisation of water, $+55.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, gives $K_w \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-14}$ at $310\ \text{K}$. The estimate is ours, not a value printed in NCERT, but its trend is the one that matters.

</Step>

## 6.11.2 The pH scale

Hydronium concentrations in the lab run from about $1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ down to $10^{-14}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, which is awkward to read. The **pH** turns the number into a logarithm:

$$
\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\ce{H3O+}]
$$

Each pH unit is a factor of ten in $[\ce{H3O+}]$, so a solution at pH 3 is ten times more acidic than one at pH 4. Take a lemon-water sample at $[\ce{H3O+}] = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. Its pH is $3 - \log 4.0 = 2.40$, and it is acidic. For a basic sample, use pOH instead. A solution with pH 9.40 has pOH $= 14.00 - 9.40 = 4.60$, so $[\ce{OH-}] = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. At $298\ \text{K}$ the two scales are tied together:

$$
\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = \text{p}K_w = 14.00
$$

The pH of some common substances spans almost the whole scale. NCERT's table of common substances lists values from about 1.2 for gastric juice, through 7.4 for human blood, to about 13 for 0.1 M sodium hydroxide, and the simulation below places each one on the same scale.

You measure pH in three ways. **Universal indicator paper** has several strips that change colour across the range, and it reads to about half a pH unit. A **pH meter** measures the electrical potential that depends on $[\ce{H3O+}]$ and is far more precise. **Indicators** such as phenolphthalein and bromothymol blue are weak acids whose acid form and conjugate base have different colours, so each changes colour over a limited pH range.

### The water term in very dilute acid

A very dilute strong acid is where water cannot be ignored. Acid and water both supply $\ce{H3O+}$. If the acid gives $c$ and water gives $x$, then $[\ce{H3O+}] = c + x$, and $[\ce{OH-}] = x$, so $K_w = (c + x)x$. Solving for $x$ gives a small quadratic. The water term matters only when $c$ is near $10^{-7}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ or less. The pH is then always below 7 for an acid, however dilute, and it approaches 7 without reaching it.

<KeyIdeas>
- $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\ce{H3O+}]$: each pH unit is a factor of ten in hydronium concentration, and a solution is acidic when $[\ce{H3O+}] > [\ce{OH-}]$.
- $K_w = [\ce{H3O+}][\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at $298\ \text{K}$, and it rises with temperature, so the neutral pH is not always 7.
- Neutral means $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{OH-}] = \sqrt{K_w}$, whatever the temperature. At $310\ \text{K}$ this gives pH 6.81.
- In very dilute strong acid, water supplies $\ce{H3O+}$ as well, so $3.0 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ HCl has pH 6.94, not 7.52.
- At $298\ \text{K}$, $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00$ links the two scales. Measure pH with indicator paper, a pH meter or an indicator.
</KeyIdeas>
