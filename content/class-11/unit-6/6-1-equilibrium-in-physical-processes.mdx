<Hook>
Twist the cap off a cold bottle of soda and it hisses. Leave the same bottle open on the table and within an hour it tastes flat. The gas was in the drink the whole time, so what changed when the cap came off? And why does a sealed flask of water stay full while an open dish of water slowly disappears?
</Hook>

Equilibrium is not only a property of chemical reactions. Phase changes and the dissolving of solids and gases settle into the same kind of balance, and these physical processes are the simplest place to see what equilibrium means. The idea is always the same: two opposite processes run side by side, and when they run at equal rates, the amounts on each side stop moving.

## (a) Solid and liquid

Put ice and water together in a perfectly insulated flask at 273 K and atmospheric pressure. Nothing seems to happen. The mass of ice stays the same and the temperature does not drift. The calm is misleading. Water molecules strike the ice, stick to it and freeze on, while molecules leave the ice and join the liquid. The two directions run at the same rate, so neither side wins.

Only one temperature gives this balance at a given pressure. For a pure substance at 1 atm (1.013 bar), the temperature at which its solid and liquid coexist is called the normal melting point, or normal freezing point. For water it is 273 K. Change the pressure and the balance point moves to a new temperature.

## (b) Liquid and vapour

Imagine a dish of water inside a sealed box that has a pressure gauge fitted to it. At first the gauge reads almost nothing. Molecules leave the surface, and each one that escapes adds to the vapour above it. As the vapour thickens, more molecules strike the surface and are caught again. The gauge needle climbs more slowly and finally stops at a fixed reading. At that point evaporation and condensation run at equal rates, and the pressure is called the equilibrium vapour pressure.

The vapour pressure depends on the liquid and on the temperature, and it does not depend on how much liquid is in the box. Water at 25 °C has a vapour pressure of about 23.7 mmHg, which is 0.0316 bar. At 100 °C it reaches about 760 mmHg, one atmosphere. A liquid with a higher vapour pressure at the same temperature is more volatile. Ethanol at 25 °C has about 58.8 mmHg, more than twice the value for water, so ethanol evaporates more readily and boils at a lower temperature.

Boiling is the same balance seen from another side. A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure of the air or the gas above it. Two boiling points are named for that reason. The standard boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure equals 1 bar, about 99.6 °C for water. The normal boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure equals 1 atm (1.01325 bar), which is 100.0 °C for water. On a mountain, the air pressure is lower, so water boils below 100 °C and needs a pressure cooker to cook faster.

## (c) Solid and vapour

Some solids skip the liquid altogether. Solid iodine, placed in a closed vessel, sublimes into a violet vapour. The colour deepens for a while and then stops getting stronger. Solid and vapour are then in equilibrium:

$$
\ce{I2(s) <=> I2(g)}
$$

Camphor behaves the same way, as $\ce{C10H16O(s) <=> C10H16O(g)}$. Ammonium chloride is a special case. Its vapour is mostly ammonia and hydrogen chloride, which recombine as the vapour cools, so the process is a dissociation as well as a sublimation:

$$
\ce{NH4Cl(s) <=> NH3(g) + HCl(g)}
$$

Whatever the substance, the vapour pressure above the solid is fixed by the temperature alone, just as it is for a liquid.

## (d) Dissolution of solids and gases

### Solids in liquids

Dissolve sugar in hot water until no more will go in. The solution is then saturated: it holds all the sugar it can at that temperature. Cool it, and crystals begin to settle out. A saturated solution is an equilibrium between the solid and the dissolved sugar:

$$
\ce{C12H22O11(s) <=> C12H22O11(aq)}
$$

The rate of dissolution equals the rate of crystallisation. Because the amount that dissolves depends on temperature, the solubility of a solid is fixed at a given temperature and changes when the temperature changes.

How do we know the exchange is real? Label some sugar molecules with a radioactive atom and drop them into a saturated solution of ordinary sugar. At first the solution carries no label. After a while the label shows up in the crystals too, although the crystals began without it. The molecules have been swapping between solid and solution the whole time, and the labelled fraction in the solution settles to a constant value.

### Gases in liquids

A soda bottle is sealed with carbon dioxide held above the liquid at a few bar. Some of the gas dissolves, and the equilibrium is

$$
\ce{CO2(g) <=> CO2(aq)}
$$

Henry's law describes this balance. At a fixed temperature, the concentration of a dissolved gas is proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid:

$$
c = k_\text{H}\,p
$$

Here $k_\text{H}$ is the Henry's-law constant, in mol L⁻¹ bar⁻¹, and $p$ is in bar. A bigger $k_\text{H}$ means the gas dissolves more readily. When the cap comes off, the pressure above the liquid drops to the small partial pressure of CO₂ in air, and the dissolved amount must fall to match it. The gas bubbles out, and the drink goes flat. Gas solubility also falls as the temperature rises, which is why a warm drink fizzes less than a cold one.

## (e) General characteristics of physical equilibria

The four kinds of physical equilibrium above share a pattern. At a fixed temperature, one measurable quantity stays constant once equilibrium is reached.

| Process | What stays constant |
| --- | --- |
| $\ce{H2O(l) <=> H2O(g)}$ | vapour pressure of the liquid |
| $\ce{H2O(s) <=> H2O(l)}$ | melting point, at a fixed pressure |
| $\ce{C12H22O11(s) <=> C12H22O11(aq)}$ | concentration of the saturated solution |
| $\ce{CO2(g) <=> CO2(aq)}$ | the ratio of dissolved to gaseous concentration |

Five features hold for all of them:

- Equilibrium is possible only in a closed system held at a fixed temperature.
- The two opposite processes run at the same rate, so the state is dynamic but stable.
- Every measurable property of the system stays the same once equilibrium is reached.
- One quantity in the table above characterises the equilibrium at that temperature.
- The size of that quantity, at any moment, shows how far the process has already gone.

<KeyIdeas>
- At equilibrium the forward and reverse processes continue at equal rates. The amounts stay fixed, but nothing has stopped.
- A liquid in a sealed container settles at a vapour pressure set by the temperature alone: 23.7 mmHg for water at 25 °C.
- The normal boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure equals 1 atm (1.01325 bar): 100.0 °C for water. The standard boiling point at 1 bar is 99.6 °C.
- For a dissolved gas, $c = k_\text{H}\,p$ at fixed temperature. Opening a bottle drops the pressure, so the gas leaves the liquid; cold liquids hold more gas.
- Equilibrium exists only in a closed system at a given temperature, and the constant it leaves behind tells you how far the process has gone.
</KeyIdeas>
