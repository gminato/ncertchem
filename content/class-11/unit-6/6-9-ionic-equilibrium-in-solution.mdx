<Hook>
A school conductivity tester is a battery, a bulb and two bare electrodes. Dip the electrodes in a glass of sugar syrup and the bulb stays dark. Dip them in a glass of lemon water and the bulb lights, though only dimly. Both are water-based drinks, so why does one carry a current and the other not?
</Hook>

Michael Faraday sorted dissolved substances by how they behave in water. Some solutions carry an electric current. These are called electrolytes, and the current is carried by the ions in them. Others, like sugar, carry no current at all, because they dissolve as whole molecules and leave no ions behind. Those are non-electrolytes.

Faraday then split the electrolytes in two. A strong electrolyte breaks into ions almost completely when it dissolves. A weak electrolyte breaks into ions only partly, and most of its molecules stay whole. The difference is in how far the splitting goes, not in how much salt or acid you add.

The idea behind the conductance test is simple. The more ions there are per litre, the more charge the solution can carry, and the brighter the bulb. Two solutions at the same concentration can therefore differ a great deal, depending on how many of their molecules have split.

## Strong and weak electrolytes

A solution of sodium chloride holds almost only sodium ions and chloride ions. A solution of acetic acid holds mostly whole acetic acid molecules, plus a few acetate ions and hydronium ions. The gap is wide: sodium chloride is ionised almost completely, while acetic acid is ionised by only a few per cent. The scrolly below follows a 0.10 mol L⁻¹ solution of acetic acid, then a solution of hydrochloric acid of the same concentration, and then the equilibrium that holds the acetic acid in its partly ionised state.

<Scrolly animation="ionic-electrolytes">
<Step>
Start with 0.10 mol L⁻¹ acetic acid. Most of its molecules stay whole. Only a few pass a proton to water, and the acetate and hydronium ions they form can also come back together. Solving the equilibrium exactly gives a degree of ionisation of 1.3 %, which fits a beaker with only a small share of its molecules split.
</Step>
<Step>
Now take hydrochloric acid at the same concentration. Every molecule has split into hydronium and chloride ions, so the degree of ionisation is 100 %. The solution holds about 0.10 mol L⁻¹ of each ion, and its pH is −log(0.10) = 1.00. The same concentration of ions in the two beakers is not the same thing: the strong acid has far more of them.
</Step>
<Step>
Acetic acid sits in an equilibrium between molecules and ions. Its ionisation constant is $K_a = 1.74 \times 10^{-5}$ from NCERT Table 6.6. Put the equilibrium amounts into the expression, with $[\ce{H3O+}] = [\ce{CH3COO-}] = 1.31 \times 10^{-3}$ mol L⁻¹ and $[\ce{CH3COOH}] = 0.0987$ mol L⁻¹, and the value returns to $1.74 \times 10^{-5}$. The equilibrium is dynamic, so ions form and recombine at equal rates.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Why a few per cent is enough to matter

Even a weak electrolyte does not keep splitting without limit. Ions recombine into molecules, so the numbers settle at a balance, and that balance is set by the ionisation constant. This is the start of ionic equilibrium. Section 6.11 uses the same balance to work out pH.

For a weak acid HA, the equilibrium is written as

$$
\ce{HA + H2O <=> H3O+ + A-}, \qquad K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$

Water is the solvent and its concentration hardly changes, so it does not appear in $K_a$. Acids, bases and salts are all electrolytes, and each may be strong or weak. That is why the next Section looks at what makes an acid or a base in the first place.

<KeyIdeas>
- Electrolytes carry a current in solution because their ions move. Non-electrolytes, such as sugar, dissolve as whole molecules and carry none.
- A strong electrolyte is ionised almost completely, so a given concentration gives a large number of ions.
- A weak electrolyte is ionised only partly, and an equilibrium sits between its molecules and its ions.
- For acetic acid at 0.10 mol L⁻¹, the degree of ionisation is 1.31 %, and $K_a = 1.74 \times 10^{-5}$ ties the molecules and ions together.
- The bulb responds to the ions actually present, not to the concentration written on the label.
</KeyIdeas>
