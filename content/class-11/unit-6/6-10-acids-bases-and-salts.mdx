<Hook>
Lemon juice on the tongue is sharp. The stomach is full of strong acid, and yet an antacid tablet can calm it down. Soap, which is basic, feels slippery between the fingers. Three definitions have been offered for the question "what makes a substance an acid?", and each one covers a different part of this puzzle.
</Hook>

Most acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red. Bases taste bitter, feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. Acids and bases react with each other to give salts, and the salt is the other half of the story: common salt, for example, is what you get when hydrochloric acid meets sodium hydroxide.

Taste and litmus are a good start, but a chemist needs a definition that predicts what will happen in a new reaction. The next three subsections give the three definitions in order of increasing scope, and each one contains the one before it. The scrolly below shows all three on one picture, so you can see how the same reactions fit each definition.

Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid because this transfer goes almost to completion.

<Step>
Now the proton, shown as the small red dot, has moved onto the water molecule. The water has become $\ce{H3O+}$, and the chloride ion is left behind. ## 6.10.1 The Arrhenius concept

Svante Arrhenius worked on electrolyte solutions, and his definition follows from water. An acid is a substance that gives hydrogen ions in water. A base is a substance that gives hydroxide ions in water. For hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, the two equations are

$$
\ce{HCl(aq) -> H+(aq) + Cl-(aq)} \qquad \ce{NaOH(aq) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

A bare proton cannot survive in water. It attaches to a water molecule and forms hydronium, $\ce{H3O+}$. Throughout this Section, $\ce{H+}$ is used only as a shorthand for the hydrated proton, and the proper symbol in water is $\ce{H3O+}$. The Arrhenius definition works well for water solutions. It has a limit, though: ammonia has no hydroxyl group, yet it is clearly basic, so the definition misses it.

## 6.10.2 The Brønsted–Lowry concept

Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry widened the definition. An acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor. Nothing in this definition needs water, and it covers ammonia.

Take ammonia dissolving in water:

$$
\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

Water donates a proton here, so it is the Brønsted acid, and ammonia accepts it, so it is the base. Read the equation the other way and the roles swap: the ammonium ion is now the acid, and the hydroxide ion is the base.

Two species that differ by exactly one proton form a conjugate pair. The species that has one extra proton is the conjugate acid, and the one with one fewer is the conjugate base. In the reaction above, $\ce{NH4+}$ is the conjugate acid of $\ce{NH3}$, and $\ce{OH-}$ is the conjugate base of $\ce{H2O}$. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base, and a weak acid has a strong one.

Water can play either role. In hydrochloric acid, water accepts the proton and acts as the base. In ammonia solution, it donates one and acts as the acid. A species that can do both, such as water, $\ce{HCO3-}$ or $\ce{HSO4-}$, is called amphiprotic. For the ammonia equilibrium the conjugate constant is worked out from the water constant:

$$
K_a(\ce{NH4+}) \times K_b(\ce{NH3}) = K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \quad \Rightarrow \quad K_a(\ce{NH4+}) = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.77 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.6 \times 10^{-10}
$$

Here $K_b(\ce{NH3}) = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}$ is the value in Table 6.7. The weak base ammonia therefore has a weak conjugate acid, which is the rule stated above.

## 6.10.3 The Lewis concept

G. N. Lewis took the definition one step further. An acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a base is an electron-pair donor. For bases the two Brønsted and Lewis definitions agree, because the base supplies a lone pair in both. For acids they differ. Many acids have no proton to donate, and yet they still react, such as the boron trifluoride reaction in the scrolly.

Electron-deficient species are Lewis acids. Examples include $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$, $\ce{Co^3+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$, each of which can take up an electron pair. Species with a lone pair to give are Lewis bases, such as $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{OH-}$. A bare proton is a Lewis acid as well, since it accepts a lone pair from hydroxide.

$$
\ce{BF3 + :NH3 -> BF3:NH3} \qquad \ce{Mg^2+ + 6H2O -> [Mg(H2O)6]^2+}
$$

Every Brønsted acid–base reaction is also a Lewis one, because the base donates the lone pair that the proton accepts. The reverse is not true: the Lewis concept covers reactions with no proton anywhere in them.

<KeyIdeas>
- The Arrhenius definition says an acid gives H⁺ and a base gives OH⁻ in water. It works for water solutions but misses ammonia, which has no hydroxyl group.
- A Brønsted acid donates a proton and a Brønsted base accepts one. Water can act as either, so it is amphiprotic.
- Two species in a conjugate pair differ by exactly one proton. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base.
- In the Lewis picture an acid accepts an electron pair and a base donates one, which covers boron trifluoride with no proton at all.
- $\ce{H+}$ is shorthand for the hydrated proton. In water, write $\ce{H3O+}$, which is hydronium.
</KeyIdeas>
