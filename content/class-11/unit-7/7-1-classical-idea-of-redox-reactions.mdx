## 7.1 Classical Idea of Redox Reactions

Chemistry is fundamentally the study of how matter transforms from one form into another. Among all chemical transformations, few classes are as widespread or technologically vital as **redox reactions**. They supply energy when fuels burn in household stoves and vehicle engines, generate electricity in torch batteries and mobile phone cells, extract metals from natural ores, and drive biological respiration in every living cell.

Historically, chemists recognised these transformations long before they understood electrons or atomic orbitals. They grouped reactions by visible additions and removals of elements, creating the classical framework of oxidation and reduction.

### The historical origin: addition of oxygen

Originally, the word **oxidation** described the direct addition of oxygen to an element or a compound. Because dioxygen gas makes up approximately $20\%$ of the Earth's atmosphere, many elements spontaneously burn in air or react over time to form stable oxides:

$$
\ce{2Cu(s) + O2(g) -> 2CuO(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}
$$

In both reactions, copper and carbon combine directly with oxygen, so both are oxidised according to the earliest definition. Compounds undergo similar changes; for example, burning natural gas oxidises methane to carbon dioxide and water:

$$
\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}
$$

### Broadening to the removal of hydrogen

When chemists examined the combustion of methane more closely, they noticed that carbon did not merely gain oxygen—it also parted with four hydrogen atoms. This observation prompted chemists to broaden the definition: **oxidation also includes the removal of hydrogen from a substance**.

Consider the oxidation of hydrogen sulphide by chlorine gas:

$$
\ce{2H2S(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g) + 2S(s)}
$$

Here, hydrogen is stripped away from sulphur, converting hydrogen sulphide into elemental yellow sulphur. Even though no oxygen appears anywhere in the reaction vessel, $\ce{H2S}$ is classified as oxidised under this broadened definition.

### Broadening to electronegative and electropositive elements

As chemical discovery advanced, it became clear that oxygen is simply one representative of a larger family: the electronegative elements (such as fluorine, chlorine, bromine, and sulphur). Combining a metal with chlorine or sulphur produces compounds with properties analogous to metal oxides.

When calcium metal burns in dry chlorine gas:

$$
\ce{Ca(s) + Cl2(g) -> CaCl2(s)}
$$

Calcium combines with an electronegative element (chlorine), so calcium is oxidised. Similarly, when iron reacts with molten sulphur to yield iron(II) sulphide:

$$
\ce{Fe(s) + S(s) -> FeS(s)}
$$

Iron is oxidised because the electronegative element sulphur is added to it.

Conversely, if adding an electronegative element is oxidation, then **removing an electropositive element** from a compound must also count as oxidation. When chlorine gas oxidises potassium iodide in aqueous solution:

$$
\ce{2KI(aq) + Cl2(g) -> 2KCl(aq) + I2(s)}
$$

The electropositive potassium atom ($\ce{K}$) is stripped away from iodide, converting it into free elemental iodine ($\ce{I2}$). Potassium iodide is therefore oxidised. A classic analytical illustration occurs when hydrogen peroxide oxidises potassium ferrocyanide to potassium ferricyanide:

$$
\ce{2K4[Fe(CN)6](aq) + H2O2(aq) -> 2K3[Fe(CN)6](aq) + 2KOH(aq)}
$$

Potassium (an electropositive element) is removed from the complex anion, oxidising ferrocyanide to ferricyanide.

<Step>
Start with the historical root. In an atmosphere carrying twenty percent dioxygen, exposed elements slowly combine with oxygen to form oxides. When copper metal is heated in air or charcoal burns, oxygen is added directly to the substance. In copper oxide reduction, carbon seizes oxygen from copper(II) oxide to yield carbon dioxide, while copper metal is left behind. Carbon gains oxygen and is oxidised; copper(II) oxide loses oxygen and is reduced.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, chemists observed that burning hydrogen sulphide strips hydrogen away from sulphur, leaving elemental sulphur. Does oxidation require oxygen alone? Not necessarily. Stripping hydrogen from a substance produces a comparable change in chemical nature. When hydrogen sulphide meets chlorine gas, sulphur surrenders its hydrogen atoms to form solid sulphur, while chlorine acquires those hydrogen atoms to yield hydrogen chloride. Hydrogen sulphide is oxidised because it loses hydrogen; chlorine is reduced because it gains hydrogen.
</Step>
<Step>
Because oxygen is merely one electronegative element among non-metals, chemists generalised the rule. Combining a metal with chlorine, fluorine, or sulphur mirrors combining it with oxygen. When shiny calcium metal burns in a jar of pale green chlorine gas, each calcium atom bonds with electronegative chlorine to form calcium chloride. Calcium is oxidised because an electronegative element is added to it; chlorine is reduced because electropositive calcium has been added to it.
</Step>
<Step>
The final classical extension looked at the partner of hydrogen: electropositive elements. If adding an electronegative element counts as oxidation, removing an electropositive element must count as oxidation too. When chlorine gas is bubbled through a colourless aqueous solution of potassium iodide, electropositive potassium is stripped away from iodide ions, yielding violet-brown elemental iodine. Potassium iodide is oxidised through the removal of potassium; chlorine is reduced through the addition of potassium.
</Step>
<Step>
These four expansions unite into a single symmetric ledger. Oxidation represents the addition of oxygen or any electronegative element, or the removal of hydrogen or any electropositive element. Reduction represents the exact mirror image. Because every atom surrendered by one reactant must be accepted by another, neither process can ever occur in isolation. They are twin faces of the same chemical transaction, which is why we speak of redox reactions.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

### The classical definition of reduction

In early metallurgy, **reduction** described the extraction of a free metal from its mineral ore—literally reducing the bulk mass of the rocky ore by removing oxygen. For example, heating copper(II) oxide over charcoal strips away oxygen:

$$
\ce{2CuO(s) + C(s) -> 2Cu(s) + CO2(g)}
$$

Because oxygen is removed from $\ce{CuO}$, copper(II) oxide is reduced. Heating red mercuric oxide also liberates mercury metal:

$$
\ce{2HgO(s) -> 2Hg(l) + O2(g)}
$$

Just like oxidation, the definition of reduction was systematically broadened to cover four symmetric operations:

1. **Removal of oxygen** from a substance (as in $\ce{2HgO -> 2Hg + O2}$).
2. **Removal of an electronegative element** from a substance. In the reduction of iron(III) chloride by hydrogen:
   $$
   \ce{2FeCl3(aq) + H2(g) -> 2FeCl2(aq) + 2HCl(aq)}
   $$
   Chlorine is removed from $\ce{FeCl3}$, reducing it to $\ce{FeCl2}$.
3. **Addition of hydrogen** to a substance. When vegetable oils or ethene are hydrogenated over a nickel catalyst:
   $$
   \ce{CH2=CH2(g) + H2(g) -> CH3-CH3(g)}
   $$
   Ethene gains hydrogen, so ethene is reduced.
4. **Addition of an electropositive element** to a substance. When mercury(II) chloride reacts with tin(II) chloride:
   $$
   \ce{2HgCl2(aq) + SnCl2(aq) -> Hg2Cl2(s) + SnCl4(aq)}
   $$
   Mercury (an electropositive metal) is added to mercuric chloride, reducing it to mercurous chloride ($\ce{Hg2Cl2}$). Simultaneously, electronegative chlorine is added to $\ce{SnCl2}$, oxidising it to tin(IV) chloride ($\ce{SnCl4}$).

### Oxidation and reduction always occur together

Examine the reaction between mercury(II) chloride and tin(II) chloride again:

$$
\ce{2HgCl2(aq) + SnCl2(aq) -> Hg2Cl2(s) + SnCl4(aq)}
$$

While $\ce{HgCl2}$ is being reduced through the addition of mercury, $\ce{SnCl2}$ is being oxidised through the addition of chlorine. The oxygen, halogen, or electropositive atom removed from one species does not vanish into empty space—it is captured by the other species.

Whenever oxidation occurs, reduction must take place simultaneously in the same chemical system. To honour this inseparable union, chemists fused the two words into the term **redox** (reduction–oxidation).

### Worked Problem: Classifying classical redox changes

Let us test these classical criteria on three distinct chemical transformations:

1. **Reaction 1:** $\ce{2FeCl3(aq) + H2S(g) -> 2FeCl2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) + S(s)}$  
   - $\ce{FeCl3}$ loses electronegative chlorine to become $\ce{FeCl2}$; hence $\ce{FeCl3}$ is **reduced**.  
   - $\ce{H2S}$ loses electropositive hydrogen to form solid $\ce{S}$; hence $\ce{H2S}$ is **oxidised**.
2. **Reaction 2:** $\ce{ZnO(s) + C(s) -> Zn(s) + CO(g)}$  
   - Zinc oxide loses oxygen to form zinc metal; hence $\ce{ZnO}$ is **reduced**.  
   - Carbon gains oxygen to form carbon monoxide; hence $\ce{C}$ is **oxidised**.
3. **Reaction 3:** $\ce{2Na(s) + H2(g) -> 2NaH(s)}$  
   - Sodium combines with hydrogen to form sodium hydride. But sodium is more electropositive than hydrogen (electronegativities: $\text{Na} \approx 0.93$, $\text{H} \approx 2.20$).  
   - Hydrogen is the more electronegative partner here! Adding electronegative hydrogen to sodium means sodium is **oxidised**, while hydrogen gains electropositive sodium and is **reduced**.

Reaction 3 highlights the natural limit of classical definitions: relying on vague notions of "electronegative" or "electropositive" becomes confusing when hydrogen behaves as the more electronegative element. To understand reactions like $\ce{2Na + H2 -> 2NaH}$ with complete clarity, we must turn to the transfer of electrons.

<KeyIdeas>
- The classical definition began with oxygen: oxidation was the addition of oxygen, and reduction was its removal.
- The concept expanded systematically: oxidation is the addition of oxygen or an electronegative element, or the removal of hydrogen or an electropositive element.
- Reduction is the exact converse: removal of oxygen or an electronegative element, or addition of hydrogen or an electropositive element.
- Oxidation and reduction are inseparable: an element surrendered by one reactant must be assimilated by another, meaning every such reaction is a coupled redox process.
</KeyIdeas>
