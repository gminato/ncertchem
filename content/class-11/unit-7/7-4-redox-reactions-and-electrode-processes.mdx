All the chemical energy liberated by this spontaneous electron transfer is dissipated directly into the solution as heat. But what if we could intercept those leaping electrons, guide them through an external copper wire, and make them power a motor or light a bulb before they ever reach the copper ions?

## Direct versus indirect electron transfer

When zinc metal is placed directly into an aqueous solution of copper sulphate, oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously at the immediate metal-solution boundary:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) -> Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

In this direct transfer, each zinc atom gives up two electrons directly to an adjacent hydrated copper ion ($\ce{Cu^{2+}}$) touching the strip. The transfer takes place over sub-nanometre atomic distances. Because the electrons jump directly from donor to acceptor without traversing an external conductor, the free energy change ($\Delta G^\circ < 0$) cannot do electrical work; it is degraded into thermal energy, warming the solution ($q < 0$).

To harness this energy as continuous electrical power, we must separate the electron donor from the electron acceptor. This fundamental insight leads to the construction of a galvanic or voltaic cell, historically demonstrated by John Frederic Daniell in 1836.

## The Daniell cell and redox couples

Indirect electron transfer requires dividing the overall redox reaction into two distinct vessels, termed **half-cells**:

1. In the first beaker, a metallic zinc rod is immersed in an aqueous solution of zinc sulphate ($\ce{ZnSO4}$).
2. In the second beaker, a metallic copper strip is immersed in an aqueous solution of copper sulphate ($\ce{CuSO4}$).

At the interface between the metal strip and its electrolyte solution in each beaker, both the oxidised and reduced forms of the same chemical element are in intimate contact. Such an intimate pairing is formally defined as a **redox couple**:

> [!NOTE]
> A **redox couple** consists of the oxidised and reduced forms of a substance taking part in an oxidation or reduction half-reaction.

By international convention, a redox couple is denoted with the oxidised form written before the reduced form, separated by a vertical line or slash representing the phase boundary:

$$
\ce{Zn^{2+}/Zn} \quad \text{and} \quad \ce{Cu^{2+}/Cu}
$$

In each half-cell, an equilibrium tendency exists between the neutral metal atoms on the electrode and the metal cations in the surrounding solution. However, when the two beakers merely sit side by side on a workbench, no reaction occurs in either beaker because neither half-reaction can proceed without a complementary partner to accept or supply electrons.

## The salt bridge and complete circuit

To enable electron transfer without allowing the two electrolyte solutions to mix uncontrollably, two connections are required:

1. **The external circuit:** A metallic wire (such as copper wire) connects the two metal rods through a switch and an ammeter or voltmeter.
2. **The internal circuit (the salt bridge):** An inverted U-shaped glass tube is filled with an inert electrolyte — typically potassium chloride ($\ce{KCl}$), potassium nitrate ($\ce{KNO3}$), or ammonium nitrate ($\ce{NH4NO3}$) — dissolved in warm agar-agar jelly. When cooled, the agar sets into a porous gelatinous plug.

The salt bridge performs two vital functions:
- It maintains electrical neutrality in both half-cells by allowing counterions to migrate.
- It provides an electrical contact between the two solutions without permitting bulk mixing of $\ce{ZnSO4}$ and $\ce{CuSO4}$.

## Electrode potential and standard electrode potential

At the junction between a metal rod and an electrolyte solution containing its own ions, an electric double layer forms. Metal atoms tend to dissolve as cations leaving electrons on the rod, while cations in solution tend to gain electrons and deposit on the rod. When dynamic balance is reached, a potential difference develops between the electrode and the surrounding electrolyte:

> [!NOTE]
> The electrical potential difference developed between an electrode and its surrounding electrolyte solution is called the **electrode potential**.

When every species participating in the electrode reaction is present in its thermodynamic standard state:
- Every dissolved solute species is present at unit concentration ($1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ or $1\ \text{M}$).
- Any gas taking part in the electrode reaction is maintained at a standard pressure of $1\ \text{bar}$. (Note: NCERT mentions $1\ \text{atm}$; since $1\ \text{atm} = 1.01325\ \text{bar}$, the numerical difference in electrode potential is negligible, below $0.001\ \text{V}$).
- The temperature is maintained at $298\ \text{K}$ ($25\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

The resulting potential is termed the **Standard Electrode Potential**, denoted by the symbol $E^\circ$.

### The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

An isolated single electrode potential cannot be measured directly, because any electrical measurement requires a complete circuit involving a second electrode. Therefore, a universal reference benchmark is chosen: the **Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)**.

By IUPAC international convention, the standard electrode potential of the hydrogen electrode is assigned the value of exactly zero volts at all temperatures:

$$
\ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- <=> H2(g)} \quad E^\circ = 0.00\ \text{V}
$$

The SHE consists of a platinised platinum foil immersed in an aqueous acidic solution with $[\ce{H+}] = 1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, over which pure hydrogen gas is bubbled continuously at $1\ \text{bar}$ pressure and $298\ \text{K}$. To measure the standard potential of any couple, it is paired with the SHE in a galvanic cell, and the measured cell potential is ascribed entirely to that couple.

## Standard reduction potentials and Table 7.1

All standard electrode potentials tabulated in IUPAC tables and NCERT Table 7.1 are defined as **standard reduction potentials** ($E^\circ_{\text{red}}$), representing the half-reaction written strictly in the reduction direction:

$$
\text{Oxidised form} + n\ce{e-} \longrightarrow \text{Reduced form}
$$

The magnitude and sign of $E^\circ$ provide a direct thermodynamic measure of the relative tendency of a couple to undergo reduction compared to hydrogen ions:

- **A negative $E^\circ$:** The redox couple has a lower reduction potential than $\ce{H+/H2}$. Its reduced form (the elemental metal, such as $\ce{Zn}$ or $\ce{Mg}$) loses electrons more readily than $\ce{H2}$, making the couple a **stronger reducing agent** than the $\ce{H+/H2}$ couple.
- **A positive $E^\circ$:** The redox couple has a higher reduction potential than $\ce{H+/H2}$. Its oxidised form (such as $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ or $\ce{Ag+}$) gains electrons more readily than $\ce{H+}$, making the couple a **weaker reducing agent** (and its oxidised form a stronger oxidising agent) than the $\ce{H+/H2}$ couple.

In Table 7.1, electrode processes are arranged in descending order of standard reduction potential:
- At the very top stands fluorine: $\ce{F2(g) + 2e- -> 2F-}$, with $E^\circ = +2.87\ \text{V}$. Fluorine gas has the greatest tendency to accept electrons, making $\ce{F2}$ the strongest oxidising agent in the table.
- At the very bottom stands lithium: $\ce{Li+ + e- -> Li(s)}$, with $E^\circ = -3.05\ \text{V}$. Lithium ions have the lowest tendency to be reduced, which conversely makes lithium metal ($\ce{Li}$) the most powerful reducing agent in aqueous solution.

### Predicting cell EMF and spontaneity

When any two redox couples are coupled together to form a galvanic cell:
1. The couple with the **higher (more positive) $E^\circ$** has the stronger electron-attracting power; it undergoes reduction and serves as the **cathode** (the positive pole in a galvanic cell).
2. The couple with the **lower (more negative) $E^\circ$** releases electrons; it undergoes oxidation and serves as the **anode** (the negative pole in a galvanic cell).

The standard electromotive force of the cell ($E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$) is always computed as:

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ(\text{cathode}) - E^\circ(\text{anode})
$$

For the Daniell cell:

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ(\ce{Cu^{2+}/Cu}) - E^\circ(\ce{Zn^{2+}/Zn}) = +0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.76\ \text{V}) = +1.10\ \text{V}
$$

Because $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$, the cell reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions. (The quantitative relationship between cell potential and standard Gibbs energy change, $\Delta G^\circ = -n F E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$, is developed in detail in Class 12 Electrochemistry.)

<KeyIdeas>
- In direct electron transfer (such as zinc dipped in aqueous copper sulphate), electrons hop directly at the metal surface and the chemical free energy is degraded into heat ($q < 0$) with zero electrical work.
- In a galvanic cell (such as the Daniell cell), the oxidation and reduction half-reactions are physically segregated into separate half-cells connected by an external metallic wire and an internal salt bridge.
- A redox couple comprises the oxidised and reduced forms of a substance ($\text{Ox}/\text{Red}$, e.g. $\ce{Zn^{2+}/Zn}$). The salt bridge (agar gel with $\ce{KCl}$ or $\ce{NH4NO3}$) maintains electrical neutrality by ion migration without allowing bulk mixing.
- Standard electrode potentials ($E^\circ$) are measured against the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE, $E^\circ = 0.00\ \text{V}$) at $1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ solute concentration, $1\ \text{bar}$ gas pressure, and $298\ \text{K}$.
- Table 7.1 tabulates standard reduction potentials: couples with higher $E^\circ$ act as cathodes; couples with lower $E^\circ$ act as anodes. $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ(\text{cathode}) - E^\circ(\text{anode})$. Spontaneous cells have $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$.
</KeyIdeas>
