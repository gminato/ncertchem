## 7.3.1 Electron shifts in covalent bonds

When sodium burns in chlorine gas, electron transfer is unequivocal: each neutral sodium atom completely surrenders an electron to form $\ce{Na+}$, and each chlorine atom captures an electron to become $\ce{Cl-}$. The resulting lattice consists of distinct, physically separated ions held together by electrostatic attraction.

Now consider the formation of water from its elemental gases:

$$
2\ce{H2(g)} + \ce{O2(g)} \rightarrow 2\ce{H2O(l)}
$$

Hydrogen and oxygen are non-metals. In the water molecule, oxygen shares pairs of electrons with hydrogen rather than stripping them away completely. Yet oxygen possesses a substantially higher electronegativity ($3.44$ on the Pauling scale) than hydrogen ($2.20$). As a result, the shared electron pairs spend the majority of their time drawn tightly towards the oxygen nucleus. The displacement is not a complete transfer; it is a **partial electron shift**. Oxygen acquires a partial negative charge ($2\delta-$), while each hydrogen atom acquires a partial positive charge ($\delta+$).

Similar partial shifts occur when hydrogen combines with chlorine:

$$
\ce{H2(g)} + \ce{Cl2(g)} \rightarrow 2\ce{HCl(g)}
$$

and when methane undergoes free-radical chlorination to carbon tetrachloride:

$$
\ce{CH4(g)} + 4\ce{Cl2(g)} \rightarrow \ce{CCl4(l)} + 4\ce{HCl(g)}
$$

In methane, carbon is slightly more electronegative than hydrogen ($2.55$ versus $2.20$), so carbon draws bonding electrons towards itself. When chlorination substitutes the hydrogens with chlorine atoms ($3.16$), chlorine is more electronegative than carbon, pulling electron density in the opposite direction.

To keep track of these varied shifts across millions of chemical reactions without having to compute exact quantum mechanical charge densities each time, chemists adopted a universal convention: the **oxidation number**.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The oxidation number is a formal book-keeping device. It is formulated on the simplifying assumption that the shared electron pair in any covalent bond belongs entirely to the more electronegative atom. It does not represent a real physical charge, except in the case of simple monatomic ions.

## 7.3.2 Rules for determining oxidation numbers

Because electronegativity differences across complex molecules and polyatomic ions are not always obvious, IUPAC and NCERT establish a hierarchy of rules evaluated in strict priority order:

1. **Free or uncombined elements:** In the elemental state, each atom has an oxidation number of **zero**, regardless of allotropic form or molecular atomicity. Thus, each atom in $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{O3}$, $\ce{P4}$, $\ce{S8}$, $\ce{Na(s)}$, $\ce{Mg(s)}$, and $\ce{Al(s)}$ has an oxidation number of $0$.
2. **Monatomic ions and fixed-valence metals:** For ions composed of a single atom, the oxidation number strictly equals the electrical charge on the ion. For example, $\ce{Na+}$ is $+1$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ is $+2$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ is $+3$, $\ce{Cl-}$ is $-1$, and $\ce{O^2-}$ is $-2$. In all their chemical compounds, alkali metals (Group 1: $\ce{Li}$, $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{K}$, $\ce{Rb}$, $\ce{Cs}$) are always $+1$, alkaline earth metals (Group 2: $\ce{Be}$, $\ce{Mg}$, $\ce{Ca}$, $\ce{Sr}$, $\ce{Ba}$) are always $+2$, and aluminium is always $+3$.
3. **Oxygen:** In most chemical compounds, oxygen has an oxidation number of **$-2$**. Two categories of exceptions arise:
   - *Peroxides and superoxides:* In peroxides (such as $\ce{H2O2}$, $\ce{Na2O2}$, and $\ce{BaO2}$), oxygen atoms are bonded directly to each other in an $[\ce{O-O}]^{2-}$ unit, and each oxygen has an oxidation number of **$-1$**. In superoxides (such as $\ce{KO2}$ and $\ce{RbO2}$), each oxygen has an average oxidation number of **$-\frac{1}{2}$**.
   - *Oxygen-fluorine compounds:* Because fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen, oxygen assumes a positive oxidation number. In oxygen difluoride ($\ce{OF2}$), oxygen is **$+2$**; in dioxygen difluoride ($\ce{O2F2}$), oxygen is **$+1$**.
4. **Hydrogen:** Hydrogen has an oxidation number of **$+1$** when bonded to non-metals. However, when bonded to reactive metals in binary metal hydrides (such as $\ce{LiH}$, $\ce{NaH}$, and $\ce{CaH2}$), hydrogen acts as the hydride anion $\ce{H-}$ and has an oxidation number of **$-1$**.
5. **Halogens:** Fluorine is the most electronegative element in the periodic table and has an oxidation number of **$-1$ in all its compounds**. Other halogens ($\ce{Cl}$, $\ce{Br}$, and $\ce{I}$) have an oxidation number of $-1$ when present as halide ions. When combined with more electronegative elements (namely oxygen or fluorine) in oxoacids, oxoanions, and interhalogens, chlorine, bromine, and iodine display positive oxidation states up to $+7$.
6. **The algebraic sum rule:** In a neutral compound, the algebraic sum of the oxidation numbers of all constituent atoms must equal **zero**. In a polyatomic ion, the algebraic sum must equal the **net electrical charge** of the ion.

Start with potassium dichromate, $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$. Because this is a neutral crystalline compound with no net ionic charge, the algebraic sum of all oxidation numbers across every atom must equal zero. Our objective is to determine the unknown oxidation state of chromium, which possesses variable valence as a transition metal. We write down the charge framework before consulting any individual element rules.

<Step>
Consult the element priority rules. Rule 2 instructs that Group 1 alkali metals always exhibit an oxidation number of $+1$ in all their chemical compounds. Potassium cannot lose more than its single valence electron without disrupting a stable noble-gas octet core. Because two potassium atoms are present in the formula unit, potassium contributes a combined charge of $+2$ to our book-keeping ledger.
</Step>
<Step>
Next examine oxygen using Rule 3. Oxygen displays an oxidation number of $-2$ in nearly all oxides and oxoanions, provided it is not directly linked to another oxygen atom in a peroxo group or bonded to more electronegative fluorine. Potassium dichromate contains seven oxide oxygens, yielding a total negative contribution of $7 \times (-2) = -14$ in the sum rule ledger.
</Step>
<Step>
Now apply Rule 6, the algebraic sum rule. The sum of all oxidation numbers must equal the net charge of zero: $2(+1) + 2x + 7(-2) = 0$. Simplifying gives $+2 + 2x - 14 = 0$, so $2x = +12$, which yields $x = +6$. Each chromium atom sits in the $+6$ oxidation state, matching its group maximum valence for Group 6.
</Step>
<Step>
Now examine barium peroxide, $\ce{BaO2}$. A hasty student might instinctively assign oxygen its usual $-2$ oxidation number, which would mathematically force barium into a $+4$ state. But barium is an alkaline earth metal in Group 2, possessing only two valence electrons. Rule 2 takes strict priority over Rule 3: barium must be $+2$. Never violate a fixed metal valence.
</Step>
<Step>
With barium rigidly fixed at $+2$, we apply the algebraic sum rule to solve for oxygen: $(+2) + 2x = 0$, giving $2x = -2$ and therefore $x = -1$. An oxidation number of $-1$ is the unmistakable chemical hallmark of a peroxide containing the $[\ce{O-O}]^{2-}$ link. The rules prevent false assignments and expose true chemical structure.
</Step>

## 7.3.3 Fractional oxidation numbers and structural sites

When the sum rule is applied to species such as carbon suboxide ($\ce{C3O2}$), tribromooctaoxide ($\ce{Br3O8}$), the tetrathionate ion ($\ce{S4O6^2-}$), or magnetic iron oxide ($\ce{Fe3O4}$), the resulting value is a fraction:

- In $\ce{C3O2}$, three carbons share a total oxidation state of $+4$, yielding an average of $+\frac{4}{3}$.
- In $\ce{Br3O8}$, three bromines share a total of $+16$, giving an average of $+\frac{16}{3}$.
- In $\ce{S4O6^2-}$, four sulphurs share a charge of $+10$, giving an average of $+\frac{5}{2} = +2.5$.
- In $\ce{Fe3O4}$, three irons share a total of $+8$, yielding an average of $+\frac{8}{3}$.

Electrons cannot be transferred in fractions. A fractional oxidation number is an **arithmetic average** across distinct atoms of the same element sitting in non-equivalent chemical environments:

1. **Carbon suboxide ($\ce{C3O2}$):** The linear structure is $\ce{O=C=C*=C=O}$. The two terminal carbon atoms are each double-bonded to oxygen (an electronegative partner), so each terminal carbon has an oxidation number of $+2$. The central carbon atom ($\ce{C*}$) is bonded exclusively to carbon atoms of identical electronegativity, so its oxidation number is $0$. The average is $(+2 + 0 + +2)/3 = +\frac{4}{3}$.
2. **Tetrathionate ion ($\ce{S4O6^2-}$):** The ion contains a chain of four sulphur atoms, $[\ce{O3S-S-S-SO3}]^{2-}$. The two terminal sulphur atoms are bonded to three oxygen atoms each, carrying an oxidation number of $+5$. The two central sulphur atoms form a homonuclear $\ce{-S-S-}$ bridge and possess an oxidation number of $0$. The formula average is $(+5 + 0 + 0 + +5)/4 = +\frac{5}{2}$.
3. **Mixed valence oxides ($\ce{Fe3O4}$):** Magnetite is a stoichiometric mixed oxide composed of one formula unit of iron(II) oxide and one formula unit of iron(III) oxide: $\ce{FeO} \cdot \ce{Fe2O3}$. One iron atom sits as $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ($+2$), and two iron atoms sit as $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ($+3$). The average is $(+2 + 2 \times +3)/3 = +\frac{8}{3}$.
4. **Site-specific salts ($\ce{NH4NO3}$):** In ammonium nitrate, assigning a single number to nitrogen produces an unhelpful average of $+1$. In reality, the crystal contains two distinct polyatomic ions: in the ammonium cation ($\ce{NH4+}$), nitrogen has an oxidation state of **$-3$**, whereas in the nitrate anion ($\ce{NO3-}$), nitrogen has an oxidation state of **$+5$**.

## 7.3.4 Periodic trends in highest oxidation states across Period 3

A representative element's highest possible oxidation state is constrained by its valence shell electronic configuration. In the long form of the periodic table, the maximum group oxidation number equals:

- The **group number** for Groups 1 and 2.
- The **group number minus 10** for Groups 13 through 17.

Across Period 3 ($\ce{Na}$ to $\ce{Cl}$), the maximum oxidation state increases progressively from $+1$ to $+7$:

| Group | Element | Valence shell | Highest oxidation compound | Highest oxidation number |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | $\ce{Na}$ | $3s^1$ | $\ce{NaCl}$ | $+1$ |
| 2 | $\ce{Mg}$ | $3s^2$ | $\ce{MgSO4}$ | $+2$ |
| 13 | $\ce{Al}$ | $3s^2\,3p^1$ | $\ce{AlF3}$ | $+3$ |
| 14 | $\ce{Si}$ | $3s^2\,3p^2$ | $\ce{SiCl4}$ | $+4$ |
| 15 | $\ce{P}$ | $3s^2\,3p^3$ | $\ce{P4O10}$ | $+5$ |
| 16 | $\ce{S}$ | $3s^2\,3p^4$ | $\ce{SF6}$ | $+6$ |
| 17 | $\ce{Cl}$ | $3s^2\,3p^5$ | $\ce{HClO4}$ | $+7$ |

> [!NOTE]
> In the NCERT textbook table for Period 3 highest oxidation numbers, the compound for chlorine's $+7$ state is misprinted as $\ce{HClO}$. In hypochlorous acid ($\ce{HClO}$), chlorine has an oxidation number of $+1$. The oxoacid exhibiting chlorine's true group-maximum state of $+7$ is perchloric acid, $\ce{HClO4}$.

## 7.3.5 Stock notation

Transition metals and post-transition metals frequently exhibit multiple positive oxidation states due to the participation of $(n-1)d$ electrons in chemical bonding. To eliminate ambiguity between different valence states, German chemist Alfred Stock introduced **Stock notation**.

In Stock notation, the oxidation state of the metal is designated by a Roman numeral in parentheses placed immediately after the metal's symbol and its subscript in the chemical formula:

- Auric chloride is $\ce{Au(III)Cl3}$, while aurous chloride is $\ce{Au(I)Cl}$.
- Stannic chloride is $\ce{Sn(IV)Cl4}$, and stannous chloride is $\ce{Sn(II)Cl2}$.
- Ferric oxide is $\ce{Fe2(III)O3}$, and ferrous oxide is $\ce{Fe(II)O}$.
- Cuprous oxide is $\ce{Cu2(I)O}$, and cupric oxide is $\ce{Cu(II)O}$.
- Chloroauric acid is $\ce{HAu(III)Cl4}$.
- Manganese dioxide is $\ce{Mn(IV)O2}$, and manganous oxide is $\ce{Mn(II)O}$.

Stock notation directly reveals whether a compound represents an oxidised or reduced form of the metal. For example, mercury(I) chloride, $\ce{Hg2(I)Cl2}$, is the reduced form of mercury(II) chloride, $\ce{Hg(II)Cl2}$.

## 7.3.6 Modern definitions in terms of oxidation numbers

Equipped with the concept of oxidation number, we can define all fundamental redox terms with mathematical precision:

1. **Oxidation:** An **increase** in the oxidation number of an element in a given substance (corresponding to a loss of electron density).
2. **Reduction:** A **decrease** in the oxidation number of an element in a given substance (corresponding to a gain of electron density).
3. **Oxidising agent (oxidant):** A reagent that **increases** the oxidation number of an element in another substance. Because it extracts electron density from another reactant, the oxidant is itself **reduced**.
4. **Reducing agent (reductant):** A reagent that **lowers** the oxidation number of an element in another substance. By donating electron density, the reductant is itself **oxidised**.
5. **Redox reaction:** Any chemical process in which interacting species undergo changes in oxidation number.

### Worked example: The thermite reduction

Consider the vigorous thermite displacement reaction used to weld railway tracks:

$$
\ce{Fe2O3(s)} + 2\ce{Al(s)} \rightarrow 2\ce{Fe(l)} + \ce{Al2O3(s)}
$$

Let us assign oxidation numbers to every atom:

- In $\ce{Fe2O3}$: Oxygen is $-2$ (Rule 3), so $2x + 3(-2) = 0 \implies x = +3$ for iron.
- In elemental aluminium $\ce{Al(s)}$: Oxidation number is $0$ (Rule 1).
- In molten iron $\ce{Fe(l)}$: Oxidation number is $0$ (Rule 1).
- In aluminium oxide $\ce{Al2O3}$: Aluminium is $+3$ (Rule 2) and oxygen is $-2$ (Rule 3).

Iron changes from $+3$ to $0$ (a decrease of $3$ units per atom): iron is **reduced**. Aluminium changes from $0$ to $+3$ (an increase of $3$ units per atom): aluminium is **oxidised**. Oxygen remains unchanged at $-2$. Therefore:
- The substance oxidised is $\ce{Al}$; it acts as the **reductant**.
- The substance reduced is $\ce{Fe2O3}$; it acts as the **oxidant**.

<KeyIdeas>
- The oxidation number is an electron-accounting book-keeping convention where bonding electrons in every covalent bond are allocated wholly to the more electronegative partner; it is not a physical ionic charge.
- Priority rules dictate assignments in order: free elements ($0$) $\rightarrow$ monatomic ions (charge) $\rightarrow$ Group 1 ($+1$), Group 2 ($+2$), $\ce{Al}$ ($+3$) $\rightarrow$ fluorine (always $-1$) $\rightarrow$ hydrogen ($+1$, but $-1$ in metal hydrides) $\rightarrow$ oxygen ($-2$, but $-1$ in peroxides, $-\frac{1}{2}$ in superoxides, $+2$ in $\ce{OF2}$) $\rightarrow$ algebraic sum rule ($0$ for neutral molecules, net charge for ions).
- Fractional oxidation numbers are mathematical averages across distinct atoms of an element in non-equivalent chemical environments, as in $\ce{C3O2}$ ($+\frac{4}{3}$), $\ce{Br3O8}$ ($+\frac{16}{3}$), $\ce{S4O6^2-}$ ($+\frac{5}{2}$), and $\ce{Fe3O4}$ ($+\frac{8}{3}$).
- In Period 3, the highest oxidation state increases from $+1$ ($\ce{NaCl}$) to $+7$ ($\ce{HClO4}$); NCERT misprints this $+7$ oxoacid as $\ce{HClO}$.
- Oxidation is an increase in oxidation number (electron loss); reduction is a decrease (electron gain). The oxidant is itself reduced, and the reductant is itself oxidised.
</KeyIdeas>
