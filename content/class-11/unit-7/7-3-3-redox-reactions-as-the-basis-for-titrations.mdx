## The principle of redox titrations

In acid–base chemistry, you determine the unknown concentration of an acid by neutralizing it with a standard base, using a pH-sensitive dye such as phenolphthalein to mark the neutralization point. Redox titrations follow the exact same stoichiometric philosophy, but instead of transferring protons between an acid and a base, the reacting species transfer electrons between a reductant and an oxidant.

A solution of known concentration, called the **titrant**, is placed in a graduated burette and delivered drop by drop into a measured volume of the unknown solution, called the **analyte**, held in an Erlenmeyer flask. To determine the unknown strength reliably, three chemical criteria must be met:

1. The redox reaction between titrant and analyte must be rapid, quantitative, and proceed with a well-defined stoichiometric equation.
2. No side reactions must interfere with the primary electron transfer.
3. A clear, sharp physical signal—usually a dramatic colour change—must mark the precise moment when the reactants have consumed each other completely.

## Equivalence point versus end point

Before evaluating specific titration systems, you must distinguish between two terms that are frequently confused:

- **Equivalence point (stoichiometric point)**: The theoretical point in the titration where the number of equivalents of oxidant added matches the number of equivalents of reductant originally present, strictly according to the balanced redox equation.
- **End point**: The practical, experimental observation (such as the appearance or disappearance of a colour) that signals to the experimenter that the titration is complete.

In an ideal titration, the end point coincides exactly with the equivalence point. In real experiments, a minute extra amount of titrant—often less than half of a single drop ($< 0.03\ \text{mL}$)—is required to produce the perceptible optical change. Selecting an indicator that responds at exceptionally low concentrations ensures that this titration error, or **overshoot**, remains negligible.

## Case 1: Self-indicating titrations (potassium permanganate)

The simplest and most elegant situation arises when the titrant itself is intensely coloured. Potassium permanganate ($\ce{KMnO4}$) is the foremost example. The permanganate ion ($\ce{MnO4-}$) possesses a vivid deep purple colour due to intense charge-transfer absorption. 

When acidified permanganate solution is added to a reducing agent such as iron(II) ions ($\ce{Fe^{2+}}$) or oxalate ions ($\ce{C2O4^{2-}}$), $\ce{MnO4-}$ is reduced to the manganese(II) ion ($\ce{Mn^{2+}}$):

$$
\ce{MnO4- + 5Fe^{2+} + 8H+ -> Mn^{2+} + 5Fe^{3+} + 4H2O}
$$

While $\ce{MnO4-}$ is deeply violet, $\ce{Mn^{2+}}$ is practically colourless in dilute aqueous solutions (it has a barely perceptible pale pink hue only visible at high concentrations). As long as unreacted $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ remains in the conical flask, every drop of $\ce{MnO4-}$ that enters is reduced immediately upon swirling, and the liquid in the flask remains colourless.

The moment the very last trace of $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ is consumed, the next drop of $\ce{MnO4-}$ finds no reductant to react with. It remains in the solution as unreduced $\ce{MnO4-}$. Because the human eye can perceive the faint pink colour of $\ce{MnO4-}$ at concentrations as low as $10^{-6}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ ($10^{-6}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$), that single excess drop imparts a permanent, pale pink tinge across the flask. Thus, $\ce{KMnO4}$ acts as its own indicator, termed a **self-indicator**, guaranteeing minimal overshoot beyond the true equivalence point.

## Case 2: Internal redox indicators (potassium dichromate)

Unlike potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate ($\ce{K2Cr2O7}$) is an outstanding primary chemical standard: it can be obtained in high purity, dried at $150^\circ\text{C}$ without decomposing, weighed directly on an analytical balance, and stored indefinitely without degradation.

However, dichromate cannot serve as a self-indicator. The reactant dichromate ion ($\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}$) is bright orange, and its reduction product, chromium(III) ($\ce{Cr^{3+}}$), is deep emerald green:

$$
\ce{Cr2O7^{2-} + 6Fe^{2+} + 14H+ -> 2Cr^{3+} + 6Fe^{3+} + 7H2O}
$$

As titrant is added, the solution steadily accumulates green $\ce{Cr^{3+}}$ ions. Discerning the first faint excess of orange $\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}$ against a background of dense green $\ce{Cr^{3+}}$ is optically impossible. 

To solve this, an internal redox indicator such as **diphenylamine** (or sodium diphenylamine sulphonate) is added to the flask. Diphenylamine is colourless in its reduced state. It has an oxidation potential such that it is not oxidised by $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$, nor by $\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}$ as long as unreacted $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ remains in solution. 

The moment the last trace of $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ has been oxidised, the first minute fraction of excess $\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}$ oxidises diphenylamine to its quinoid oxidation product, producing an intense violet-blue colour that stands out sharply against the green chromium solution. Phosphoric acid ($\ce{H3PO4}$) is added to complex the $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$ ions (forming colourless $[\ce{Fe(HPO4)}]^+$), which both lowers the formal potential of the $\ce{Fe^{3+}}/\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ couple and removes yellow $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$ colour interference.

## Case 3: Iodometric titrations (indirect analysis)

A third widely used class of redox titrations is **iodometry**, which operates indirectly by exploiting species capable of oxidising iodide ions ($\ce{I-}$) to molecular iodine ($\ce{I2}$). A classic example is the estimation of copper(II) salts:

When excess potassium iodide ($\ce{KI}$) is added to an aqueous solution containing copper(II) ions ($\ce{Cu^{2+}}$), $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ is reduced to copper(I), precipitating white insoluble cuprous iodide ($\ce{Cu2I2}$), while iodide is quantitatively oxidised to iodine:

$$
\ce{2Cu^{2+}(aq) + 4I-(aq) -> Cu2I2(s) + I2(aq)}
$$

Although elemental iodine is only sparingly soluble in pure water, it readily dissolves in the presence of excess iodide by forming the soluble triiodide complex, $\ce{I3-}$ (often written as $\ce{KI3}$):

$$
\ce{I2(aq) + I-(aq) <=> I3-(aq)}
$$

The liberated iodine is subsequently titrated against a standardized solution of sodium thiosulphate ($\ce{Na2S2O3}$), commonly called "hypo":

$$
\ce{I2(aq) + 2S2O3^{2-}(aq) -> 2I-(aq) + S4O6^{2-}(aq)}
$$

In this reaction, iodine is reduced back to colourless iodide ($\ce{I-}$), while thiosulphate ($\ce{S2O3^{2-}}$, average sulfur oxidation state $+2$) is oxidised to tetrathionate ($\ce{S4O6^{2-}}$, average sulfur oxidation state $+2.5$). 

To track this end point, **freshly prepared starch solution** is employed as the indicator. Starch forms an intensely deep blue-black inclusion complex with polyiodide ions. Starch is added towards the end of the titration when the dark brown iodine solution has faded to a pale straw yellow (adding starch too early traps iodine permanently inside the helical amylose chains). As the final drops of thiosulphate reduce the remaining traces of iodine, the deep blue starch colour discharges abruptly to a clear solution or off-white suspension of $\ce{Cu2I2}$.

Combining the two balanced equations reveals the net stoichiometric ratio:

- $2\ \text{mol}\ \ce{Cu^{2+}}$ produce $1\ \text{mol}\ \ce{I2}$.
- $1\ \text{mol}\ \ce{I2}$ reacts with $2\ \text{mol}\ \ce{S2O3^{2-}}$.
- Therefore, $1\ \text{mol}\ \ce{Cu^{2+}} \equiv 1\ \text{mol}\ \ce{S2O3^{2-}}$.

## Limitations of the oxidation number concept (Section 7.3.4)

Throughout this unit, the definition of redox processes has evolved across three successive stages:

1. **Classical view**: Oxidation as the gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen; reduction as the loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
2. **Electrochemical view**: Oxidation as the loss of electrons; reduction as the gain of electrons.
3. **Oxidation number convention**: Oxidation as an algebraic increase in oxidation state; reduction as an algebraic decrease.

While formal oxidation numbers provide a reliable book-keeping system for balancing complex reactions, the concept has an inherent limitation: **it treats all chemical bonds as 100% ionic**. 

When assigning an oxidation number, the shared bonding electron pair is awarded entirely to the more electronegative atom, regardless of whether the bond is truly ionic or covalent. For example, in methane ($\ce{CH4}$), carbon is assigned an oxidation state of $-4$ because carbon is slightly more electronegative than hydrogen ($2.55$ versus $2.20$). However, carbon does not physically hold four full unit negative charges ($\ce{C^4-}$); the $\ce{C-H}$ bonds are covalent with only a modest dipole moment.

Modern physical chemistry therefore visualises redox processes through the **electron-density view**:

- **Oxidation** is visualised as a **decrease in electron density** around the atom or group of atoms involved in the reaction.
- **Reduction** is visualised as an **increase in electron density** around the atom or group of atoms involved.

When methane burns to form carbon dioxide:

$$
\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}
$$

carbon's formal oxidation state changes from $-4$ to $+4$. In physical terms, the electron cloud that was formerly shared relatively equally between carbon and hydrogen is now drawn strongly towards the highly electronegative oxygen atoms in $\ce{CO2}$. Carbon has experienced a significant loss of valence electron density: it has been oxidised. The oxidation number formalises this continuous redistribution of electronic charge into whole-number book-keeping.

<KeyIdeas>
- A redox titration determines the unknown concentration of an oxidant or reductant by delivering a standard titrant until stoichiometric electron transfer is complete.
- Potassium permanganate ($\ce{KMnO4}$) acts as a self-indicator in acidic solutions; unreduced $\ce{MnO4-}$ produces a permanent faint pink end point at concentrations as low as $10^{-6}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$.
- Potassium dichromate ($\ce{K2Cr2O7}$) is a reliable primary standard but requires an internal indicator such as diphenylamine, which turns intense violet-blue immediately after equivalence.
- Iodometry indirectly measures oxidants like $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ by liberating $\ce{I2}$ with excess $\ce{KI}$, which is titrated against standard thiosulphate ($\ce{S2O3^{2-}}$) with a starch indicator (1 : 1 net $\ce{Cu^{2+}} : \ce{S2O3^{2-}}$ ratio).
- Formal oxidation numbers treat every bond as 100% ionic; modern chemical theory visualises oxidation and reduction as continuous decreases and increases in electron density around reacting atoms.
</KeyIdeas>
