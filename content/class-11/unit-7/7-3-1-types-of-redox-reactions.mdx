## Classifying electron-transfer reactions

Every redox process involves the simultaneous loss and gain of electrons. Because oxidation numbers assign electrons by strict electronegativity book-keeping, tracking how these numbers shift allows you to categorise chemical reactions systematically. Section 7.3.1 groups redox reactions into four fundamental types: **combination**, **decomposition**, **displacement**, and **disproportionation**.

Understanding these categories also resolves a long-standing puzzle: why algebraic rules occasionally assign an element a fractional oxidation state such as $+2.5$ or $+\frac{4}{3}$.

## 1. Combination reactions

A combination reaction unites two or more reactants into a single chemical compound:

$$
A + B \longrightarrow C
$$

For a combination reaction to qualify as a redox process, **at least one of the reacting substances must be in the elemental state** (where its oxidation number is zero). When elements combine, electron transfer inevitably changes their oxidation numbers from zero to non-zero values.

All combustion reactions that consume elemental dioxygen ($\ce{O2}$) are combination redox reactions. When carbon in coal burns completely, carbon and oxygen begin with oxidation state $0$ and form carbon dioxide:

$$
\ce{\overset{0}{C}(s) + \overset{0}{O2}(g) -> \overset{+4}{-2}{CO2}(g)}
$$

Similarly, magnesium burns with a brilliant white flame in air to form magnesium oxide:

$$
\ce{2\overset{0}{Mg}(s) + \overset{0}{O2}(g) -> 2\overset{+2}{-2}{MgO}(s)}
$$

Here, magnesium is oxidised from $0$ to $+2$ and acts as the reductant (reducing agent). Oxygen is reduced from $0$ to $-2$ and acts as the oxidant (oxidising agent). Elemental combinations extend beyond oxygen: heating magnesium with nitrogen gas forms magnesium nitride:

$$
\ce{3\overset{0}{Mg}(s) + \overset{0}{N2}(g) -> \overset{+2}{-3}{Mg3N2}(s)}
$$

Combustion of hydrocarbons, such as methane in kitchen burners, is also grouped here:

$$
\ce{\overset{-4}{C}\overset{+1}{H4}(g) + 2\overset{0}{O2}(g) -> \overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O2}(g) + 2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l)}
$$

Notice that the oxidation state of hydrogen remains $+1$ on both sides. Carbon is oxidised from $-4$ to $+4$, while oxygen is reduced from $0$ to $-2$.

## 2. Decomposition reactions

Decomposition reactions represent the exact reverse of combination: a single compound splits into two or more simpler components:

$$
C \longrightarrow A + B
$$

Just as in combination, a decomposition reaction is a redox reaction only if **at least one of the products is formed in the elemental state**. 

Consider heating potassium chlorate in the presence of manganese dioxide catalyst:

$$
\ce{2\overset{+1}{K}\overset{+5}{Cl}\overset{-2}{O3}(s) -> 2\overset{+1}{K}\overset{-1}{Cl}(s) + 3\overset{0}{O2}(g)}
$$

Chlorine is reduced from $+5$ to $-1$ (gaining electrons), while oxygen is oxidised from $-2$ to $0$ in elemental dioxygen (losing electrons). Potassium retains its $+1$ oxidation state throughout. Similarly, the electrolysis of water decomposes liquid water into its constituent gases:

$$
\ce{2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l) -> 2\overset{0}{H2}(g) + \overset{0}{O2}(g)}
$$

and thermal heating of sodium hydride yields elemental sodium and dihydrogen:

$$
\ce{2\overset{+1}{Na}\overset{-1}{H}(s) -> 2\overset{0}{Na}(s) + \overset{0}{H2}(g)}
$$

### Not all decompositions are redox

It is essential to recognise that **not every decomposition reaction is a redox reaction**. When limestone (calcium carbonate) is roasted in a kiln to produce quicklime:

$$
\ce{\overset{+2}{Ca}\overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O3}(s) -> \overset{+2}{Ca}\overset{-2}{O}(s) + \overset{+4}{C}\overset{-2}{O2}(g)}
$$

Check the oxidation numbers: calcium is $+2$ on both sides, carbon is $+4$ on both sides, and oxygen is $-2$ on both sides. Because no element changes its oxidation state, no electrons are transferred. This is an acid-base thermal cleavage, not a redox process.

## 3. Displacement reactions

In a displacement reaction, an atom or ion in a compound is replaced by an atom or ion of another element:

$$
X + YZ \longrightarrow XZ + Y
$$

Displacement reactions divide naturally into **metal displacement** and **non-metal displacement**.

### (a) Metal displacement

In metal displacement, an uncombined metal replaces another metal from its dissolved salt or molten ore:

$$
\ce{\overset{0}{Zn}(s) + \overset{+2}{Cu}\overset{+6}{S}\overset{-2}{O4}(aq) -> \overset{+2}{Zn}\overset{+6}{S}\overset{-2}{O4}(aq) + \overset{0}{Cu}(s)}
$$

Zinc surrenders two electrons per atom to become $\ce{Zn^2+}$, climbing from $0$ to $+2$ (oxidation). Copper(II) ions receive those electrons, dropping from $+2$ to $0$ (reduction). Zinc can displace copper because zinc has a greater electron-releasing tendency, reflected in its more negative standard reduction potential ($E^\circ_{\ce{Zn^2+/Zn}} = -0.76\ \text{V}$ versus $E^\circ_{\ce{Cu^2+/Cu}} = +0.34\ \text{V}$).

Metal displacement reactions are indispensable in metallurgy for extracting pure metals from their oxides and halides:

- **Titanium extraction (Kroll process):** Magnesium reduces titanium tetrachloride vapour at high temperature:
  $$
  \ce{\overset{+4}{Ti}\overset{-1}{Cl4}(l) + 2\overset{0}{Mg}(s) -> \overset{0}{Ti}(s) + 2\overset{+2}{Mg}\overset{-1}{Cl2}(s)}
  $$
- **Vanadium extraction:** Metallic calcium reduces vanadium(V) oxide:
  $$
  \ce{\overset{+5}{V2}\overset{-2}{O5}(s) + 5\overset{0}{Ca}(s) -> 2\overset{0}{V}(s) + 5\overset{+2}{Ca}\overset{-2}{O}(s)}
  $$
- **Chromium thermite reduction:** Aluminium powder reduces chromium(III) oxide:
  $$
  \ce{\overset{+3}{Cr2}\overset{-2}{O3}(s) + 2\overset{0}{Al}(s) -> \overset{+3}{Al2}\overset{-2}{O3}(s) + 2\overset{0}{Cr}(s)}
  $$

In every case, the reducing metal is a more powerful reducing agent than the metal being liberated.

### (b) Non-metal displacement

Non-metal displacement reactions predominantly involve the displacement of hydrogen, or the displacement of one halogen by another.

#### Hydrogen displacement from water and acids

The ease with which a metal displaces hydrogen mirrors the electrochemical activity series:

1. **Cold water:** Highly reactive alkali metals ($\ce{Na}$, $\ce{K}$) and alkaline earth metals ($\ce{Ca}$, $\ce{Sr}$, $\ce{Ba}$) are such potent reductants that they displace dihydrogen from cold liquid water with rapid vigour:
   $$
   \ce{2\overset{0}{Na}(s) + 2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l) -> 2\overset{+1}{Na}\overset{-2}{O}\overset{+1}{H}(aq) + \overset{0}{H2}(g)}
   $$
   $$
   \ce{\overset{0}{Ca}(s) + 2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l) -> \overset{+2}{Ca}(\overset{-2}{O}\overset{+1}{H})2(aq) + \overset{0}{H2}(g)}
   $$

2. **Steam:** Moderately active metals such as magnesium and iron do not react visibly with cold water, but displace hydrogen when exposed to steam ($\ce{H2O(g)}$) at elevated temperatures:
   $$
   \ce{\overset{0}{Mg}(s) + 2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(g) -> \overset{+2}{Mg}(\overset{-2}{O}\overset{+1}{H})2(s) + \overset{0}{H2}(g)}
   $$
   $$
   \ce{3\overset{0}{Fe}(s) + 4\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(g) -> \overset{+8/3}{Fe3}\overset{-2}{O4}(s) + 4\overset{0}{H2}(g)}
   $$
   *Note:* Iron with steam produces ferrosoferric oxide ($\ce{Fe3O4}$, magnetic iron oxide), not ferric oxide ($\ce{Fe2O3}$). Because steam is gaseous water, it is written as $\ce{H2O(g)}$.

3. **Dilute acids:** Metals less active than magnesium, including zinc, iron, cadmium, and tin, displace dihydrogen from hydrochloric acid:
   $$
   \ce{\overset{0}{Zn}(s) + 2\overset{+1}{H}\overset{-1}{Cl}(aq) -> \overset{+2}{Zn}\overset{-1}{Cl2}(aq) + \overset{0}{H2}(g)}
   $$
   $$
   \ce{\overset{0}{Fe}(s) + 2\overset{+1}{H}\overset{-1}{Cl}(aq) -> \overset{+2}{Fe}\overset{-1}{Cl2}(aq) + \overset{0}{H2}(g)}
   $$
   The rate of dihydrogen evolution reflects metal reactivity: fastest for $\ce{Mg}$, moderate for $\ce{Zn}$, and slow for $\ce{Fe}$. Unreactive native metals like silver ($\ce{Ag}$) and gold ($\ce{Au}$) do not displace hydrogen from acids.

#### Halogen displacement and the layer test

Among the halogens (Group 17), oxidising strength decreases down the column:

$$
\ce{F2} > \ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{I2}
$$

Fluorine is such a fierce oxidant that it attacks water directly, displacing oxygen instead of halogens:

$$
\ce{2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l) + 2\overset{0}{F2}(g) -> 4\overset{+1}{H}\overset{-1}{F}(aq) + \overset{0}{O2}(g)}
$$

Because fluorine decomposes aqueous solvents immediately, displacement reactions using $\ce{F2}$ cannot be conducted in water.

Chlorine, however, smoothly oxidises bromide and iodide ions in aqueous solution:

$$
\ce{\overset{0}{Cl2}(g) + 2\overset{-1}{Br-}(aq) -> 2\overset{-1}{Cl-}(aq) + \overset{0}{Br2}(l)}
$$

$$
\ce{\overset{0}{Cl2}(g) + 2\overset{-1}{I-}(aq) -> 2\overset{-1}{Cl-}(aq) + \overset{0}{I2}(s)}
$$

Similarly, bromine displaces iodide:

$$
\ce{\overset{0}{Br2}(l) + 2\overset{-1}{I-}(aq) -> 2\overset{-1}{Br-}(aq) + \overset{0}{I2}(s)}
$$

In the laboratory, this behaviour underpins the **layer test**. When chlorine water is shaken with an aqueous halide solution in the presence of an immiscible non-polar solvent like carbon tetrachloride ($\ce{CCl4}$) or chloroform ($\ce{CHCl3}$), the liberated elemental halogens dissolve preferentially into the denser organic bottom layer:
- Displaced bromine imparts an **orange or reddish-brown** colour to the organic layer.
- Displaced iodine imparts an **intense violet** colour to the organic layer.

Recovering elemental halogens requires oxidation ($\ce{2X- -> X2 + 2e-}$). While chlorine, bromine, and iodine can be obtained with chemical oxidants, fluoride cannot be oxidised chemically by any known reagent; $\ce{F2}$ can only be produced through electrolytic oxidation.

## 4. Disproportionation reactions

A disproportionation reaction is a specialised redox process where **an element in a single intermediate oxidation state is simultaneously oxidised and reduced**.

For a substance to undergo disproportionation, the reacting element must possess **at least three distinct oxidation states**. The element must start in an intermediate state, so that one portion of the atoms can step up to a higher state while another portion steps down to a lower state.

A quintessential example is the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide:

$$
\ce{2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-1}{O2}(aq) -> 2\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l) + \overset{0}{O2}(g)}
$$

Here, oxygen starts in the $-1$ oxidation state in the peroxide link. In water it is reduced to $-2$, while in dioxygen gas it is oxidised to $0$.

Non-metals such as phosphorus, sulphur, and chlorine readily undergo disproportionation in alkaline media:

- **White phosphorus in alkali:**
  $$
  \ce{\overset{0}{P4}(s) + 3\overset{-2}{O}\overset{+1}{H-}(aq) + 3\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l) -> \overset{-3}{P}\overset{+1}{H3}(g) + 3\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{+1}{P}\overset{-2}{O2-}(aq)}
  $$
  Phosphorus ($0$) disproportionates into phosphine ($\text{P}$ at $-3$, reduced) and hypophosphite ion ($\text{P}$ at $+1$, oxidised).

- **Sulphur in hot alkali:**
  $$
  \ce{\overset{0}{S8}(s) + 12\overset{-2}{O}\overset{+1}{H-}(aq) -> 4\overset{-2}{S^2-}(aq) + 2\overset{+2}{S2}\overset{-2}{O3^2-}(aq) + 6\overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l)}
  $$
  Sulphur ($0$) disproportionates into sulphide ($\text{S}$ at $-2$) and thiosulphate ($\text{S}$ at $+2$).

- **Chlorine in cold alkali (household bleach):**
  $$
  \ce{\overset{0}{Cl2}(g) + 2\overset{-2}{O}\overset{+1}{H-}(aq) -> \overset{+1}{Cl}\overset{-2}{O-}(aq) + \overset{-1}{Cl-}(aq) + \overset{+1}{H2}\overset{-2}{O}(l)}
  $$
  Chlorine ($0$) simultaneously oxidises to hypochlorite ($\ce{ClO-}$, $\text{Cl}$ at $+1$) and reduces to chloride ($\ce{Cl-}$, $\text{Cl}$ at $-1$). The hypochlorite ion acts as an oxidant that destroys coloured stain-bearing chromophores on fabrics.

### Which oxoanions can disproportionate?

Consider the chlorine oxoanions: $\ce{ClO-}$ ($+1$), $\ce{ClO2-}$ ($+3$), $\ce{ClO3-}$ ($+5$), and $\ce{ClO4-}$ ($+7$).

The first three oxoanions readily undergo disproportionation upon heating:

$$
\ce{3\overset{+1}{Cl}O-(aq) -> 2\overset{-1}{Cl-}(aq) + \overset{+5}{Cl}O3-(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{4\overset{+5}{Cl}O3-(aq) -> \overset{-1}{Cl-}(aq) + 3\overset{+7}{Cl}O4-(aq)}
$$

However, **perchlorate ion ($\ce{ClO4-}$) cannot undergo disproportionation**. In $\ce{ClO4-}$, chlorine already sits at its maximum possible oxidation state of $+7$ (its total count of valence electrons). Because chlorine cannot lose any more electrons to reach a higher oxidation state, oxidation is impossible, ruling out disproportionation.

Similarly, **fluorine never disproportionates**. Because fluorine is the most electronegative element in the periodic table, it cannot exhibit any positive oxidation state. When fluorine reacts with dilute alkali:

$$
\ce{2\overset{0}{F2}(g) + 2OH-(aq) -> 2\overset{-1}{F-}(aq) + \overset{+2}{O}\overset{-1}{F2}(g) + H2O(l)}
$$

Fluorine drops from $0$ to $-1$ in both fluoride and oxygen difluoride. It is oxygen that is oxidised from $-2$ to $+2$ in $\ce{OF2}$.

## 5. The paradox of fractional oxidation numbers

Applying formal algebraic rules to certain compounds yields fractional oxidation numbers:
- Carbon in carbon suboxide ($\ce{C3O2}$): $+\frac{4}{3}$
- Bromine in tribromooctaoxide ($\ce{Br3O8}$): $+\frac{16}{3}$
- Sulphur in tetrathionate ion ($\ce{S4O6^2-}$): $+2.5$ ($+\frac{5}{2}$)
- Iron in magnetite ($\ce{Fe3O4}$): $+\frac{8}{3}$
- Lead in red lead ($\ce{Pb3O4}$): $+\frac{8}{3}$

Electrons are indivisible fundamental particles. A chemical bond cannot transfer half an electron. **A fractional oxidation number is merely an arithmetic average of different integer oxidation states possessed by distinct atoms of the same element.**

Examining the actual chemical structures reveals the true, whole-number oxidation state of each individual atomic site:

### Carbon suboxide ($\ce{C3O2}$)

Carbon suboxide possesses a linear cumulative double-bond structure:

$$
\ce{\overset{+2}{O}=\overset{+2}{C}=\overset{0}{C^*}=\overset{+2}{C}=\overset{-2}{O}}
$$

The two terminal carbon atoms are each double-bonded to an electronegative oxygen atom ($\Delta\chi > 0$) and double-bonded to the central carbon. Because the carbon-carbon double bond links identical atoms, its electrons are shared equally ($\Delta\chi = 0$). Each terminal carbon gives up two electron equivalents to oxygen, establishing an oxidation number of $+2$. The central carbon ($\text{C}^*$) is bonded only to carbon atoms on both flanks, experiencing zero net electron displacement, giving an oxidation state of $0$.

$$
\text{Average O.N.}(\text{C}) = \frac{(+2) + (0) + (+2)}{3} = +\frac{4}{3}
$$

### Tribromooctaoxide ($\ce{Br3O8}$)

In $\ce{Br3O8}$, the three bromine atoms form a chain flanked by terminal oxygen atoms:
- Each terminal bromine atom is bonded to three oxygen atoms ($\text{O}_3\text{Br}-$), giving an oxidation state of $+6$.
- The central bromine atom is bonded to two oxygen atoms ($-\text{BrO}_2-$), giving an oxidation state of $+4$.

$$
\text{Average O.N.}(\text{Br}) = \frac{(+6) + (+4) + (+6)}{3} = +\frac{16}{3}
$$

### Tetrathionate ion ($\ce{S4O6^2-}$)

In tetrathionate, four sulphur atoms form an unbranched backbone $[\ce{O3S-S-S-SO3}]^{2-}$:
- The two terminal sulphur atoms are bonded to three oxygen atoms each (two coordinate/double bonds and one single bond with formal charge $-1$). Sulphur donates 5 electron equivalents to the more electronegative oxygens, establishing an oxidation number of $+5$.
- The two interior sulphur atoms participate strictly in homonuclear $\ce{S-S}$ bonds. Because there is zero difference in electronegativity between bonded sulphur atoms, the shared electron pairs contribute zero to the oxidation number. Each central sulphur has an oxidation state of $0$.

$$
\text{Average O.N.}(\text{S}) = \frac{(+5) + (0) + (0) + (+5)}{4} = +\frac{10}{4} = +2.5
$$

### Mixed oxides: the curious case of red lead ($\ce{Pb3O4}$)

Mixed metal oxides such as $\ce{Fe3O4}$ ($\ce{FeO . Fe2O3}$), $\ce{Mn3O4}$ ($\ce{2MnO . MnO2}$), and red lead ($\ce{Pb3O4}$) similarly yield an average oxidation state of $+\frac{8}{3}$.

Red lead ($\ce{Pb3O4}$) is a stoichiometric crystal lattice comprising two moles of lead(II) oxide and one mole of lead(IV) oxide:

$$
\ce{Pb3O4} \equiv \ce{2PbO . PbO2}
$$

In $\ce{PbO}$, lead is present in its stable $+2$ oxidation state, while in $\ce{PbO2}$, lead is in the $+4$ oxidation state. Due to the inert pair effect in heavy Group 14 elements, $\text{Pb(II)}$ is far more thermodynamically stable than $\text{Pb(IV)}$. Consequently, $\ce{PbO2}$ acts as an energetic oxidising agent.

This structural composition explains why red lead behaves completely differently when treated with hydrochloric acid compared to nitric acid:

1. **Reaction with $\ce{HCl}$:**
   The basic component $\ce{PbO}$ undergoes a straightforward neutralisation reaction:
   $$
   \ce{2PbO + 4HCl -> 2PbCl2 + 2H2O}
   $$
   Meanwhile, $\ce{PbO2}$ oxidises chloride ions ($\ce{Cl-}$) to elemental chlorine gas ($\ce{Cl2}$), while itself reducing to $\text{Pb(II)}$:
   $$
   \ce{\overset{+4}{Pb}O2 + 4H\overset{-1}{Cl} -> \overset{+2}{Pb}Cl2 + \overset{0}{Cl2} + 2H2O}
   $$
   Summing both steps produces the overall equation:
   $$
   \ce{Pb3O4 + 8HCl -> 3PbCl2 + Cl2 + 4H2O}
   $$

2. **Reaction with $\ce{HNO3}$:**
   The basic component $\ce{PbO}$ again neutralises the acid:
   $$
   \ce{2PbO + 4HNO3 -> 2Pb(NO3)2 + 2H2O}
   $$
   However, nitric acid is itself a strong oxidising agent where nitrogen is already at its maximum oxidation state of $+5$. Because neither $\ce{PbO2}$ nor $\ce{HNO3}$ can oxidise the other, $\ce{PbO2}$ remains completely passive. It precipitates out as an insoluble chocolate-brown solid:
   $$
   \ce{Pb3O4 + 4HNO3 -> 2Pb(NO3)2 + PbO2(s) + 2H2O}
   $$

This striking difference directly verifies that $\ce{Pb3O4}$ is not a compound with a single fractional $+8/3$ state, but a genuine composite containing separate $\text{Pb(II)}$ and $\text{Pb(IV)}$ entities.
