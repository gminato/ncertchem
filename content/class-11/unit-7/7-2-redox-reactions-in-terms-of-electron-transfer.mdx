## 7.2 Redox Reactions as Electron Transfer Processes

The classical definitions of oxidation and reduction served early chemistry well, but they break down when no oxygen, hydrogen, or obvious elemental ions are traded. The true physical foundation of redox chemistry lies in the movement of subatomic particles: the **transfer of electrons**.

Consider the violent reactions of metallic sodium with non-metals:

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + Cl2(g) -> 2NaCl(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{4Na(s) + O2(g) -> 2Na2O(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + S(s) -> Na2S(s)}
$$

Under classical rules, sodium is oxidised in each reaction because oxygen or an electronegative element (chlorine, sulphur) is added to it. Simultaneously, chlorine, oxygen, and sulphur are reduced because the electropositive element sodium has been added to them.

However, from our study of chemical bonding, we know that sodium chloride, sodium oxide, and sodium sulphide are crystalline ionic lattices. They are far more accurately written as $\ce{Na+Cl-(s)}$, $\ce{(Na+)2O^{2-}(s)}$, and $\ce{(Na+)2S^{2-}(s)}$. The atoms did not merely rearrange; neutral sodium atoms lost electrons to become positively charged cations, while neutral non-metal atoms accepted those electrons to become negatively charged anions.

### Half-reactions reveal the mechanism

To see the electron transfer clearly, we can split any overall redox reaction into two separate steps called **half-reactions**. In the formation of sodium chloride:

$$
\ce{2Na(s) -> 2Na+ + 2e-} \quad \text{(electron loss)}
$$

$$
\ce{Cl2(g) + 2e- -> 2Cl-} \quad \text{(electron gain)}
$$

Adding these two half-reactions together cancels the two electrons on opposite sides, returning the balanced chemical equation:

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + Cl2(g) -> 2NaCl(s)}
$$

This splitting establishes the modern definitions of redox chemistry:

- **Oxidation:** The loss of one or more electrons by any chemical species.
- **Reduction:** The gain of one or more electrons by any chemical species.
- **Oxidising agent (oxidant):** An electron acceptor. By removing electrons from another substance, it oxidises that substance while being **itself reduced**.
- **Reducing agent (reductant):** An electron donor. By supplying electrons to another substance, it reduces that substance while being **itself oxidised**.

A popular mnemonic to keep the signs straight is **OIL RIG**: *Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain*.

As the electron enters the half-filled 3p orbital of chlorine, sodium begins to take on positive character, and chlorine begins to take on negative character. The transition state directly connects the neutral atoms to the newly born ions.
</Step>
<Step>
The transfer is complete. Sodium has shed its outermost electron to become the sodium cation, possessing the closed-shell configuration of neon. Chlorine has captured the electron to become the chloride anion, matching the closed-shell configuration of argon. The cation contracts while the anion expands. Powerful electrostatic attraction pulls the oppositely charged ions together into the regular repeating lattice of solid sodium chloride, releasing substantial lattice enthalpy.
</Step>
<Step>
We can split the overall chemical change into two separate equations, each showing the electrons explicitly. The oxidation half-reaction shows two sodium atoms yielding two sodium cations and releasing two electrons. The reduction half-reaction shows a chlorine molecule absorbing those two electrons to produce two chloride anions. Adding the two halves together causes the electrons on opposite sides to cancel completely, returning the balanced stoichiometric equation.
</Step>
<Step>
Every electron transfer defines two active roles. The substance that surrenders electrons is oxidised, and because its surrendered electrons reduce the partner, it acts as the reducing agent or reductant. The substance that captures electrons is reduced, and by stripping electrons from its partner, it acts as the oxidising agent or oxidant. Remember the classic mnemonic: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
</Step>

### Worked Problem: Justifying ionic hydride formation as a redox change

Recall the puzzling reaction from Section 7.1:

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + H2(g) -> 2NaH(s)}
$$

Because sodium hydride is an ionic solid consisting of $\ce{Na+}$ cations and $\ce{H-}$ hydride anions, we can write the two participating half-reactions directly:

$$
\ce{2Na(s) -> 2Na+ + 2e-} \quad \text{(Oxidation half)}
$$

$$
\ce{H2(g) + 2e- -> 2H-} \quad \text{(Reduction half)}
$$

Splitting the process into half-reactions proves that sodium loses electrons (it is oxidised and acts as the reducing agent) while elemental hydrogen gains electrons (it is reduced and acts as the oxidising agent). The electron concept removes all ambiguity.

## 7.2.1 Competitive Electron Transfer Reactions

Just as acids compete with one another to donate protons in aqueous solutions, different metals compete with one another to release electrons. To see this hierarchy in action, let us examine three benchmark laboratory experiments.

### Experiment 1: Zinc strip in aqueous copper nitrate

Place a clean strip of metallic zinc into an aqueous solution of copper(II) nitrate, $\ce{Cu(NO3)2}$. Within an hour:

1. The submerged portion of the zinc strip becomes coated with a velvety, reddish deposit of metallic copper.
2. The vibrant blue colour of the solution, which originates from hydrated $\ce{Cu^{2+}(aq)}$ ions, gradually fades until the solution becomes completely colourless.
3. If the colourless solution is decanted, made alkaline with aqueous ammonia, and treated with hydrogen sulphide gas ($\ce{H2S}$), a white precipitate of zinc sulphide ($\ce{ZnS}$) forms, confirming that $\ce{Zn^{2+}(aq)}$ ions have entered the solution.

The net ionic equation is:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) -> Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

Here, metallic zinc loses two electrons to form $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ (oxidation), and copper(II) ions accept those two electrons to plate out as metallic copper (reduction). Zinc releases electrons to copper ions.

What happens if we reverse the experiment by placing a strip of pure copper into an aqueous zinc sulphate solution? **No visible reaction occurs.** Even passing $\ce{H2S}$ gas into the solution yields no black precipitate of copper(II) sulphide ($\ce{CuS}$). Because copper(II) sulphide has an exceptionally tiny solubility product ($K_\text{sp} \approx 6 \times 10^{-36}$), this test can detect even infinitesimal traces of $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$. Its complete absence confirms that the equilibrium for the forward reaction overwhelmingly favours the products:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) -> Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)} \qquad (K \gg 1)
$$

### Experiment 2: Copper strip in aqueous silver nitrate

Now place a copper strip into a colourless solution of silver nitrate, $\ce{AgNO3}$. Over several hours:

1. The solution gradually turns blue due to the formation of hydrated $\ce{Cu^{2+}(aq)}$ ions.
2. Glittering, crystalline needles of metallic silver deposit onto the copper strip.

The balanced ionic equation is:

$$
\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
$$

Here copper metal releases electrons to silver cations: copper is oxidised to $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$, and silver ions are reduced to elemental silver.

### Experiment 3: Cobalt in aqueous nickel sulphate

By contrast, consider placing a strip of metallic cobalt into an aqueous solution of nickel sulphate:

$$
\ce{Co(s) + Ni^{2+}(aq) <=> Co^{2+}(aq) + Ni(s)}
$$

Chemical analysis at equilibrium reveals that both $\ce{Ni^{2+}(aq)}$ and $\ce{Co^{2+}(aq)}$ remain present at moderate concentrations. Neither the reactants nor the products are overwhelmingly favoured, because the electron-releasing tendencies of cobalt and nickel are very close to each other.

*(Note: While cobalt is discussed in NCERT Section 7.2.1, its standard reduction potential $E^\circ(\ce{Co^{2+}/Co}) = -0.28\ \text{V}$ is cited from standard electrochemical tables and is not printed in NCERT Table 7.1.)*

### The activity series and standard cell EMF

Comparing our three experiments establishes a clear hierarchy for the tendency to release electrons:

$$
\ce{Zn} > \ce{Cu} > \ce{Ag}
$$

Zinc displaces copper from its salts, and copper displaces silver from its salts. Listing metals in order of decreasing electron-releasing power creates the **metal activity series** (or electrochemical series).

Quantitatively, this tendency is measured by the **standard reduction potential**, $E^\circ$, tabulated in NCERT Table 7.1 at $298\ \text{K}$ (with solutes at $1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ and gases at $1\ \text{bar}$ standard pressure). A more negative reduction potential signifies a stronger reducing agent:

- $E^\circ(\ce{Zn^{2+}/Zn}) = -0.76\ \text{V}$
- $E^\circ(\ce{Fe^{2+}/Fe}) = -0.44\ \text{V}$
- $E^\circ(\ce{Pb^{2+}/Pb}) = -0.13\ \text{V}$
- $E^\circ(\ce{Cu^{2+}/Cu}) = +0.34\ \text{V}$
- $E^\circ(\ce{Ag+/Ag}) = +0.80\ \text{V}$

The standard cell potential for any combination is:

$$
E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ(\text{cathode}) - E^\circ(\text{anode})
$$

Where the cathode is the site of reduction (the higher potential) and the anode is the site of oxidation (the lower potential). For the classic Daniell cell pair ($\ce{Zn}$ in $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$):

$$
E^\circ_\text{cell} = +0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.76\ \text{V}) = +1.10\ \text{V}
$$

Because $E^\circ_\text{cell} > 0$, the forward displacement is thermodynamically spontaneous. For the reverse pair ($\ce{Cu}$ in $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$), $E^\circ_\text{cell} = -0.76\ \text{V} - (+0.34\ \text{V}) = -1.10\ \text{V}$, confirming that copper cannot spontaneously reduce $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$.

<KeyIdeas>
- Oxidation is the loss of electrons by a chemical species; reduction is the gain of electrons (OIL RIG).
- Splitting a reaction into half-reactions makes electron book-keeping explicit; in every balanced redox change, electrons lost must equal electrons gained.
- A reducing agent donates electrons and is itself oxidised; an oxidising agent accepts electrons and is itself reduced.
- Metals differ in their electron-releasing power ($\ce{Zn > Cu > Ag}$), which governs whether a metal can displace another cation from solution.
- The standard cell potential $E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ(\text{cathode}) - E^\circ(\text{anode})$ predicts spontaneity: a positive value denotes a spontaneous forward displacement.
</KeyIdeas>
