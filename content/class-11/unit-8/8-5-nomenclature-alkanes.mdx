<Hook>
The petrol you buy is sold with an octane number, and the top of that scale is set by one molecule. Its name, 2,2,4-trimethylpentane, looks like a password. It is not one. It is a set of instructions that tells you the shape of the molecule, carbon by carbon. Once you can read it, the name is the drawing.
</Hook>

Organic chemistry deals with millions of compounds, so a fixed, rule-based naming method is essential. Before one existed, compounds were named for where they came from or for a striking property. Citric acid is named after citrus fruit, and formic acid after the Latin for ant. Those names are still in use. The IUPAC system, which stands for the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, is the one that makes a name a reliable guide to a structure.

## 8.5.1 The IUPAC system

A systematic name has three parts, and you read them in a fixed order.

- The **word root** gives the number of carbons in the parent chain: *meth*, *eth*, *prop*, *but*, and so on.
- The **suffix** names the functional group, or the kind of bond: *-ane* for single bonds only, *-ene* for a double bond, *-ol* for an alcohol.
- The **prefixes** name the side chains and substituents, with the position of each one written as a locant.

The common names still have a place. Table 8.1 keeps some of them, for molecules that everybody uses. Isobutane and neopentane are two of the alkanes in that table, and the systematic names of both are short enough to build from the rules in the next section: isobutane is 2-methylpropane and neopentane is 2,2-dimethylpropane.

## 8.5.2 IUPAC nomenclature of alkanes

Alkanes contain only carbon-carbon single bonds, so the suffix is always *-ane*. We build their names in three steps: a straight chain, then the alkyl groups that hang off it, and finally the branched and cyclic cases.

### Straight chains

An unbranched alkane takes its name from its carbon count. The first four have trivial names, methane, ethane, propane and butane, and the rest take their stems from Greek or Latin number words. Every member of the series differs from the next by one $\ce{CH2}$ group, and every member has the formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$. Hexane, for instance, is $\ce{C6H14}$, and you can check that by counting.

Table 8.2 gives the names of the common straight-chain alkanes, from methane to decane, with icosane and triacontane at the far end of the table.

### Alkyl groups

An alkyl group is what remains when one hydrogen is taken from an alkane. You name it by changing the *-ane* of the alkane to *-yl*. Taking a hydrogen from methane gives the methyl group, $\ce{-CH3}$, and taking one from ethane gives the ethyl group, $\ce{-CH2CH3}$. Table 8.3 lists the common ones: methyl, ethyl, propyl and butyl.

Some groups have branched shapes, and they keep trivial names. The propyl group can be a straight chain or the isopropyl group, $\ce{-CH(CH3)2}$. The branched butyl groups are sec-butyl, isobutyl and tert-butyl. A group such as $\ce{-CH2C(CH3)3}$ is the neopentyl group, and the systematic name of that one is 2,2-dimethylpropyl, which is the form a systematic name takes.

In alphabetical order the prefixes *iso-* and *neo-* count as part of the group's name, but *sec-* and *tert-* do not. We will meet that rule again when we alphabetise the side chains.

### Branched alkanes

A branched alkane is named from a parent chain with side chains on it. Four rules settle the name, and they are used in this order.

1. **The longest chain is the parent.** Find the longest carbon chain in the molecule, even if it bends through a side chain. It sets the stem: a parent of seven carbons is a heptane.
2. **The numbering gives the branched carbons the lowest possible numbers.** Number the parent from the end nearest a side chain, and compare the two ends. The set of locants is compared term by term from the smallest, so the first point of difference decides.
3. **Side chains are named in alphabetical order, with a locant in front of each.** The multiplying prefixes *di-*, *tri-* and *tetra-* are ignored when alphabetising.
4. **Identical side chains are grouped.** Two methyls on carbon 2 are written as 2,2-dimethyl, with the locant repeated and the prefix *di-* added, and the locants of a group are separated by commas.

When two chains are of equal length, the parent is the one with more side chains. The worked example below shows that rule in action.

</Step>

</Step>

The example shows that the name is an argument, and each step must hold before the next one is taken. If you find yourself numbering from the end that gives a larger set, go back to rule 2.

### Worked example: a tie that the alphabet breaks

Sometimes the two ends give the same set of locants. Then the alphabetical rule decides, and the side chain that comes first in the alphabet takes the lower number. Take 3-ethyl-4-methylhexane: numbered from either end, the two side chains sit on carbons 3 and 4. The ethyl comes first alphabetically, so it takes carbon 3, and the methyl takes carbon 4. The name would be wrong if the methyl were given the 3.

### Cyclic compounds

A saturated ring of carbons is named by putting the prefix *cyclo-* in front of the alkane name. Cyclopropane has three carbons, and methylcyclopentane has a methyl on a five-carbon ring. When a single side chain sits on a ring, the locant can be dropped, because every ring carbon is equivalent to every other.

For two or more side chains, the ring is numbered so that the lowest locants go to the side chains together. If the locant set still ties, the alphabetical rule sets the order. In 1-ethyl-2-methylcyclopentane, the ethyl, which comes first alphabetically, takes carbon 1, and the methyl takes carbon 2.

</Check>

A branched alkyl group on a ring is treated the same way. Isopropylcyclohexane needs no locant, because a single side chain on a ring is unnumbered. When a second group is present, as in 1-isopropyl-2-methylcyclohexane, the alphabetical rule again gives the lower number to the group whose name comes first, and *isopropyl* begins with *i*, before *methyl*.

<KeyIdeas>
- A systematic name is built in order: the word root gives the parent chain, the suffix gives the bond or functional group, and the prefixes give the side chains with their locants.
- The parent is the longest carbon chain. Between chains of equal length, the one with more side chains wins.
- Numbering gives the lowest locant set, compared term by term from the smallest. Only when the two ends tie does the alphabetical rule decide.
- Side chains are named in alphabetical order, ignoring *di-*, *tri-* and *tetra-*, with *iso-* and *neo-* counted as part of the name and *sec-* and *tert-* not.
- A saturated ring takes the prefix *cyclo-*, and it is numbered so that the lowest locants go to its side chains.
</KeyIdeas>
