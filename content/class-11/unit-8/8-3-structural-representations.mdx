In a notebook, a classmate writes $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}$ for butane, a fuel gas. In the teacher's diagram the same molecule is a zig-zag line with no letters at all. Both are right. What did the zig-zag leave out, and why does nobody miss it?
</Hook>

## 8.3.1 Complete, condensed and bond-line formulas

A carbon compound has many atoms, and a drawing that shows every one of them is slow to read. Chemists therefore write the same molecule in three ways, and each way keeps what the reader needs for the job at hand. The complete formula keeps every bond and every atom. The condensed formula writes the atoms in a line and folds the hydrogens into the carbon they belong to. The bond-line formula keeps only the skeleton of the molecule.

<Step>
Here is one molecule in its complete structural formula. Every atom is written out, and every bond, including the bonds to hydrogen, is a line. The name is 4-methylpentan-2-ol, and the molecular formula $\ce{C6H14O}$ counts six carbons, fourteen hydrogens and one oxygen. The lines show which atom is joined to which. They say nothing about the shape in space.
</Step>
<Step>
The condensed formula is the same molecule written as a line of text: $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH(CH3)CH3}$. Read it from left to right. Each group is one carbon with the hydrogens written on it, such as $\ce{CH(OH)}$. A branch of a single carbon is written in brackets, as in $\ce{CH(CH3)}$. The bonds along the chain are understood, not drawn.
</Step>
<Step>
The bond-line formula is drawn from the same skeleton. Carbons sit at the corners of the zig-zag and at the ends of the lines. The hydrogens on carbon are left out, because a carbon with four bonds does not need them to be drawn. The oxygen and its hydrogen stay on the page, so the OH group is still there. Watch the complete formula fold into this picture as the hydrogens slide into their carbons.
</Step>
<Step>
A ring is drawn as a polygon. Cyclohexane, $\ce{C6H12}$, is a hexagon with a carbon at each corner. Each corner carries two hydrogens, which are not drawn, so the hexagon hides twelve hydrogens in all. Nothing in the picture is a letter, yet every corner is a carbon and every bond has its place.
</Step>
<Step>
A substituent on a benzene ring is a line that leaves the hexagon. This drawing is methylbenzene, $\ce{C7H8}$. The ring is drawn in its Kekulé form with three double bonds placed alternately. Five corners carry one hydrogen each, and the corner that holds the methyl group carries none, so the ring contributes five hydrogens and the methyl group adds three more.
</Step>
<Step>
A flat drawing cannot show depth on its own. A solid wedge comes towards you, a dashed wedge goes away from you, and the plain lines lie in the page. Bromochlorofluoromethane, $\ce{CHBrClF}$, has one carbon with four different groups. Turn the 3D model. The four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, and each pair of bonds makes an angle of $109.5^\circ$.
</Step>

## Rings and branches in bond-line form

NCERT draws a ring in bond-line form as a polygon, with a carbon at each corner. The same rule applies to the ring as to a chain: a line end or a corner is a carbon, and its hydrogens are implied. A substituent is a line that leaves the ring, and the atom at its end is written if it is not a carbon. A condensed line is written for open chains. For a ring, the bond-line hexagon is the clearer form, because it shows at once that the first and the last carbon are joined.

A complete formula and a bond-line formula describe the same molecule, so they must give the same molecular formula. Count the atoms in whichever drawing you have, and you can check the count against the formula in a second drawing.

## 8.3.2 Three-dimensional representation

A flat page cannot show the three-dimensional shape of a molecule, so chemists use two conventions. In a wedge and dash drawing, the bonds in the plane of the page are drawn as plain lines. A solid wedge is a bond that projects out of the page towards the reader. A dashed wedge is a bond that projects behind the page, away from the reader. The broad end of a wedge is always the end that is nearer the viewer.

NCERT's first example of this convention is methane, drawn with two plain bonds, one solid wedge and one dash. The Scrolly above uses a different carbon with four different groups, so that you can see which bonds are which.

Molecular models give the same information in a physical or on-screen form. A framework model shows only the bonds, a ball-and-stick model shows atoms as balls and bonds as sticks, and a space-filling model shows each atom at its relative size. All three show the same arrangement of atoms in space.

<KeyIdeas>
- A complete formula shows every bond and every atom. A condensed formula writes each carbon with its hydrogens in a line of text. A bond-line formula draws the skeleton only.
- In a bond-line drawing a corner or a line end is a carbon, and its hydrogens are implied. A heteroatom such as O and the hydrogen on it are always written.
- A ring is drawn as a polygon with a carbon at each corner. A condensed line is written for open chains only.
- A solid wedge is a bond towards the reader, a dashed wedge is a bond away from the reader, and a plain line lies in the page.
- A carbon with four bonds has them pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, with each bond angle $109.5^\circ$.
</KeyIdeas>
