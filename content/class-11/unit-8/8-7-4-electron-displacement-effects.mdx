## Electron displacement in covalent bonds

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, but the sharing is rarely even. When the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the pair leans towards the more electronegative atom, and the bond is polar. This lean is the first electron displacement effect. Some of them are permanent, because the displacement is built into the molecule. Others are temporary, because they appear only when an attacking reagent comes close.

The Scrolly below follows the four effects of this Section in the order they are usually met. The first two are permanent, the third and fourth are temporary, and the last explains why some carbocations last longer than others.

Start with 1-chloropentane. The $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is polar, because chlorine holds a shared pair more strongly than carbon does. That pull is passed along the chain through the σ bonds. The carbon next to chlorine carries a small positive charge, δ+. The next carbon feels a weaker pull, and the one after that feels weaker still. Each arrow marks the shift of electrons along one σ bond.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the acetate ion, $\ce{CH3COO-}$. Its negative charge is not fixed on one oxygen. Two Lewis structures can be drawn, and a pair of curved arrows turns one into the other. The lone pair on the lower oxygen forms a new $\ce{C=O}$ bond, and the old $\ce{C=O}$ pair slides onto the upper oxygen. Neither structure is the real ion. The real ion is their hybrid, with each $\ce{C-O}$ bond between a single and a double bond, and δ− on both oxygens.
</Step>
<Step>
Benzene has two Kekulé forms that differ only in where the three double bonds sit. The forms are equally good, so the real molecule is their hybrid. All six $\ce{C-C}$ bonds have the same length, 139 pm. That lies between the 154 pm of a $\ce{C-C}$ single bond and the 134 pm of a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond. The circle in the hexagon shows that the six π electrons spread evenly round the ring.
</Step>
<Step>
In aniline, the nitrogen carries a lone pair next to the ring. That lone pair can move into the ring, which leaves the ortho and para carbons richer in electrons, δ−. The arrows show one of these shifts: the N lone pair forms a bond to the ring carbon, and a ring pair slides onto the carbon at the far side. This is the positive resonance effect, +R, in which the group releases electrons into the ring.
</Step>
<Step>
Nitrobenzene works the other way. Electrons move out of the ring and into the nitro group, so the ortho and para carbons lose electron density and become δ+. The nitro group takes the electrons, which is the negative resonance effect, −R. The same two positions, ortho and para, are the ones that carry the δ+ here, so they are where an electron-rich reagent is drawn to attack.
</Step>
<Step>
The electromeric effect needs a reagent. Ethene has a π bond that is held loosely. When a hydrogen ion comes close, the π pair moves completely onto the carbon that takes the ion, and the other carbon is left as a positive centre. This is the +E effect, drawn with a curved arrow. Take the reagent away and the π pair returns to its bond. The shift is temporary.
</Step>
<Step>
The ethyl cation has an empty p orbital on its positive carbon. The $\ce{C-H}$ σ bonds of the neighbouring $\ce{CH3}$ group can line up with that orbital and donate a little electron density into it. This is hyperconjugation. The more $\ce{C-H}$ bonds that can do this, the more the charge is spread out. The ladder on the right shows the α-hydrogens counted for each carbocation: 9 for tertiary, 6 for secondary, 3 for primary and none for methyl.
</Step>

## Inductive effect (8.7.5)

The $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond is polar, and the size of the polarity depends on how far apart the two atoms sit on the Pauling scale. On that scale, the $\ce{C-Cl}$ difference is 3.16 − 2.55 = 0.61, the $\ce{C-Br}$ difference is 0.41 and the $\ce{C-H}$ difference is 0.35. These are the values in the electronegativity table of this unit.

$$
\Delta\chi(\text{$\ce{C-Cl}$}) = \chi(\text{Cl}) - \chi(\text{C}) = 3.16 - 2.55 = 0.61
$$

The polarity of one bond pulls on the next. The shift of the σ electrons in $\ce{C-Cl}$ makes the carbon next to it slightly positive, and that carbon pulls on its neighbour in turn. This transmission through σ bonds is the **inductive effect**. It is permanent, because it belongs to the molecule itself, and it weakens quickly along the chain. A substituent that pulls electrons towards itself is called electron-withdrawing, and it shows a −I effect. A substituent that pushes electrons away is electron-donating, with a +I effect.

The groups that show −I are the halogens, the nitro group ($\ce{-NO2}$), the cyano group ($\ce{-CN}$), the carboxyl group ($\ce{-COOH}$), the ester group ($\ce{-COOR}$) and the aryloxy group ($\ce{-OAr}$). The alkyl groups methyl ($\ce{-CH3}$) and ethyl ($\ce{-C2H5}$) are the usual +I groups. NCERT lists these groups without ranking them within each class, and this Section does the same.

## Resonance structures (8.7.6)

Some molecules cannot be written with one Lewis structure. Benzene is the classic case: a single structure with three double bonds would predict two lengths of $\ce{C-C}$ bond, yet all six bonds are equal. The answer is that the real molecule is a **resonance hybrid** of structures that differ only in where the electrons sit. Each of those structures is called a resonance (or canonical) contributor. A contributor is a hypothetical picture, and no single contributor is the molecule. A double-headed arrow, ↔, joins them.

The energy of the hybrid is lower than that of any contributor. The gap between the hybrid and the most stable contributor is the **resonance stabilisation energy**. The more important contributors there are, and the closer they are in energy, the larger that energy.

Contributors are written by moving electron pairs with curved arrows. Certain rules decide which contributors matter most. Read them as NCERT gives them, here in this Section's own words:

- Every contributor has the same nuclei in the same places and the same number of unpaired electrons.
- A contributor with more covalent bonds, counting each pair in a double bond twice, is more important.
- Every carbon and oxygen has a complete octet. Hydrogen is the exception, since it has only two electrons.
- A form with less separation of opposite charges is more important.
- A negative charge is better on the more electronegative atom, and a positive charge on the more electropositive one.
- The more the charge is spread out, the more stable the form.

The acetate ion has two contributors of equal energy, so both count equally. The hybrid draws each $\ce{C-O}$ bond at 1½ and puts δ− on both oxygens. Nitrobenzene is different. Its first contributor keeps the nitro group intact and has more covalent bonds. The two ring-charged contributors are less important, and the ranking tells you so.

## Resonance effect (8.7.7)

The inductive effect acts through σ bonds. The **resonance effect** acts through π bonds and lone pairs that lie next to a conjugated system. It is the polarity produced when a π bond, or a lone pair on an adjacent atom, interacts with the rest of the conjugated system. Like the inductive effect it is permanent, and it is written as R or M.

When the group releases electrons into the ring, the effect is positive, **+R**. The groups that do this are the halogens, $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-OR}$, $\ce{-OCOR}$, $\ce{-NH2}$, $\ce{-NHR}$, $\ce{-NR2}$ and $\ce{-NHCOR}$. Aniline is the example in the Scrolly: the lone pair on nitrogen raises the electron density at the ortho and para positions.

When the group takes electrons out of the ring, the effect is negative, **−R**. The groups are $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-CHO}$, the carbonyl group ($\ce{>C=O}$), $\ce{-CN}$ and $\ce{-NO2}$. In nitrobenzene the ring loses electron density at the ortho and para positions, which are the δ+ carbons of the hybrid.

## Electromeric effect (8.7.8)

The electromeric effect is the complete transfer of a shared π pair to one atom of a multiple bond, on the demand of an attacking reagent. It is temporary: the shift ends as soon as the reagent leaves the region of the reaction. It is written with the letter E and a curved arrow.

The positive electromeric effect, **+E**, moves the π pair to the atom that takes the reagent. In ethene, a hydrogen ion joins one carbon, and the π pair moves to that carbon, leaving the other carbon positive. The negative effect, **−E**, works the other way: a reagent such as the cyanide ion, $\ce{CN-}$, is the attacking species, and the π pair moves onto the atom that does not take the reagent. When the inductive and electromeric effects act in opposite directions, the electromeric effect is the stronger one, because it is triggered by the reagent.

## Hyperconjugation (8.7.9)

Hyperconjugation is a general stabilising interaction. It involves the delocalisation of the σ electrons of a $\ce{C-H}$ bond on an alkyl group into an adjacent empty p orbital or into a π bond. Consider the ethyl cation, $\ce{CH3CH2+}$. Its positive carbon has an empty p orbital. One of the $\ce{C-H}$ bonds of the methyl group lies in the plane of that orbital and can donate into it. This donation disperses the charge, and it is the reason why a carbocation with more α-hydrogens is more stable.

The α-hydrogens are the hydrogens on the carbon attached to the positive centre. They are counted for each carbocation, and the counts give the ordering in the ladder of the Scrolly: $\ce{(CH3)3C+}$ is tertiary, with nine α-hydrogens, then $\ce{(CH3)2CH+}$, then $\ce{CH3CH2+}$, and $\ce{CH3+}$ is the least stable, with none. The stability order is therefore 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl for carbocations. Carbanions show the opposite trend, because alkyl groups push electrons onto the negative carbon, which it does not want.

Because no atom moves and no bond breaks, hyperconjugation is sometimes called no-bond resonance. Its structures show an extra $\ce{C=C}$ bond and an $\ce{H+}$, but they are only contributors and not separate molecules.

## Types of organic reactions (8.7.10)

Organic reactions are grouped by what happens to the bonds. There are four main types, and Unit 9 and Class XII study them in detail. The first three are the familiar ones.

**Substitution.** One atom or group is replaced by another. The chloride in methyl chloride is replaced by hydroxide:

$$
\ce{CH3Cl + OH- -> CH3OH + Cl-}
$$

**Addition.** Two molecules join across a multiple bond, and the multiple bond becomes single. Ethene takes up hydrogen:

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2 -> CH3CH3}
$$

**Elimination.** A small molecule leaves from neighbouring atoms, and a multiple bond forms. In alcoholic potassium hydroxide, bromoethane gives ethene:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2Br + $\ce{KOH}$ -> CH2=CH2 + $\ce{KBr}$ + H2O}
$$

**Rearrangement.** An atom or group moves from one place in the molecule to another, and the molecule keeps the same atoms. A carbocation can change into a more stable one by such a shift.

<KeyIdeas>
- A polar covalent bond is an electron displacement. The inductive effect passes it along σ bonds and weakens with every bond, so it has nothing left after about three bonds.
- A resonance hybrid is the real molecule, and its contributors are hypothetical. Equal contributors share the charge equally; a form with more covalent bonds and less charge separation counts for more.
- The +R groups (such as $\ce{-NH2}$ and $\ce{-OH}$) feed electrons into a ring, and the −R groups (such as $\ce{-NO2}$ and $\ce{-CN}$) draw them out. Both leave δ charges on the ortho and para positions.
- The electromeric effect is temporary and needs a reagent. The +E shift moves a π pair onto the atom that takes the reagent.
- Hyperconjugation, a σ–π overlap, is called no-bond resonance. It explains why 3° carbocations are more stable than 1° carbocations and methyl.
</KeyIdeas>
