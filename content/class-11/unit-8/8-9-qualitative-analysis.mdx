<Hook>
Your medicine box holds a strip of paracetamol. Its formula, C₈H₉NO₂, is a fact that no one can read off a tablet by looking at it. Nobody saw the nitrogen in that molecule with their eyes. So how did chemists first learn that a carbon compound held nitrogen, when the molecules are far too small to see and the bonds are invisible?
</Hook>

## Which elements to look for

Carbon and hydrogen are the two elements in every organic compound. Many also hold oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, a halogen (chlorine, bromine or iodine) or phosphorus. Qualitative analysis answers one plain question for an unknown compound: which of these are present? It does not ask how much of each element there is. That is the job of quantitative analysis, in Section 8.10.

The tests work on a simple principle. Each element is first converted into a form that gives a colour or a precipitate you can see. Carbon and hydrogen are converted into gases that a reagent can detect. The other elements are converted into ions by fusing the compound with sodium, and the ions are tested in a solution.

## (a) Carbon and hydrogen

Heat the compound with copper(II) oxide, a strong oxidising agent. The carbon in the compound is burnt to carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen to water. Two observations then tell you what was there.

$$
\ce{C + 2CuO ->[\Delta] 2Cu + CO2}
$$

$$
\ce{H2 + CuO ->[\Delta] Cu + H2O}
$$

Pass the gas through lime water. If carbon was present, the carbon dioxide reacts with calcium hydroxide and the solution turns milky, because a fine precipitate of calcium carbonate forms:

$$
\ce{CO2 + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCO3 v + H2O}
$$

The water from the hydrogen is caught on anhydrous copper(II) sulphate. The white powder turns blue as it takes up water of crystallisation:

$$
\ce{5H2O + CuSO4 -> CuSO4.5H2O}
$$

White on the left, blue on the right. A compound with no hydrogen leaves the copper sulphate white, and a compound with no carbon leaves the lime water clear.

## (b) Nitrogen, sulphur, halogens and phosphorus

Sodium is the key reagent. Fuse the compound with a small piece of sodium in a dry tube, and the covalent bonds that hold N, S and the halogens to carbon are broken. Each of those atoms takes an electron from sodium and becomes an ion in a salt:

$$
\ce{Na + C + N -> NaCN}
$$

$$
\ce{2Na + S -> Na2S}
$$

$$
\ce{Na + X -> NaX}
$$

Here X stands for Cl, Br or I. The fused mass is boiled with distilled water. The solution is the **sodium fusion extract**, and it holds the cyanide, sulphide and halide ions. Every test for N, S and the halogens works on this one solution.

The scrolly below takes the compound through the fusion, the extract and the test tubes that name each element.

<Step>
Place the compound and a small sodium pellet together in a fusion tube. The sample is still covalent: its nitrogen, sulphur and halogen atoms sit in ordinary bonds, and the sodium is waiting to take them. Carbon and hydrogen have their own test with copper(II) oxide, so they are not part of this step.
</Step>
<Step>
Heat the tube until the mixture glows, and keep it hot until the fusion is complete. Sodium gives up its electron, and each atom of N, S or X takes one and becomes an ion: $\ce{Na + C + N -> NaCN}$, $\ce{2Na + S -> Na2S}$ and $\ce{Na + X -> NaX}$. The glow tells you the mixture has fused, and the tube must still be hot when it is plunged.
</Step>
<Step>
Plunge the hot tube into distilled water in a china dish. The glass cracks and throws off steam. Boil the fused mass with the water to dissolve the salts. You now have a solution of the sodium salts, and the rest of the tests are run on it.
</Step>
<Step>
Filter the mixture and keep the clear liquid. This is the sodium fusion extract. It holds the cyanide ion, CN⁻, and the sulphide ion, S²⁻, when those elements were in the compound, and the halide ion, X⁻, when a halogen was present. Each test that follows looks for one of these ions.
</Step>
<Step>
The first tubes test for nitrogen and sulphur. A solution of cyanide, boiled with iron(II) sulphate and then acidified, gives Prussian blue, Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃. Sulphide gives a black precipitate of PbS with lead acetate, and a violet colour with sodium nitroprusside. When nitrogen and sulphur are both present, the blood-red tube is the one to read, as the next Step explains.
</Step>
<Step>
The last tubes test the halogens and phosphorus. Acidify the extract with dilute HNO₃, and if N or S may be present, boil it with concentrated HNO₃ first. Then add AgNO₃. A white AgCl precipitate that dissolves in ammonium hydroxide means chlorine, a pale yellow AgBr that is only sparingly soluble means bromine, and a yellow AgI that stays insoluble means iodine. Phosphorus is tested on its own: oxidised to phosphate, it gives a yellow precipitate with ammonium molybdate.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## (c) The nitrogen test

Sodium cyanide reacts with iron(II) ions to form hexacyanoferrate(II). When the mixture is heated with concentrated sulphuric acid, some of the iron(II) is oxidised to iron(III), and the iron(III) reacts with the complex to form the intensely coloured Prussian blue, Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃. The colour confirms nitrogen.

$$
\ce{6CN- + Fe^2+ -> [Fe(CN)6]^4-}
$$

$$
\ce{3[Fe(CN)6]^4- + 4Fe^3+ -> Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3.xH2O}
$$

The second equation is written with x molecules of water retained in the solid, as the NCERT text does. Only the four iron(III) ions and the three hexacyanoferrate(II) units need to balance, and they do: the charges are −12 and +12.

## (d) The sulphur test

Sulphide ions from the extract react with lead(II) ions. The black precipitate of lead sulphide confirms sulphur.

$$
\ce{S^2- + Pb^2+ -> PbS v}
$$

With sodium nitroprusside, sulphide gives a violet complex, [Fe(CN)₅NOS]⁴⁻. This is a second test, and the two together make a sound case for sulphur.

## (e) When nitrogen and sulphur are both present

Now the ordinary fusion behaves differently. The cyanide and the sulphide do not come out as separate ions. Sodium, nitrogen and sulphur combine into sodium thiocyanate, NaSCN. Thiocyanate holds no free cyanide, so the Prussian blue test is negative. It also holds no free sulphide, and the usual sulphide tests need excess sodium, as the next paragraph explains.

$$
\ce{Na + C + N + S -> NaSCN}
$$

Thiocyanate gives its own test. Fe³⁺ ions bind it to form a blood-red complex, and the colour is the sign that both nitrogen and sulphur are in the compound.

$$
\ce{Fe^3+ + SCN- -> [Fe(SCN)]^2+}
$$

If you fuse with an excess of sodium, the thiocyanate is broken down. The surplus sodium does the work:

$$
\ce{NaSCN + 2Na -> NaCN + Na2S}
$$

Now the cyanide and sulphide are free again, and the Prussian blue and sulphide tests come back. The blood-red test does not, because the thiocyanate has gone.

## (f) The halogen test

After a fusion, the halide ion is in the extract as X⁻. Silver nitrate gives a precipitate of the silver halide:

$$
\ce{X- + Ag+ -> AgX v}
$$

There is a catch. If the compound also held nitrogen or sulphur, the extract also holds CN⁻ or S²⁻, and silver ions precipitate those too. Silver cyanide and silver thiocyanate are white, and silver sulphide is black, so they would hide the halide colour. That is why, when nitrogen or sulphur is present, the extract is boiled with concentrated nitric acid first. The acid destroys the cyanide and the sulphide, and only the halide is left to precipitate. Without N or S there is nothing to remove, and the acidified extract can go straight to AgNO₃.

The three silver halides differ in colour and in solubility in ammonium hydroxide, which is how the halogen is named:

- chlorine gives a white AgCl precipitate that dissolves in ammonium hydroxide;
- bromine gives a pale yellow AgBr precipitate that is only sparingly soluble in ammonium hydroxide;
- iodine gives a yellow AgI precipitate that does not dissolve in ammonium hydroxide.

## (g) The phosphorus test

Phosphorus is not tested in the sodium fusion extract. The compound is heated with an oxidising agent, sodium peroxide, which converts the phosphorus into phosphate. The solution is boiled with nitric acid and then treated with ammonium molybdate. A yellow precipitate shows phosphorus.

$$
\ce{Na3PO4 + 3HNO3 -> H3PO4 + 3NaNO3}
$$

$$
\ce{H3PO4 + 12(NH4)2MoO4 + 21HNO3 -> (NH4)3PO4.12MoO3 v + 21NH4NO3 + 12H2O}
$$

The yellow precipitate is written here in the NCERT form. Its ammonium phosphomolybdate structure is more complex than this shorthand, but the yellow colour is the test.

<KeyIdeas>
- Carbon and hydrogen are found by heating the compound with CuO: CO₂ turns lime water milky, and water turns anhydrous CuSO₄ blue.
- Sodium fusion turns covalently bound N, S and halogens into NaCN, Na₂S and NaX. The sodium fusion extract is the solution that every test for those elements uses.
- Nitrogen shows as Prussian blue and sulphur as black PbS or violet nitroprusside. With N and S together, the ordinary fusion gives thiocyanate and a blood-red colour instead.
- The halide test needs the extract boiled with HNO₃ first, because AgCN, AgSCN and Ag₂S would otherwise hide the silver halide colour.
- Phosphorus has its own test: peroxide fusion, nitric acid and ammonium molybdate give a yellow precipitate.
</KeyIdeas>
