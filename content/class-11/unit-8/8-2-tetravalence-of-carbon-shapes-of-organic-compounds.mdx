## 8.2 Tetravalence of carbon

Carbon has four electrons in its outer shell and forms four covalent bonds in almost every compound. That is what tetravalence means. Unit 4 explained the bonds through the mixing of atomic orbitals, and the same idea sets the shape of every carbon atom in an organic molecule. The number of groups bonded to a carbon, and the number of π bonds it carries, decide which hybrid orbitals it uses.

### 8.2.1 The shapes of carbon compounds

Methane, ethene and ethyne show the three shapes. In methane, each carbon uses four $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals, which point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron, so every H–C–H angle is $109.5^\circ$. In ethene, each carbon uses three $sp^2$ hybrids that lie in one plane at $120^\circ$, and the leftover $p$ orbital stands at right angles to that plane. In ethyne, each carbon uses two $sp$ hybrids that point in opposite directions, so the molecule is linear and every bond angle is $180^\circ$.

The hybrid orbitals also decide the bond lengths. An $sp$ orbital has $50\%$ $s$ character, so it sits closer to the nucleus than an $sp^2$ orbital ($33\%$) or an $sp^3$ orbital ($25\%$). The bonds it forms are shorter and stronger. The carbon itself changes too: the more $s$ character a carbon's hybrid orbitals have, the more strongly it pulls on bonding electrons.

### 8.2.2 Some characteristic features of π bonds

A π bond is weaker than the σ bond in the same pair of atoms, because its electron cloud overlaps less. The overlap is sideways, so the two $p$ orbitals must be parallel to each other for the overlap to be large. Both $p$ orbitals in a C=C bond must stand perpendicular to the plane of the σ framework, and that is why every atom of ethene lies in one plane.

Rotation about a double bond is therefore restricted. If one CH₂ group turns, its $p$ orbital tilts away from the other one, and the overlap falls. The energy of that lost overlap is the barrier to turning. Single bonds, which have no π overlap, turn much more freely.

The electron density of a π bond lies above and below the plane of the atoms, where an incoming reagent can reach it. For that reason, π bonds are usually the most reactive centres in a molecule with multiple bonds. Acrylonitrile, CH₂=CH–C≡N, shows it: its three carbons are $sp^2$, $sp^2$ and $sp$, and the π bonds are where its reactions begin.

<KeyIdeas>

- Carbon's shape comes from its hybrid orbitals: sp³ is tetrahedral at $109.5^\circ$, sp² is trigonal planar at $120^\circ$ and sp is linear at $180^\circ$.
- A more $s$-rich hybrid, such as sp (50% $s$), gives a shorter, stronger bond: C–C is about $154\ \text{pm}$ for a single bond, $134\ \text{pm}$ for a double bond and $120\ \text{pm}$ for a triple bond.
- A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond. A triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds.
- A π bond forms by sideways overlap of parallel $p$ orbitals, so rotation about the double bond is restricted.
- Multiple bonds are the most reactive centres, because their π electrons lie above and below the plane of the atoms.

</KeyIdeas>
