<Hook>
Ethanol, the spirit in a tincture bottle, boils at about 78 °C. Add one $\ce{-CH2-}$ unit to its chain and you get propan-1-ol, which boils at about 97 °C. One carbon and two hydrogens move the boiling point by nearly twenty degrees, yet the two liquids behave in much the same way. Why does a small step in a chain change so much, and so little at the same time?
</Hook>

## Two ways to sort a molecule

There are very many organic compounds, so chemists sort them. The first sort looks at the skeleton: whether the carbons form an open chain or a ring. The second sort looks at the functional group, the atom or group of atoms that decides how the compound reacts. The two sorts together place almost any compound in a family.

The Scrolly below follows the first sort, one class at a time, and then turns to the second idea with a series of alcohols.

<Scrolly animation="classification-tree">
<Step>
Start with the skeleton. An acyclic compound has its carbons joined in an open chain, straight or branched, and no ring closes it. Hexane, $\ce{CH3(CH2)4CH3}$, is a straight chain. 2-methylpentane has a branch but no ring, so it is acyclic too. Acetic acid, called ethanoic acid by IUPAC, is open-chain as well: a two-carbon chain can carry a functional group and still have no ring.
</Step>
<Step>
Now close the chain into a ring of carbon atoms. Cyclopropane, cyclohexane and cyclohexene are alicyclic compounds: their rings are made of carbon, and none of them is aromatic. Cyclohexene keeps one double bond in its ring, and the other two are saturated. The alicyclic column is the one the tree adds next.
</Step>
<Step>
Some rings are aromatic. Benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, is a flat ring of six carbons with six π electrons, and it is the parent of the benzenoid compounds. Naphthalene, two fused rings, is benzenoid too. Tropone is a seven-membered ring with a C=O group. Its ring still holds six π electrons, because the carbonyl carbon contributes none. It is aromatic but not benzenoid, so it is non-benzenoid.
</Step>
<Step>
When an atom of the ring is not carbon, the compound is heterocyclic. Furan (one O), thiophene (one S) and pyridine (one N) are heterocyclic aromatic compounds. Tetrahydrofuran has an oxygen in a saturated ring. It is heterocyclic but not aromatic, and it behaves like an ether. Every compound in the tree now has a home, and the four columns are the four classes.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the second sort. A homologous series is a family of compounds that share one functional group and differ by a $\ce{-CH2-}$ unit. These five alcohols run from methanol to pentan-1-ol. Each member is the one before it with one more carbon in the chain. From ethanol on, the shaded carbon is the one that was added. Every member keeps the same –OH group, so every member behaves as an alcohol.
</Step>
<Step>
Boiling points rise along the series: 65 °C for methanol, 78 °C for ethanol, 97 °C for propan-1-ol, 118 °C for butan-1-ol and 138 °C for pentan-1-ol. Each CH₂ adds between 13 and 21 degrees. The physical properties change gradually along the series, and the chemistry of the –OH group stays much the same. The boiling points are handbook data, not NCERT figures.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## The functional group

A functional group is the atom or group of atoms responsible for the characteristic chemistry of a compound. The –OH group makes an alcohol, the –CHO group makes an aldehyde and the –COOH group makes a carboxylic acid. Two compounds with the same skeleton can have very different reactions if their functional groups differ. Ethanol and methoxymethane, for example, share the formula $\ce{C2H6O}$ but have different groups, and so different chemistry.

Some molecules carry more than one functional group. They are called polyfunctional compounds. When that happens, the highest-priority group names the compound. For the groups this Section uses, NCERT's order runs: carboxylic acid, ester, amide, nitrile, aldehyde, ketone, alcohol, amine, alkene and alkyne. ## Why the series behaves so regularly

A homologous series is held together by its functional group and by the common $\ce{-CH2-}$ step. Members share the same general molecular formula, and each successive member differs from the one before it by exactly one CH₂ unit. Because the size of the molecule grows steadily, the forces between molecules grow steadily too, and the physical properties move in step. The chemistry, which depends on the group, stays put.

<KeyIdeas>
- Organic compounds sort first by skeleton: acyclic (open chain) or cyclic, and the cyclic ones into alicyclic, aromatic and heterocyclic classes.
- Aromatic compounds are benzenoid (containing a benzene ring, such as benzene or naphthalene) or non-benzenoid (such as tropone).
- A heterocyclic compound has an atom other than carbon in its ring. Furan, thiophene and pyridine are aromatic; tetrahydrofuran is not.
- A functional group is the atom or group that sets a compound's chemistry. Polyfunctional compounds are named by the highest-priority group.
- A homologous series differs by one $\ce{-CH2-}$ unit at each step. Physical properties change gradually along the series, and the chemistry stays the same.
</KeyIdeas>
