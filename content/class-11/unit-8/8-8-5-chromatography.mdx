## What chromatography separates

Chromatography is a family of techniques for separating the parts of a mixture, purifying a compound and checking how pure it is. The name comes from the Greek *chroma*, colour, because the first separations were of coloured plant pigments. The idea is the same for every member of the family. A mixture is placed on a **stationary phase**, which stays put, and a **mobile phase** moves over it. Each component is held back by the stationary phase to a different degree, so the components leave the stationary phase at different times or places.

Organic chemistry uses two families. In **adsorption chromatography** the stationary phase is a solid adsorbent that holds the molecules on its surface, most often silica gel or alumina. In **partition chromatography** the stationary phase is a liquid, here the water held in the fibres of special paper, and the components divide between that liquid and the moving solvent.

## (a) Adsorption chromatography

### The column

A glass tube is packed with a fine adsorbent, and a stopcock at its lower end controls the flow. The mixture is loaded at the top, and an eluent, which is a liquid or a mixture of liquids, is run slowly down the column. The compounds that are adsorbed most readily are retained near the top, and the others come down to different depths. Collected in tubes, the eluent carries each component out separately.

### Thin layer chromatography

In thin layer chromatography, or TLC, the adsorbent is spread as a layer about 0.2 mm thick over a glass plate. The layer is usually silica gel, chiefly $\ce{SiO2}$, or alumina, $\ce{Al2O3}$. The solution of the mixture is applied as a small spot on a line near one end of the plate, which is called the origin. The plate is stood upright in a closed jar containing a little solvent, and the solvent rises up the plate by capillary action, carrying the spots with it. When the solvent front is near the top, the plate is removed and dried.

Plates like this are called chromaplates, and the sample must sit above the level of the solvent in the jar. Otherwise the sample dissolves into the pool and never climbs.

### Rf: the one number you read from a plate

Each spot moves a characteristic fraction of the solvent's distance. The retardation factor, $R_f$, records that fraction:

$$
R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the substance from the baseline}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent from the baseline}}
$$

Both distances are measured from the same origin, so the ratio is a pure number. It is always less than 1, because no spot can outrun the front, and it has no unit. Under the same solvent and adsorbent, the same substance gives the same $R_f$, which is why an unknown can be compared with a standard run beside it. Change the solvent or the adsorbent, and the values change.

### Making colourless spots visible

Coloured spots are visible on a plate to the eye. Colourless compounds are not, so the plate must be treated. A plate with a fluorescent indicator glows under ultraviolet light, and a spot that absorbs the UV appears dark against that glow; ultraviolet light therefore reveals many colourless compounds without touching them. Iodine works differently: the plate is placed in a chamber with a few crystals of iodine, $\ce{I2}$, and the spots of compounds that adsorb iodine turn brown. Sometimes an appropriate spray is used instead. Amino acids, for example, are detected by spraying the plate with ninhydrin.

## (b) Partition chromatography: paper

Partition chromatography depends on continuous, differential partitioning of the components between a stationary and a mobile phase. Paper chromatography is one type of it. The paper is a special grade whose fibres trap water, $\ce{H2O}$, and that water is the stationary phase. A strip of the paper is spotted near its base and dipped in a suitable solvent, or a mixture of solvents, which is the mobile phase. Capillary action carries the solvent up the strip, and each component divides between the trapped water and the moving solvent in its own proportion. The developed strip is called a chromatogram, and its spots are read off by their heights, or by $R_f$ values measured the same way as on a plate.

The lesson is the same: the component that spends more time in the stationary phase travels less far.

<KeyIdeas>
- A mixture separates because its components move at different rates over a stationary phase carried along by a mobile phase.
- Column and thin layer chromatography are adsorption methods; paper chromatography is a partition method, with water in the paper as the stationary phase.
- $R_f$ is the distance moved by the spot divided by the distance moved by the solvent front, both measured from the origin. It is between 0 and 1 and has no unit.
- In a column, the most strongly adsorbed component stays nearest the top, and the least strongly adsorbed comes off first.
- Colourless spots show under ultraviolet light, or as brown spots after iodine vapour, which adsorbs onto many organic compounds.
</KeyIdeas>
