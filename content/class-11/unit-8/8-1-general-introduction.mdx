<Hook>
The urea in your urine was, for a long time, thought to be beyond any laboratory. A living body had to make it, and a flask could not. Then a chemist heated a salt that no animal had ever touched, and urea came out of the glass. Why did that one result matter so much?
</Hook>

## 8.1 Organic compounds and their history

Organic compounds are everywhere, and most of them carry the element carbon. The genetic molecule DNA, the proteins in blood and muscle, the skin and the fibres of clothing are all organic. So are the fuels in your kitchen and your car, the plastics, the dyes and most medicines. Organic chemistry is the branch that studies all of them.

The subject is about two hundred years old. Around 1780, chemists began to tell apart the compounds that came from plants and animals from those that came from minerals. Berzelius, the Swedish chemist, proposed that a *vital force* was needed to build the first kind. On that view, a laboratory could take apart an organic compound, but it could never put one together from inorganic materials.

<Step>
In 1828, Wöhler heated ammonium cyanate, a salt with no living origin. The salt rearranged in the flask, and it gave urea, a compound that the body makes and passes out in urine. The reaction is $\ce{NH4CNO ->[heat] NH2CONH2}$, and no vital force was involved.
</Step>
<Step>
The vital force idea did not survive the result. Urea was an organic compound that had come from an inorganic salt. The drawing shows the molecule and its formula, $\ce{CH4N2O}$, which follows from the structure. Its name is urea, which is the IUPAC name the drawing shows.
</Step>
<Step>
In 1845, Kolbe made acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, from inorganic starting materials. Its IUPAC name is ethanoic acid. In 1856, Berthelot made methane, the simplest organic molecule. Both syntheses started from inorganic materials, so neither needed a living source, and each showed that an organic compound is not a special product of living things.
</Step>
<Step>
Carbon then offers a second reason for the subject to be large. Its atoms bond to one another, so they form straight chains, branched chains and rings, as the three skeletons here show. This ability is called catenation, and it lets carbon build an enormous variety of compounds from one element.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## 8.1.1 Why carbon forms so many compounds

Carbon forms four bonds. It also bonds to other carbon atoms, and the chain can keep growing. Hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, phosphorus and the halogens join the chain at many points. That is why carbon forms so many compounds, and the number of possible arrangements grows quickly with the number of carbon atoms.

## 8.1.2 What the history leaves us

The vital force idea is gone, but the lesson it taught stays. Organic compounds are made of the same atoms as the rest of matter, joined by the same bonding ideas that you met in Unit 4. What sets them apart is carbon's ability to form long chains and rings, and the rules of that bonding are the subject of the rest of this unit.

<KeyIdeas>

- Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds, and it covers DNA, proteins, fuels, polymers, dyes and medicines.
- Berzelius's vital force idea said that organic compounds could only come from living things. Wöhler's synthesis of urea from ammonium cyanate in 1828 showed that idea was wrong.
- Kolbe (1845) made acetic acid and Berthelot (1856) made methane from inorganic sources, which confirmed the result in the laboratory.
- Catenation is carbon's ability to bond to carbon, so it forms chains, branches and rings, and this is why the number of organic compounds is so large.

</KeyIdeas>
