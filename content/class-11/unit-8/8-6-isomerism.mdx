<Hook>
Ethanol, the alcohol in a pharmacy bottle, and dimethyl ether, a gas used as a fuel and as an aerosol propellant, both have the formula $\ce{C2H6O}$. The same two elements and the same six atoms, yet one is a liquid at room temperature and the other is a gas. What are the atoms doing differently?
</Hook>

## Same formula, different molecule

Two compounds with the same molecular formula but different properties are called isomers, and the phenomenon is isomerism. A molecular formula counts the atoms and nothing more. It does not say how the atoms are joined, or how they sit in space, and those two details decide most of a compound's behaviour.

NCERT divides isomerism into two families. In structural isomerism the atoms are joined in a different order. In stereoisomerism the joins are the same, but the atoms or groups are arranged differently in space.

## (a) Structural isomerism

Structural isomers differ in their bonding. The four kinds are named by what has changed:

- **Chain isomers** have different carbon skeletons: the same carbons form a straight chain, or a shorter chain with a branch.
- **Position isomers** keep the same skeleton and the same functional group, but the group sits on another carbon.
- **Functional group isomers** have different functional groups, such as an alcohol and an ether.
- **Metamers** keep the same functional group, but the alkyl groups on either side of it differ.

The Scrolly below takes one example of each kind. Its last two Steps show the geometrical isomers of but-2-ene, which belong to the next subsection.

<Step>
All three molecules have the formula $\ce{C5H12}$. Pentane, $\ce{CH3(CH2)3CH3}$, is the straight chain of five carbons. The shaded bonds mark the longest chain of each molecule. 2-methylbutane has a four-carbon chain with one carbon branching off it. 2,2-dimethylpropane has a three-carbon chain with two methyl groups on the middle carbon. The formula is the same in all three, so these are chain isomers.
</Step>
<Step>
Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are both $\ce{C3H8O}$ alcohols. Each has a three-carbon chain and an –OH group, and the only difference is the carbon the –OH is attached to: the end carbon in one and the middle carbon in the other. Moving a group to another position on the same skeleton gives position isomers.
</Step>
<Step>
Ethanol has an –OH group on a carbon, so it is an alcohol. Methoxymethane has an oxygen between two methyl groups and no O–H bond, so it is an ether. Both have the formula $\ce{C2H6O}$. A change in functional group is what makes these functional group isomers, and it is a much bigger change than moving a group.
</Step>
<Step>
Ethoxyethane has an ether oxygen between two ethyl groups. 1-methoxypropane has the same ether oxygen, now between a methyl group and a propyl group. The functional group is unchanged, and only the alkyl groups on either side differ. Both are $\ce{C4H10O}$, so they are metamers.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the stereoisomers. In but-2-ene, $\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}$, the double bond holds both central carbons in one plane, and the two methyl groups can point to the same side. Drag the molecule to turn it. Here both $\ce{CH3}$ groups lie on one side of the double bond: this is cis-but-2-ene.
</Step>
<Step>
Turn the other way and the methyl groups point to opposite sides of the double bond: trans-but-2-ene. Every atom is bonded to the same neighbours in both molecules, so they are not structural isomers. Only their arrangement in space differs, and that is what makes them stereoisomers.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## (b) Stereoisomerism

The compounds in this group have the same constitution and the same sequence of covalent bonds. They differ only in the relative positions of their atoms or groups in space. NCERT names two branches of stereoisomerism: geometrical isomerism and optical isomerism. The Scrolly above draws the first. Optical isomerism is named here but not drawn in this Section.

Geometrical isomerism needs two conditions. First, the molecule must have a double bond or a ring that stops free rotation. A σ bond can turn, but the π bond of a C=C double bond would have to break for the two carbons to twist. Second, each doubly bonded carbon must carry two different groups. In but-2-ene every carbon has one $\ce{CH3}$ group and one H, so a cis form and a trans form exist.

<KeyIdeas>
- Isomers share a molecular formula but differ in their structure, or in the arrangement of their atoms in space.
- Structural isomers differ in the skeleton (chain), the position of a group (position), the functional group, or the alkyl groups on either side of a group (metamers).
- $\ce{C5H12}$ has three structural isomers and $\ce{C7H16}$ has nine. Each count comes from the enumeration of carbon skeletons, not from a list copied by hand.
- Geometrical isomers need a double bond, or a ring, that stops free rotation, and two different groups on each carbon of that bond. In cis-but-2-ene the two methyl groups lie on one side; in trans-but-2-ene they lie on opposite sides.
- Optical isomerism is the other branch of stereoisomerism. NCERT names it at this stage, and this Section does not draw it.
</KeyIdeas>
