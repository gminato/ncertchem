## Mechanisms: the story behind a reaction

A reaction written as a single arrow tells you what goes in and what comes out, but not how it happens. In an organic reaction the molecule we are following is the **substrate**, and the other reactant that brings the new bond with it is the **reagent**. Between the two, a short-lived species called an **intermediate** may form on the way to the **product**, and a small molecule or ion that does not end up in the product is a **byproduct**.

The full, step-by-step account of a reaction is its **mechanism**. A mechanism names every step, shows which electrons move in each step, tracks the energy of each change, and says how fast each step runs. This section and the ones after it build the vocabulary for that account. Here we deal with the first question of any step: which bond breaks, and where do its electrons go?

## (a) Fission of a covalent bond

A covalent bond holds a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. When the bond breaks, the pair can go in two ways, and the two ways give very different species.

### Heterolysis: the pair goes to one atom

In **heterolytic cleavage** both electrons of the bond stay with one of the two atoms. The atom that receives them becomes negatively charged, with a lone pair. The atom that gave them up is left with six electrons in its valence shell and a positive charge. For a bromoalkane, the bromine takes the pair, as it is the more electronegative atom. The alkyl part is left as a cation:

$$
\ce{(CH3)2CH-Br -> (CH3)2CH^{+} + Br^{-}}
$$

A species with a carbon that carries six electrons and a positive charge is a **carbocation**, formerly called a carbonium ion. Its three bonds lie in one plane, and the carbon is $sp^2$ hybridised. The fourth orbital, a $p$ orbital perpendicular to that plane, is empty, and it is the reason the carbocation seeks electrons.

A species with a carbon that carries a lone pair and a negative charge is a **carbanion**. The carbon has four electron pairs in all, three in bonds and one as a lone pair, so it is $sp^3$ hybridised and pyramidal rather than flat. Reactions that go by heterolysis are called **ionic** or **polar** reactions.

### Homolysis: each atom keeps one electron

In **homolytic cleavage** the bond splits so that each atom takes one of the two electrons. No charge appears. The fragments are **free radicals**, species with an unpaired electron. Homolysis needs an input of energy, usually heat or light. A reaction that goes through free radicals is called a **free-radical** or **homopolar** reaction. Radicals are electrically neutral, but they are just as reactive as ions.

### Which intermediate is more stable?

Carbocations and radicals are most stable when the centre is surrounded by carbon. Two effects do the work. Alkyl groups push electron density towards the centre, which is the inductive, or $+I$, effect, and this spreads the positive charge or the single electron. The C–H bonds on the neighbouring carbons also overlap with the empty or half-filled $p$ orbital, which is hyperconjugation. This overlap is a $\sigma$–$\pi$ interaction, and it spreads the charge without any bond being broken. Each such C–H bond is an $\alpha$-hydrogen of the centre.

The result is a ladder. The *tert*-butyl cation has three alkyl groups and nine $\alpha$-hydrogens. The isopropyl cation has two alkyl groups and six $\alpha$-hydrogens. The ethyl cation has one and three, and the methyl cation has none. The order is $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > $ methyl, and radicals follow the same ladder.

Carbanions run the other way. Here the alkyl groups push extra electron density onto a carbon that already has a negative charge, which makes it less stable. A methyl anion is the most stable of the series, and the *tert*-butyl anion the least.

## (b) Substrate and reagent

The two reactants of a reaction do not have to be equal partners. When a new bond joins carbon to carbon, the choice of which molecule is the substrate and which is the reagent is arbitrary. The convention is to name the substrate as the molecule whose carbon we are following. If a tertiary bromide reacts with hydroxide, we call the bromide the substrate and the hydroxide the reagent, because we are tracking the tertiary carbon.

### Nucleophiles and electrophiles

A reagent attacks a site on the substrate that is short of electrons, or it is attacked at a site that has electrons to spare. The two kinds of reagent are named for this.

A **nucleophile** (nucleus-seeking) brings a pair of electrons to the reaction. It is electron-rich. Hydroxide, $\ce{OH^-}$, is one: its oxygen carries a negative charge and a lone pair. Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, is another. A neutral molecule with a lone pair on nitrogen, oxygen or sulphur can act as a nucleophile too.

An **electrophile** (electron-seeking) accepts a pair of electrons. It is electron-poor. A carbocation is an electrophile because its carbon has only six electrons and a positive charge. Boron trifluoride, with three bonds and no fourth, is another: boron has only six electrons around it. A carbon that is bonded to a halogen, or joined to oxygen by a double bond, is an electrophilic centre too, because the more electronegative atom draws electron density away from it.

The carbon of 2-chloropropane that carries the chlorine is such a centre. The bond is polar, with the carbon at $\delta^{+}$, and the nucleophile attacks there, not at the methyl groups. The lib finds it from the bond polarities alone.

## (c) Electron movement in organic reactions

Chemists show the movement of electrons with curved arrows. A curved arrow starts where an electron pair is, a lone pair on an atom or a bond, and it ends where that pair goes. It does not show the atoms moving; it shows the bonding changing. Three basic shifts appear in this unit: a pair moves from a $\pi$ bond to the adjacent bond position, from a $\pi$ bond to an adjacent atom, or from a lone pair on an atom to the adjacent bond position.

Two kinds of arrowhead carry the difference between the species. A **full head** moves a pair of electrons, two electrons together, and it is used for heterolysis and for an attack by a lone pair. A **half head**, also called a fish hook, moves a single electron, and it is used for each electron in homolysis. Homolysis needs two half heads, one to each atom of the bond, because each atom gets one electron.

<KeyIdeas>
- Heterolysis gives the bond's pair to one atom and makes ions: a carbocation, with an empty $p$ orbital and $sp^2$ hybridisation, or a carbanion, with a lone pair and $sp^3$ hybridisation.
- Homolysis shares the pair and makes radicals, each with one unpaired electron. It needs heat or light.
- Carbocations and radicals are most stable at the highest degree: $3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ >$ methyl, from alkyl groups (+I) and α-hydrogens (hyperconjugation). Carbanions run in the reverse order.
- A nucleophile brings an electron pair to an electron-poor site, the electrophile; the substrate is the molecule whose carbon we follow.
- A curved arrow starts at a pair of electrons and ends where it goes: a full head for a pair, a half head for one electron.
</KeyIdeas>
