Chemists use the same quiet change to clean a compound, and the same logic runs through every method in this Section.

## Why purify, and how do you know?

A compound taken from a plant or made in a flask is rarely alone. There may be unused starting material, a side product, some solvent, or a coloured speck that does no harm to the reaction but spoils the look of the sample. Each purification method sorts the compound from its companions by one physical difference: how readily it dissolves, how easily it vaporises, or how it shares itself between two liquids that do not mix.

How do you know the job is done? A pure compound melts or boils sharply, over a narrow range of temperature. A sample that melts over a wide range is usually still a mixture, so the melting or boiling point is the first test you run after each purification. Chromatography, the fifth technique, follows later in this Section.

## (a) Sublimation

Some solids pass directly into the vapour state when heated, without melting. Sublimation uses this to separate a sublimable compound from a non-sublimable impurity. The mixture is heated gently under a cold surface, the sublimable compound rises as vapour and condenses on that surface as crystals, and the impurity is left behind.

Sublimation is quick and needs no solvent, but it works only when the compound has a useful vapour pressure at a temperature below its decomposition point. Most organic compounds do not sublime, so the method is reserved for the few that do.

## (b) Crystallisation

Crystallisation is the most used method for purifying a solid organic compound. It rests on a difference in solubility. The impure solid is dissolved in a solvent in which it is sparingly soluble at room temperature but appreciably soluble when hot. The solution is concentrated until it is nearly saturated, then cooled. As it cools, the pure compound crystallises out and is removed by filtration, while the impurities and a small amount of the compound stay in the filtrate, the mother liquor.

Choosing the solvent is the hard part. A solvent that dissolves the compound very well at all temperatures gives poor recovery, and one that dissolves almost nothing gives no solution at all. When the compound is very soluble in one solvent and nearly insoluble in another, the two miscible solvents can be mixed to set the solubility just right.

Coloured impurities are often removed by boiling the hot solution with a little activated charcoal. The charcoal adsorbs the coloured molecules on its large surface, and the solution is filtered hot to remove it. If the impurities have solubilities close to that of the compound, one crystallisation is not enough, and the process is repeated until the melting point becomes sharp.

## (c) Distillation

Distillation separates a volatile liquid from non-volatile impurities, and two liquids whose boiling points differ enough. Here the working pair is benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, and toluene, $\ce{C7H8}$. The purification lib gives their normal boiling points from Antoine constants: benzene boils at 80.1 °C and toluene at 110.6 °C. A 1 : 1 mole mixture boils at 92.1 °C, and its vapour is 71.4 % benzene. A single boil therefore enriches the vapour, but not nearly enough for a clean product.

**Simple distillation** is one boil and one condensation. The vapour leaving the flask is collected in a condenser and a receiver, and the liquid comes over at its boiling point. It is good for removing a non-volatile residue, but when the boiling points are close the vapours of both liquids come over together.

**Fractional distillation** puts a column between the flask and the condenser. The column is packed with glass beads or fitted with bubble plates, so the rising vapour meets descending liquid many times. Each meeting is an equilibrium stage, which is called a theoretical plate. A column of length 60 cm with a plate height of 4 cm has $60 / 4 = 15$ theoretical plates, and each extra plate sharpens the separation.

The thermometer sits at the head of the column, so it reads the vapour that reaches the condenser, not the liquid in the flask. With 12 plates (60 cm, with an assumed plate height of 5 cm in the lab below), the head reads 80.1 °C while the distillate is essentially pure benzene. The flask, at 92.1 °C for an equimolar mixture, is hotter. When the benzene in the flask runs out, the head climbs to about 110 °C, and the toluene that follows is collected separately. **Distillation under reduced pressure** is for liquids that decompose before they reach their normal boiling point, or that boil far above it. Lowering the pressure with a water pump or vacuum pump lowers the temperature at which the vapour pressure matches the surroundings. The Clausius–Clapeyron relation estimates that temperature. For water, with a standard enthalpy of vaporisation of 40.79 kJ mol⁻¹ from NCERT Table 5.1, the lib gives 323.3 K (50.1 °C) at 100 mmHg, and 292.3 K (19.1 °C) at 20 mmHg. The relation assumes a constant enthalpy, so treat the result as an estimate.

**Steam distillation** handles a compound that is steam volatile and immiscible with water. In the flask, the organic liquid boils when its own vapour pressure $p_1$ plus the vapour pressure of water $p_2$ equals the atmospheric pressure $p$, so $p = p_1 + p_2$. Because each partial pressure is less than $p$, the mixture boils below the boiling point of water, and the organic compound is carried over at a temperature well below its own boiling point. For toluene and water the lib finds 357.2 K (84.1 °C). At that temperature toluene supplies 335 mmHg of the vapour pressure, so about 44 % of the vapour by mole is toluene. The distillate separates into two layers.

Steam distillation is traditionally how attar, the rose oil of Kannauj, is made: the petals are heated with water, and the oil carried over with the steam is collected and separated. Only the principle matters here. Steam is generated separately, passed into the flask, and the distillate goes to a separating funnel.

## (d) Differential extraction

When an organic compound is in an aqueous solution, it can be removed by shaking the solution with an organic solvent that does not mix with water. The two layers separate in a separating funnel, and the solute shares itself between them in a fixed ratio. The distribution (partition) coefficient $K = [\text{solute}]_\text{org} / [\text{solute}]_\text{aq}$ is the measure of that share. The organic solvent is later removed by distillation or evaporation, which leaves the compound behind.

Splitting the solvent is cheaper than using it all at once. Each extraction leaves $V_\text{aq} / (V_\text{aq} + K V_\text{org})$ of the solute in the water. With $K = 5$, a 100 mL aqueous solution extracted once with 50 mL of ether leaves $100 / (100 + 250) = 0.286$, or 28.6 %, of the solute behind. Split the same 50 mL into two portions of 25 mL, and the solute left falls to 19.8 %. Each fresh portion takes a fresh share of the solute, so repeated small extractions beat one large one.

When the compound is only slightly more soluble in the solvent, even many fresh portions use a lot of solvent. In such a case continuous extraction is used: the same solvent is boiled, its vapour condenses, and it drips through the solution again and again, so a small volume does the job.

<KeyIdeas>
- Each method exploits one difference: solubility (crystallisation), vapour pressure (sublimation, distillation), or partition between two liquids (extraction).
- A pure compound has a sharp melting or boiling point, and that is the check to run after any purification.
- In fractional distillation each theoretical plate is one more equilibrium stage, so a taller column gives a purer distillate.
- Steam distillation works because $p = p_1 + p_2$, so the mixture boils below 373 K, and the organic liquid comes over below its own boiling point.
- For extraction with partition coefficient $K$, several small portions of solvent remove more solute than one large portion of the same total volume.
</KeyIdeas>
