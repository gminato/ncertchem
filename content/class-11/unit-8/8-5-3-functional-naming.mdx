## The functional group sets the name

Most carbon compounds carry a functional group: an atom or a small cluster of atoms, bonded in a fixed way, that is the part of the molecule where it reacts. Molecules that share a group tend to behave alike, so chemists sort them into classes, and each class has a prefix and a suffix. A suffix is used when the group is the one that names the whole compound. A prefix is used when the group is a side group hanging off a parent chain.

Some names are used in daily life beside the systematic one. Ethanoic acid is also called acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, and the name is still in common use. The table gives the systematic name in each row.

## The principal group chooses the suffix

A molecule can hold more than one group. One of them is then chosen as the principal functional group. It names the parent and gives the suffix, and every other group is written as a prefix. The choice follows a fixed order of priority. From the highest rank down, the order is $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-SO3H}$ (sulphonic acid), $\ce{-COOR}$, $\ce{-COCl}$, $\ce{-CONH2}$, $\ce{-CN}$, $\ce{-CHO}$, $>\ce{C=O}$, $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-NH2}$, $>\ce{C=C<}$ and $\ce{-C#C-}$. The first group in the list that the molecule holds is the principal one.

Two rules follow from the list. A halogen, a nitro group, an alkyl group or an alkoxy group is always a prefix, whatever else the molecule holds. And a ketone outranks an alcohol, so a molecule with both is named as a hydroxy ketone. The Scrolly takes one molecule from the top of the list to its end, and then turns to the benzene ring.

<Step>
Here is a second molecule, with an alcohol and a ketone on a five-carbon chain. The ketone outranks the alcohol, so the ketone takes the suffix $-$one. The $-$OH is now a prefix, hydroxy-. The chain is numbered from the end that gives the ketone the lower locant, which is C2 rather than C4. The double bond then falls between C3 and C4, so the name is 5-hydroxypent-3-en-2-one.
</Step>
<Step>
When a benzene ring is the parent, its carbons are numbered 1 to 6 round the ring, and the substituents are prefixes. Two substituents on neighbouring carbons are at positions 1 and 2, which is called ortho, written $o$-. Positions 1 and 3 are meta, written $m$-, and positions 1 and 4 are para, written $p$-. Each row here holds a chlorine and a methyl group, and only the spacing between them changes.
</Step>
<Step>
When two numbering choices give the same set of locants, the substituent that comes first in the alphabet takes the lower number. Here both directions give the chlorine and the methyl group the locants 1 and 3. Chloro comes before methyl, so the chlorine carbon is C1. The name is 1-chloro-3-methylbenzene, and the other direction would have put the methyl group on C1, which is not the name used.
</Step>

## Parent chains, numbering and multiple bonds

The parent chain is the longest chain of carbons that holds the principal group. If the chain can run in two directions, the number goes to the end that gives the principal group the lower locant. The multiple bonds come next: a double bond takes the lower locant among the choices that remain, and the substituents come last. Locants are written before the group they refer to, and a hyphen joins them to the name.

When a molecule has two or more double bonds, the alkane ending changes to a suffix that counts them. Four carbons with two double bonds at positions 1 and 3 give buta-1,3-diene, which is made from butane. A chain with both a double bond and a triple bond takes both endings, as in but-1-en-3-yne. When a principal group is present, the multiple bonds are written as infixes instead. In penta-2,4-dien-1-ol the alcohol takes the suffix, and the two double bonds sit inside the name.

## Benzene rings as parents

A benzene ring carries no principal group of its own, so with substituents it is named as the parent, benzene, and each substituent is written before it. Substituents go in alphabetical order, ignoring any di-, tri- or tetra- in front of them. When a ring holds a hydroxy, an amino or a methoxy group, the three retained names phenol, aniline and anisole can be used as the parent, with the carbon that carries the group numbered 1. The common names toluene, anisole and aniline are still used for methylbenzene, methoxybenzene and aminobenzene. The group $\ce{C6H5-}$, when it hangs off a chain, is called phenyl and is written Ph.

For a ring with two substituents, the locants are 1,2, 1,3 or 1,4, and the words ortho, meta and para can replace them. The name of 4-chlorophenol uses the locant 4, with the hydroxy carbon at position 1, and is the para isomer. With three or more substituents the words ortho, meta and para are not used, and the locants alone show where each group sits. The last Check asks you to read the locants off a ring.

<KeyIdeas>
- The principal group is the highest one in NCERT's order of priority, and it sets the suffix. Every other group is a prefix, and the halogens, the nitro group and the alkoxy group are always prefixes.
- The parent chain holds the principal group. It is numbered from the end that gives the principal group the lower locant, then the multiple bonds, then the substituents.
- A ketone outranks an alcohol: a molecule with both is a hydroxy ketone, and the ketone takes the suffix.
- On a benzene ring, positions 1,2, 1,3 and 1,4 are ortho, meta and para. With three or more substituents, locants alone are used.
- When two numberings tie, the substituent that comes first in the alphabet takes the lower locant.
</KeyIdeas>
