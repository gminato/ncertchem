<Hook>
An old television tube lights up when you switch it on: a bright spot sweeps across the glass, and it is steered by magnets on the neck of the tube. Something invisible is travelling from the back of the tube to the front. What is it made of, and how could anyone weigh it?
</Hook>

Dalton treated the atom as indivisible. The experiments of the late nineteenth century showed otherwise. The first clue came from electricity passing through gases. Keep one rule in mind as you read: **like charges repel and unlike charges attract**.

**2.1.1 The electron.** Seal two metal electrodes into a glass tube, pump out most of the gas, and apply a high voltage. The first thing you see is a stream of particles leaving the negative electrode, the cathode, and travelling to the positive electrode, the anode. The animation below follows that beam.

<Scrolly animation="subatomic-cathode-ray">
<Step>
Seal two electrodes in a glass tube, pump the gas down to a low pressure and apply a high voltage. A stream of particles leaves the **cathode** and travels towards the anode. The beam is invisible in flight, but it makes the glass glow where it strikes. With no field switched on, the rays travel in **straight lines**.
</Step>
<Step>
Now place a pair of charged plates across the beam. The beam bends **towards the positive plate**. That is what a stream of negative charges should do, because unlike charges attract. The stronger the field, the larger the bend. The rays behave like negatively charged particles, and we call them **electrons**.
</Step>
<Step>
Switch the electric field off and turn on a magnetic field, pointing into the page. The beam bends the **other way**. A magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity, so the path curves into a circle of radius $r = mv/(eB)$. For the values used here, that radius is about 4 cm.
</Step>
<Step>
Turn both fields on, and choose their strengths so that the electric force and the magnetic force on each electron are equal and opposite. The beam is straight again, and the balance gives $v = E/B$. The radius measured in the magnetic field alone then gives Thomson's ratio:

$$
\frac{e}{m_\text{e}} = 1.758820 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}
$$
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Thomson made his measurement in 1897. The rays behaved the same whatever gas was in the tube and whatever metal the electrodes were made of. That observation led to the conclusion that electrons are a basic constituent of all atoms. Because the ratio is so large, the electron is very light for the charge it carries.

**2.1.2 The charge on the electron.** Thomson gave the ratio, but not the charge on its own. R. A. Millikan's oil-drop experiment (1906–1914) did that. Fine drops of oil were sprayed between two plates. X-rays ionised the air around them, and the drops picked up charge by colliding with the ions. An electric field could then hold a drop still, or move it up or down.

<Scrolly animation="subatomic-oil-drop">
<Step>
Millikan sprayed a mist of oil drops into the gap between two horizontal plates, through a tiny hole in the upper one. Each drop falls steadily under its **weight**, $mg$. By measuring how fast a drop falls, Millikan could work out its mass.
</Step>
<Step>
X-rays passing through the air strip electrons from the air molecules, which leaves positive ions behind. A drop that collides with these ions picks up **charge**, sometimes one unit and sometimes several. Now the field between the plates can act on the drop as well as gravity, so its motion depends on that charge.
</Step>
<Step>
Switch on the voltage. The upper plate is positive, so a negatively charged drop is pulled **upward**. When the electric force $qE$ is bigger than the weight, the drop rises. Change the field and the drop's motion changes with it. Watching the motion tells you the force, and the force tells you the charge.
</Step>
<Step>
Adjust the voltage until $qE = mg$, and the drop **hangs still**. For a drop of mass $3.0 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{kg}$ carrying three extra charges, the plates need about 612 V across the 1.0 cm gap. Because the drop does not move, the two forces are exactly equal, and the balancing voltage fixes the charge.
</Step>
<Step>
Repeat the measurement with many drops. Every charge is a whole multiple of one value: $q = ne$, with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$. The histogram uses simulated measurements to show the pattern. It is not Millikan's raw data. The fixed value is $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

Millikan's result, $e = 1.602176 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, combined with Thomson's ratio gives the electron mass of equation 2.2.

**2.1.3 Protons and neutrons.** A modified cathode-ray tube produced a second kind of ray, the canal rays, which carry positive charge. Their mass and charge-to-mass ratio depend on the gas in the tube, because they are positive ions of that gas. The lightest of them, from hydrogen, was called the **proton**. Chadwick later found the neutral **neutron** by bombarding beryllium with α particles. The table tracks the three fundamental particles.

<Scrolly animation="subatomic-particles">
<Step>
The **electron** carries a charge of $-1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, and its mass is about $9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$. Its charge-to-mass ratio is the very large value that Thomson measured. Every electron carries this same charge, and the electron's mass is so small that it contributes almost nothing to the total mass of an atom.
</Step>
<Step>
The **proton** carries an equal and opposite charge, $+1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. It is the lightest positive ion, obtained from hydrogen gas, and was characterised in 1919. Its mass is nearly 1836 times that of the electron. The proton's charge is the same size as the electron's but opposite in sign, which is why a neutral atom has equal numbers of each.
</Step>
<Step>
The **neutron** has no charge, and its mass is a little more than the proton's. Chadwick found it in 1932, when beryllium emitted neutral particles under bombardment by α particles. Being neutral, it is not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Cathode rays are streams of electrons: they travel in straight lines, bend in electric and magnetic fields, and do not depend on the gas or metal used.
- Thomson's ratio is $e/m_\text{e} = 1.758820 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$, which makes the electron very light for its charge.
- Millikan found that every drop charge is a whole number $n$ of the fundamental charge $e = 1.602176 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.
- Canal rays and Chadwick's experiment completed the picture: protons carry $+e$, and neutrons carry no charge but have a mass a little above the proton's.
</KeyIdeas>
