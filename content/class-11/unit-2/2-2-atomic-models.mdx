<Hook>
Gold varak on a barfi is beaten so thin that it is almost see-through in good light. Suppose you fired tiny, fast particles through a sheet that thin. Would they all go straight through, or would some be knocked off course? Most people guess the first. The answer changed what we think an atom is.
</Hook>

Once electrons were known to be negative and light, the question was how the rest of the atom is arranged. An atom has no net charge, so its positive charge has to be somewhere. The two models below answered that question in turn.

**2.2.1 Thomson's model.** In 1898 J. J. Thomson proposed an atom that is a sphere of radius about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$, with its positive charge spread evenly through it. The electrons sit inside like plums in a pudding, arranged so that the whole thing is electrically stable. The mass of the atom is also spread evenly. The model explained why atoms are neutral, but later experiments did not agree with it.

**2.2.2 Rutherford's nuclear model.** Ernest Rutherford, with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, fired a beam of α particles at a thin gold foil about 100 nm thick. α particles are helium nuclei, each carrying two units of positive charge. A ring of zinc sulphide around the foil flashed wherever a particle struck it. The result was not what Thomson's model predicted.

<Scrolly animation="atomic-rutherford">
<Step>
A source sends a beam of α particles at a thin gold foil, and a ring of zinc sulphide surrounds the foil. Each α particle that hits the ring makes a tiny flash of light, so every flash marks where one particle went. Thomson's model would let the particles slow down and bend only a little as they pass through the foil.
</Step>
<Step>
Most α particles pass through the foil with **almost no deflection**. Their flashes land where they would if the foil were not there. So most of an atom must be empty space, with nothing to push a fast particle off course.
</Step>
<Step>
A small fraction of particles is **deflected** through small angles. Something positive and strong must be repelling them. The positive charge cannot be spread through the atom as Thomson assumed. It has to be concentrated in a very small region.
</Step>
<Step>
Very few particles, roughly 1 in 20 000, are **turned back** through nearly 180°. Only a very dense, positive core can reverse a fast α particle. Rutherford called it the **nucleus**. The atom has a radius of about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$, while the nucleus is only about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Zoom in on one nucleus. The path depends on the **impact parameter**, the distance by which the particle would miss the nucleus if there were no repulsion. A distant particle is pushed gently. A closer one is pushed harder and turns through a larger angle. A particle aimed almost straight at the nucleus is thrown back.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

The paths in the picture come from a simple Coulomb-repulsion calculation: for a repulsive force $\propto 1/r^2$, the deflection satisfies $\tan(\theta/2) = b_0/b$. The nucleus is drawn much larger than to scale, so that its paths can be seen. The real ratio is far more extreme.

**2.2.3 Atomic number and mass number.** The number of protons in the nucleus is the **atomic number** $Z$, and in a neutral atom it also equals the number of electrons. The **mass number** $A$ counts all the nucleons, so $A = Z + n$, where $n$ is the number of neutrons. A species is written as ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, with $A$ above and $Z$ below the symbol. For example, ${}^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}$ has 26 protons, 26 electrons when neutral, and $56 - 26 = 30$ neutrons.

**2.2.4 Isotopes and isobars.** Atoms with the same $Z$ but different $A$ are **isotopes**. They differ only in their neutron number. Hydrogen has three of them: protium, ${}^{1}_{1}\text{H}$, which makes up about 99.985 % of hydrogen; deuterium, ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H}$, at about 0.015 %; and tritium, ${}^{3}_{1}\text{H}$, which is found only in trace amounts. Atoms with the same $A$ but different $Z$ are **isobars**. The chemistry follows the electrons, which are set by $Z$, so isotopes behave alike in reactions.

<Scrolly animation="atomic-isotopes">
<Step>
**Protium**, ${}^{1}_{1}\text{H}$, has one proton and no neutron. Its nucleus is just that proton, with one electron around it. Most hydrogen atoms are this isotope, about 99.985 % of them, so the hydrogen we meet every day is mostly protium.
</Step>
<Step>
**Deuterium**, ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H}$, adds one neutron. The mass number rises to 2, but the atomic number stays at 1, so the atom still has one proton and one electron. Its chemistry is the same as protium's, though it is about twice as heavy.
</Step>
<Step>
**Tritium**, ${}^{3}_{1}\text{H}$, has two neutrons. It is still hydrogen, because it has one proton and one electron. It occurs only in trace amounts, so it is rare even though its nucleus is the heaviest of the three.
</Step>
<Step>
**Isobars** have the same mass number. Argon-40 and calcium-40 each have $A = 40$. Argon has 18 protons and 22 neutrons, while calcium has 20 protons and 20 neutrons. They have the same number of nucleons but different atomic numbers, so they are different elements.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

**2.2.5 Drawbacks of the Rutherford model.** Rutherford's atom is a small solar system, with a heavy nucleus at the centre and light electrons circling it. The comparison is useful but it breaks down. An electron moving in a circle is accelerating. Under classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charge radiates energy, so the electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. The estimated time for that collapse is about $10^{-8}\ \text{s}$, yet atoms are stable. The model also says nothing about how the electrons are arranged around the nucleus or what energies they have.

<Scrolly animation="atomic-collapse">
<Step>
In the classical picture, the electron circles the nucleus at a steady radius. The circle is stable only if nothing takes energy away from the electron. The model does not say what sets that radius, so the orbit is simply assumed.
</Step>
<Step>
An electron moving in a circle is accelerating, and Maxwell's theory says that an accelerating charge radiates electromagnetic waves. Here the waves are drawn as shells expanding from the nucleus. The energy they carry must come from the electron's motion, so the electron has less energy to stay in its orbit.
</Step>
<Step>
As energy is lost, the orbit shrinks and the electron follows an inward spiral. Each turn is smaller than the last, and the electron moves faster as it nears the nucleus. The picture is qualitative: it shows the claim, not a computed orbit.
</Step>
<Step>
The classical estimate says the electron would reach the nucleus within about $10^{-8}\ \text{s}$. Real atoms do not collapse, so the model is incomplete. It needs a new rule for how electrons can move around a nucleus, which Bohr proposed in 1913.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Most α particles pass through a thin gold foil almost undeflected, so an atom is mostly empty space.
- A few α particles are deflected and very few, roughly 1 in 20 000, are turned back. The positive charge and most of the mass sit in a nucleus about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ across, inside an atom of radius about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$.
- The atomic number $Z$ is the number of protons, and the mass number is $A = Z + n$: ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$.
- Isotopes share $Z$ and differ in $n$, so they have the same chemistry. Isobars share $A$ and differ in $Z$.
- Rutherford's model cannot explain stability: an accelerating electron should radiate and spiral into the nucleus in about $10^{-8}\ \text{s}$.
</KeyIdeas>
