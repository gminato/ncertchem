<Hook>
Pass an electric discharge through hydrogen gas and it glows a soft pinkish red. Send that glow through a prism and you don't get a rainbow: you get a few sharp, separate lines of colour. Why only those colours?
</Hook>

<Scrolly animation="bohr-emission">
<Step>
Bohr's picture starts with a hydrogen atom in its **ground state**: one electron in the lowest allowed orbit, $n = 1$. In Bohr's model the electron may only occupy certain orbits, and each one has a fixed energy. Nothing in between is allowed.
</Step>
<Step>
Supply energy, with heat or an electric discharge, and the electron can absorb exactly enough of it to jump to a higher orbit, here $n = 3$. The atom is now in an **excited state**.
</Step>
<Step>
Excited states don't last. The electron falls to a lower orbit, $n = 2$, and the energy difference leaves the atom as a single **photon**. Its frequency is fixed by the gap:

$$
\Delta E = E_3 - E_2 = h\nu = 3.03 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$
</Step>
<Step>
That photon has a wavelength of $656\ \text{nm}$: red light. Every hydrogen atom making the same jump emits exactly the same colour, so the spectrum shows a sharp **line**, not a smear. Drops that end on $n = 2$ produce the visible lines of the **Balmer series**.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- In Bohr's model the electron occupies only certain orbits, each with a fixed energy $E_n = -R_\text{H}\,\dfrac{1}{n^2}$, where $R_\text{H} = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$.
- Absorbing energy moves the electron to a higher orbit; falling back releases a photon.
- The photon's energy equals the gap between the orbits: $\Delta E = h\nu$.
- Fixed gaps mean fixed wavelengths, and fixed wavelengths mean a **line spectrum**.
</KeyIdeas>
