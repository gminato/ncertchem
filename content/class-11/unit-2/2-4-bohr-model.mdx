</Hook>

Bohr's picture starts with a hydrogen atom in its **ground state**: one electron in the lowest allowed orbit, $n = 1$. In Bohr's model the electron may only occupy certain orbits, and each one has a fixed energy. Nothing in between is allowed.
</Step>
<Step>
Supply energy, with heat or an electric discharge, and the electron can absorb exactly enough of it to jump to a higher orbit, here $n = 3$. The atom is now in an **excited state**.
</Step>
<Step>
Excited states don't last. The electron falls to a lower orbit, $n = 2$, and the energy difference leaves the atom as a single **photon**. Its frequency is fixed by the gap:

$$
\Delta E = E_3 - E_2 = h\nu = 3.03 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$
</Step>
<Step>
That photon has a wavelength of $656\ \text{nm}$: red light. Every hydrogen atom making the same jump emits exactly the same colour, so the spectrum shows a sharp **line**, not a smear. Drops that end on $n = 2$ produce the visible lines of the **Balmer series**.
</Step>

## Bohr's postulates

Bohr built his model on four assumptions, and each one was a break from classical physics. A classical electron circling a nucleus should radiate continuously and spiral inwards. Bohr said it does not, and he replaced that picture with a small set of rules.

1. **Stationary states.** The electron moves in circular orbits around the nucleus without radiating. These orbits are the **stationary states**, and they are numbered by the **principal quantum number** $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$
2. **Quantised angular momentum.** Only those orbits are allowed in which the angular momentum is a whole-number multiple of $h/2\pi$:

$$
m_\text{e}\, v\, r = n\,\frac{h}{2\pi}
$$

Here $m_\text{e}$ is the mass of the electron, $v$ its speed and $r$ the radius of its orbit.

3. **Energy is exchanged in packets.** An electron changes orbit only by absorbing or emitting radiation. The energy gap between two orbits, $E_\text{f} - E_\text{i}$, is carried by a photon of frequency $\nu$, and this is Bohr's frequency rule:

$$
\Delta E = h\nu
$$

4. **A stable ladder of energies.** Each allowed orbit has a definite energy, and the electron in the lowest one, $n = 1$, is in the most stable state.

## Radii and energies of the orbits

Combining the quantisation rule with the balance of electrical and centrifugal forces gives two results for hydrogen. The radius of the $n$-th orbit grows as $n^2$:

$$
r_n = n^2\,a_0, \qquad a_0 = 52.9\ \text{pm}
$$

The first orbit, $n = 1$, has radius $a_0 = 52.9\ \text{pm}$, the **Bohr radius**, and the second has four times that radius, $211.6\ \text{pm}$. The energy of the same orbit is

$$
E_n = -R_\text{H}\,\frac{1}{n^2}, \qquad R_\text{H} = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}
$$

The energies are negative because they are measured from a free electron at rest, which has zero energy. The more negative the energy, the more tightly the electron is bound. The ground state is $E_1 = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$, which is $-13.6\ \text{eV}$. The state $n = 2$ has one quarter of that magnitude: $E_2 = -5.45 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, or $-3.40\ \text{eV}$.

Two consequences follow. Energy rises towards zero as $n$ increases, so the gaps between neighbouring orbits shrink. And the orbits spread outwards, so an excited electron sits farther from the nucleus.

### Hydrogen-like ions

Any species with one electron, such as $\ce{He+}$ or $\ce{Li^2+}$, behaves in the same way with a nucleus of charge $Z$. Only two expressions change:

$$
E_n = -R_\text{H}\,\frac{Z^2}{n^2}, \qquad r_n = \frac{52.9\ n^2}{Z}\ \text{pm}
$$

For $\ce{He+}$, with $Z = 2$, the first orbit has radius $r_1 = 52.9 / 2 = 26.45\ \text{pm}$, half the hydrogen value, and its energy is four times as negative: $E_1 = -8.72 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$. A larger nuclear charge pulls the electron closer and holds it more tightly.

## 2.4.1 Explanation of the line spectrum

The line spectrum of hydrogen, which you met in Section 2.3, is a direct consequence of the rules above. An electron can only make a jump between two allowed orbits, so only certain energy gaps are possible, and each gap gives one photon of one wavelength. Absorption happens when the electron jumps up, from a lower $n_\text{i}$ to a higher $n_\text{f}$; emission happens when it falls back down.

Substituting the energies into $\Delta E = E_\text{f} - E_\text{i}$ gives

$$
\Delta E = R_\text{H}\left(\frac{1}{n_\text{i}^2} - \frac{1}{n_\text{f}^2}\right)
$$

where the sign of $\Delta E$ tells you the direction. Dividing by $hc$ turns this into the wavenumber, $\bar{\nu} = 1/\lambda$: $\bar{\nu} = 1.09677 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_\text{i}^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_\text{f}^2}\right)$. This is the Rydberg formula. Rydberg had found it empirically, from the measured wavelengths alone. Bohr's model explains why it works.

Each series of lines is a family of transitions that share the same final level, $n_\text{f}$. Transitions ending on $n_\text{f} = 1$ form the **Lyman** series in the ultraviolet, those ending on $n_\text{f} = 2$ form the **Balmer** series, which includes the visible lines, and those ending on $n_\text{f} = 3$ form the **Paschen** series in the infrared. The brightness of a line depends on how many photons of that wavelength are emitted or absorbed.

## 2.4.2 Limitations of Bohr's model

Bohr's model was a real advance over Rutherford's nuclear model. It accounted for the stability of the atom and for the line spectrum of hydrogen and of hydrogen-like ions such as $\ce{He+}$, $\ce{Li^2+}$ and $\ce{Be^3+}$. It was, however, too simple, and it fails in four ways.

1. **Fine structure.** Sophisticated spectroscopes show that some hydrogen lines are doublets, two lines very close together. The model predicts a single line in each case.
2. **Other atoms.** The model cannot describe any atom with more than one electron, such as helium, which has two. Once the electrons repel each other, the simple orbit picture breaks down.
3. **Electric and magnetic fields.** Placing the atom in a magnetic field splits its lines (the Zeeman effect), and an electric field also splits them (the Stark effect). Bohr's theory cannot explain either.
4. **Chemical bonds.** The model says nothing about how atoms combine to form molecules through chemical bonds.

<KeyIdeas>
- In Bohr's model the electron occupies only certain orbits, each with a fixed energy $E_n = -R_\text{H}\,\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}$, where $R_\text{H} = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$.
- Absorbing energy moves the electron to a higher orbit; falling back releases a photon.
- The photon's energy equals the gap between the orbits: $\Delta E = h\nu$.
- Fixed gaps mean fixed wavelengths, and fixed wavelengths mean a **line spectrum**.
- The orbit radius is $r_n = 52.9\,n^2/Z$ pm, so $\ce{He+}$ has its first orbit at $26.45$ pm, and the model fails for atoms with more than one electron.
</KeyIdeas>
