<Hook>
Ethane is a gas that comes out of natural gas wells along with methane, and a cylinder of it is labelled as one substance. Yet each molecule in that cylinder is always twisting about its carbon–carbon bond, so its shape keeps changing. If the molecule keeps changing shape, why does the cylinder still hold one compound?
</Hook>

## Free rotation about the C–C bond

The bond between two carbon atoms in an alkane is a σ bond, made by the head-on overlap of two $\mathrm{sp^3}$ orbitals. Its electron density is symmetrical around the line joining the two nuclei. Turning one carbon about that line does not spoil the overlap, so a C–C single bond allows rotation.

Each arrangement of the atoms that can be turned into another by rotation about a C–C single bond is a **conformation**, also called a conformer or a rotamer. Because the chain can turn through any angle, an alkane can take an endless number of conformations. Rotation is not entirely free, though. The bonds on neighbouring carbons repel one another a little as they pass, and that resistance is called **torsional strain**.

## Conformations of ethane

Ethane has one C–C bond, and each carbon is bonded to three hydrogens. Keep one carbon fixed and turn the other about the C–C axis. The hydrogens of the two carbons then take up an infinite number of relative positions. Each of these is a conformer, and the infinite family has two extreme members.

In the **eclipsed** conformation, the hydrogens of the two carbons are as close together as they can be, with each back hydrogen directly behind a front one. In the **staggered** conformation, they are as far apart as possible. Every other angle between these two is a **skew** conformation. Whatever the angle, the C–C and C–H bond lengths and the bond angles stay the same, so only the relative positions of the atoms change.

Chemists draw the two extreme forms in two ways. The sawhorse projection shows the molecule from the side, and the Newman projection shows it looking straight down the bond. In a Newman drawing the front carbon is a point with three bonds, and the back carbon is a circle with three shorter bonds from its rim.

## Torsional strain and the preferred form

The dihedral angle $\theta$ fixes the conformer, and the strain depends on $\theta$. When the hydrogens are staggered, the electron clouds of the C–H bonds are as far apart as they can be, so the repulsion between them is least. Turning towards the eclipsed form brings those clouds closer, the repulsion grows, and the molecule must carry more energy. That repulsion between bonds on neighbouring carbons is the torsional strain.

So the staggered form has the least torsional strain and the minimum energy, while the eclipsed form has the most. The staggered form is therefore the more stable one, and an ethane sample spends most of its time in it. NCERT gives the energy difference between the two extreme forms of ethane as of the order of 12.5 kJ mol⁻¹. This Section uses the simple model

$$
E(\theta) = \frac{12.5}{2}\left(1 + \cos 3\theta\right)\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

which is zero at the staggered 60° and 12.5 kJ mol⁻¹ at the eclipsed 0°. It is a picture of the curve, not a measured one.

## Why the conformers cannot be separated

Conformers are not separable isomers. Every conformer of ethane turns into every other by rotation about the C–C bond, and at room temperature that rotation is almost free. A molecule in the eclipsed form therefore passes through the staggered form, and back, very many times a second. The interconversion is so fast at room temperature that no sample can be held in one conformer long enough to isolate it.

Compare this with a true isomer such as 1,2-dichloroethene, where the two forms differ in how groups are attached to the double bond and rotation about that bond is blocked. Those isomers can be separated. Conformers of an alkane cannot, and that is why a cylinder of ethane is one compound.

<KeyIdeas>
- Free rotation about a C–C σ bond gives conformations, called conformers or rotamers. Their number is infinite, and they interconvert, so they cannot be isolated as separate compounds.
- In ethane the eclipsed form is at 0° and 120°, the staggered form at 60° and 180°, and every other angle is skew.
- Torsional strain is the repulsion between bonds on neighbouring carbons. It is largest in the eclipsed form and least in the staggered form, which is therefore the preferred form.
- The barrier to rotation in ethane is about 12.5 kJ mol⁻¹, so rotation is almost free at room temperature.
- Sawhorse and Newman projections draw the same conformer. Bond lengths and bond angles stay the same in all of them.
</KeyIdeas>
