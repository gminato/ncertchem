<Hook>
A dye maker takes phenol and adds a nitro group. The new group lands on the carbon next to the OH or on the one across from it, never on the carbon in between. Take nitrobenzene and add another nitro group, and it lands two carbons away from the first. The ring is the same in both cases. What on the ring decides where the next group goes?
</Hook>

## Two kinds of answer

Put one group on a benzene ring and you have a monosubstituted benzene. Attack it again and the ring has six carbons that could take the new group, but not all six are used equally. Of the five carbons that are not bonded to the first group, two are ortho (next to it), two are meta (one carbon further away) and one is para (opposite it). Experiment shows that the first group picks one of two patterns. Some groups send the new group to the ortho and para carbons together. Others send it to the meta carbon.

Chemists call this the directive influence of the group on the ring. The pattern depends on the group already present, not on the group that is arriving. The Scrolly follows the argument in the order it is usually built: a donor, a withdrawing group, the intermediate formed by an attack, the extra form that only some attacks get, and the table of groups that NCERT lists.

The hybrid carries δ− on the two ortho carbons and the para carbon, and never on a meta carbon.

<Step>
Now nitrobenzene, $\ce{C6H5NO2}$, where the ring is the donor. Its electrons flow out into the nitro group, which takes them on its nitrogen and oxygen. The ring is left short of electrons at the ortho and para carbons, which are δ+ in the hybrid. The meta carbons keep more of their electron density than those positions do, so they are the carbons that an electrophile reaches most readily.
</Step>
<Step>
Attack phenol with the nitronium ion, $\ce{NO2+}$. The attacked carbon becomes sp³ and carries the new group and its hydrogen, so the ring loses its aromatic character. The positive charge is spread over the other five carbons, and in every σ-complex it sits on carbons ortho or para to the attacked one. The three drawings are the ortho, meta and para attacks, placed side by side.
</Step>
<Step>
For an ortho or a para attack, the + can land on the carbon that carries OH. There the oxygen's lone pair can share the charge and form $\ce{C=O+H}$: an extra form that the meta attack does not have, because its + never reaches the carbon with the OH. This extra form is why the OH group favours the ortho and para positions.
</Step>
<Step>
The table lists NCERT's groups. The ring-activating groups, $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-NH2}$, $\ce{-NHR}$, $\ce{-NHCOCH3}$, $\ce{-OCH3}$, $\ce{-CH3}$ and $\ce{-C2H5}$, direct to the ortho and para carbons. The halogens, $\ce{-F}$, $\ce{-Cl}$, $\ce{-Br}$ and $\ce{-I}$, slow the ring down but still direct to ortho and para. The deactivating groups $\ce{-NO2}$, $\ce{-CN}$, $\ce{-CHO}$, $\ce{-COR}$, $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-COOR}$ and $\ce{-SO3H}$ direct to the meta carbon.
</Step>

## Ortho and para directing groups

The activating groups release electrons into the ring. For OH, NH₂ and OCH₃, a lone pair on the atom that holds the group moves into the ring by resonance: the +R effect of Section 8.7.7. For CH₃ and C₂H₅, the alkyl group pushes electrons in by its +I effect. In each case the ortho and para carbons gain electron density. The effect is seen in phenol, where the OH group makes its ortho and para carbons electron-rich and so easier to attack by an electrophile.

The halogens behave differently. Their strong −I effect lowers the electron density of the whole ring, so chlorobenzene is less reactive than benzene. But a halogen also has lone pairs that donate by resonance, and that donation raises the electron density at the ortho and para carbons more than at the meta carbon. The halogens are therefore deactivating, yet they still direct the attack to the ortho and para positions.

Try the rule on a new molecule. Anisole, $\ce{C6H5OCH3}$, has a methoxy group, which is an activating ortho-para director. Nitration of anisole gives 2-nitroanisole and 4-nitroanisole. Both are named by the lib from the structure, and neither the meta isomer forms.

## Meta directing groups

The deactivating groups such as the nitro group pull electrons out of the ring by resonance. This is the −R effect, and it leaves the ortho and para carbons short of electrons. The meta carbon is then the comparatively electron-rich position, and an incoming electrophile is steered to it. The whole ring is less reactive as well, so the substitution is harder than it is for benzene.

Nitrobenzene shows both points. Its nitration gives 1,3-dinitrobenzene, a meta product, and its bromination with a Lewis acid gives 1-bromo-3-nitrobenzene. In each case the second group lands two carbons away from the nitro group, which is the meta position.

<KeyIdeas>
- A group already on the ring decides where the next group goes. Groups that release electrons into the ring send the attack to the ortho and para carbons.
- The groups $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-NH2}$, $\ce{-NHR}$, $\ce{-NHCOCH3}$, $\ce{-OCH3}$, $\ce{-CH3}$ and $\ce{-C2H5}$ activate the ring and direct ortho and para.
- Halogens deactivate the ring through their −I effect, but their lone pairs still direct ortho and para.
- The groups $\ce{-NO2}$, $\ce{-CN}$, $\ce{-CHO}$, $\ce{-COR}$, $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-COOR}$ and $\ce{-SO3H}$ withdraw electrons by resonance and direct the attack to the meta carbon.
- The extra form for an ortho or para attack on phenol puts the + on the carbon that carries OH; the meta attack has no such form.
</KeyIdeas>
