<Hook>
A kerosene lamp smokes and blackens its chimney. A gas ring, burning a fuel made of the same two elements, gives a clean blue flame. Carbon and hydrogen are the only elements in both fuels, so the difference cannot be the ingredients. It must be how the carbons are joined to one another.
</Hook>

Every hydrocarbon is made of carbon and hydrogen and nothing else. The fuels around the home are mixtures of them. LPG, the liquefied petroleum gas in a kitchen cylinder, is mostly propane and butane, kept liquid under pressure. CNG is natural gas compressed into a tank, and it is mostly methane. LNG is the same gas cooled until it becomes a liquid. Petrol, diesel and kerosene are not single compounds either: each is a slice of crude petroleum, separated by fractional distillation according to its boiling range.

## Sorting by the bonds between carbons

Chemists sort hydrocarbons with one question: which kinds of bond join the carbon atoms? The answer gives three families. A second question, whether the carbons form an open chain or a closed ring, cuts across all three.

### Saturated: single bonds only

A saturated hydrocarbon has single bonds everywhere, between carbon and carbon and between carbon and hydrogen. Every carbon has spent all four of its bonds. The open-chain saturated hydrocarbons are the alkanes, which Section 9.2 takes up. Older texts call them paraffins, from Latin words meaning little affinity, because under ordinary conditions they hardly react with acids, bases or most other reagents. When the same single-bonded carbons close into a ring, the molecules are cycloalkanes, and they are saturated too.

### Unsaturated: at least one multiple bond

A carbon–carbon double bond or triple bond leaves bonding capacity unused, so these molecules can take on other atoms. A molecule with a double bond is an alkene, and one with a triple bond is an alkyne. Both are unsaturated, and Sections 9.3 and 9.4 treat them one at a time.

### Aromatic: a stable ring

Aromatic hydrocarbons are cyclic molecules whose ring of conjugated double bonds is unusually stable. Benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, is the one you will meet first. Its ring does not behave like the ring of an alkene, and Section 9.5 explains why.

The four families grow from a single carbon. Follow the sequence below: the same carbon gains bonds, and the bonds change from single to double to triple until the carbons close into the ring.

<Scrolly animation="hydrocarbon-family">
<Step>
Start with a single carbon and give it four single bonds to hydrogen. The result is methane, $\ce{CH4}$. Every bond is single, so the molecule is saturated, and it belongs to the alkane family. Its four bonds point towards the corners of a tetrahedron, and the angle between any two hydrogens is $109.5^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step>
Join a second carbon to the first by a single bond, and let each carbon fill its remaining bonds with hydrogen. The result is ethane, $\ce{C2H6}$. It has no multiple bonds, so it is still an alkane. Check the count: the general formula for alkanes is $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, and with $n = 2$ it gives six hydrogens.
</Step>
<Step>
Turn the bond between the two carbons into a double bond. Two hydrogens leave, and the result is ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$. A double bond makes it an alkene, with the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$. Each carbon is $\mathrm{sp}^2$ hybridised, and the bond angle at each carbon is $120^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step>
Make the bond a triple bond, and two more hydrogens leave. The result is ethyne, $\ce{C2H2}$, an alkyne with the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n-2}}$. Each carbon is $\mathrm{sp}$ hybridised, and the three atoms of the molecule lie on a straight line, so the bond angle is $180^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step>
Now six carbons close into a ring, each carrying one hydrogen, with three double bonds placed alternately. The formula is $\ce{C6H6}$, and every carbon is $\mathrm{sp}^2$ hybridised. The ring holds six $\pi$ electrons, which fits the count of Hückel's rule, $4n+2$ with $n = 1$. That verdict is what makes benzene an aromatic hydrocarbon.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Open chains and rings

An open-chain hydrocarbon has carbons joined in a line or a branched chain. A ring closes back on itself. The ring is not the same as an aromatic compound: cyclohexane is a ring of single bonds, so it is saturated, while cyclohexene, with one double bond in its ring, is unsaturated.

The formula cannot tell two such molecules apart. Cyclobutane and but-1-ene both have the formula $\ce{C4H8}$, yet one is a ring of single bonds and the other is an open chain with a double bond. Only the bonds and the shape separate them.

<KeyIdeas>
- Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen. Their fuels and raw materials are mixtures sorted by the boiling range they distil at.
- Saturated hydrocarbons have only single bonds. Open-chain ones are alkanes, and ring ones are cycloalkanes.
- Unsaturated hydrocarbons have a double or triple bond: alkenes and alkynes.
- Aromatic hydrocarbons are rings that pass Hückel's rule, with $4n+2$ $\pi$ electrons. Benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, is the example.
- Open chain and ring are a second question: the formula can match for both, so the bonds and the shape decide the family.
</KeyIdeas>
