<Hook>
A welder's torch burns ethyne with oxygen, and a fruit seller can ripen bananas with ethene. The two gases belong to the same family of unsaturated hydrocarbons, and both carry a multiple bond. Drop a piece of sodium into ethyne, though, and it fizzes, while ethene does nothing at all. What is it about the triple bond that lets a hydrogen leave, and why does ethene keep its hydrogens?
</Hook>

## Physical properties

Alkynes follow the trend that alkanes and alkenes already show. The first three members, ethyne, propyne and but-1-yne, are gases at room temperature. The next group is liquid and the higher members are solids. Every alkyne is colourless.

Alkynes are weakly polar, lighter than water and do not mix with it. They dissolve well in organic solvents such as ether, carbon tetrachloride and benzene. As the molar mass goes up, the melting point, the boiling point and the density all rise, because the larger molecules have more surface for van der Waals attraction.

## Chemical properties

Alkynes show three kinds of reaction in this Section: acidic behaviour, addition across the triple bond, and polymerisation. The Scrolly below follows them in that order, with one worked example for each.

<Scrolly animation="alkyne-reactions" layout="wide">
<Step>
Three molecules are compared: ethane, ethene and ethyne. Each has a hydrogen on a carbon, but the carbon differs. In ethane the carbon is $sp^3$, with 25% s character. In ethene it is $sp^2$, with about 33% s character. In ethyne it is $sp$, with 50% s character. The more s character a carbon orbital has, the closer its bonding pair sits to the nucleus. So the C–H pair of ethyne is held most tightly by carbon, and that hydrogen leaves most easily.
</Step>
<Step>
Sodium metal and sodamide are strong bases, and both remove the hydrogen from ethyne. For ethyne with sodium, the equation is $\ce{2CH#CH + 2Na -> 2CH#CNa + H2}$. The product, sodium ethynide, is the acetylide ion held with a sodium ion, and the hydrogen escapes as gas. Ethene and ethane give no such reaction, so this test separates alkynes from alkenes and alkanes. The test works only on the terminal $\ce{C#C-H}$ hydrogen: an internal alkyne such as but-2-yne has none to lose.
</Step>
<Step>
Bromine adds to a triple bond one molecule at a time. The first addition gives a dibromoalkene, and the second gives a tetrabromoalkane. Both steps are shown for but-1-yne with $\ce{Br2}$ in carbon tetrachloride: $\ce{CH3CH2C#CH + Br2 -> CH3CH2CBr=CHBr}$, and then $\ce{CH3CH2CBr=CHBr + Br2 -> CH3CH2CBr2CHBr2}$. The reddish-orange bromine fades as it reacts, which is why this addition is used as a test for unsaturation.
</Step>
<Step>
Water adds to ethyne in the presence of mercury(II) ions and dilute sulphuric acid at 333 K. The first product is an enol, a molecule with an –OH on a C=C. The enol rearranges to a carbonyl compound, so ethyne gives ethanal. But-1-yne follows the same route to a different carbonyl compound: first but-1-en-2-ol, and then butan-2-one. The –OH goes on the more substituted carbon of the triple bond, which is the Markovnikov choice.
</Step>
<Step>
Ethyne can also join with itself. When it is passed over an iron tube at a red heat, 873 K, three molecules of ethyne close into one ring. The three triple bonds become three double bonds in one ring, and that ring is benzene. This route takes an aliphatic molecule to an aromatic one. Ethyne can also link end to end into a long chain, polyacetylene, which is the linear form of the same polymerisation.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Acidic character

The hydrogen of a terminal alkyne is not like the hydrogens of an alkane. Sodium and sodamide remove it, and the reason goes back to the hybridisation of its carbon. The hydrogen sits on an sp carbon, which has 50% s character. Those electrons are held closest to the nucleus, so the C–H pair is pulled towards carbon and the proton comes away more easily.

The order of acidity is ethyne, then ethene, then ethane. For the sodium test, the important point is that only the first of these reacts, because only the sp carbon holds its bonding pair tightly enough for the proton to leave. NCERT gives the order $\ce{HC#CH > H2C=CH2 > CH3-CH3}$ for the three gases, and $\ce{HC#CH > CH3-C#CH >> CH3-C#C-CH3}$ for the terminal and internal alkynes.

The Scrolly's first step shows the s character of the three carbons. The sodium test in Step 2 is the same idea as an experiment: the hydrogen that goes is the one on the sp carbon.

For sodamide, the same reaction works with a different leaving group. Propyne gives sodium propynide and ammonia:

$$
\ce{CH3-C#CH + NaNH2 -> CH3-C#C-Na + NH3}
$$

## Addition reactions

A triple bond is rich in π electrons, so it adds molecules across it. Most of these additions are electrophilic, and where the alkyne is unsymmetrical the Markovnikov rule decides the orientation. The addition happens in two steps, because the first product still has a double bond that can take a second molecule.

**Hydrogen.** With a platinum, palladium or nickel catalyst, ethyne first becomes ethene and then ethane:

$$
\ce{CH#CH + H2 ->[Pt/Pd/Ni] CH2=CH2 ->[H2] CH3CH3}
$$

**Halogens.** Chlorine or bromine adds in two steps as the Scrolly shows. The two halogens go on the two carbons of the triple bond in the first step, and in the second they join the same pair of carbons across the new double bond.

**Hydrogen halides.** Two molecules of HBr or HCl add to an alkyne, and both additions follow Markovnikov's rule. The result is a gem-dihalide, in which both halogens sit on one carbon. For example, ethyne with HBr goes to 1,1-dibromoethane through bromoethene:

$$
\ce{HC#CH + HBr -> CH2=CHBr ->[HBr] CH3CHBr2}
$$

**Water.** Water adds in the presence of mercury(II) sulphate and dilute sulphuric acid at 333 K. The product is a carbonyl compound: ethanal from ethyne, and a ketone from any other alkyne. The enol is short-lived and rearranges as soon as it forms.

## Polymerisation

Alkynes can also join into longer molecules. Two kinds of polymerisation are important.

**Linear polymerisation.** Under suitable conditions, ethyne joins end to end into polyacetylene, a polymer with alternating single and double bonds along a chain. Under special conditions this polymer conducts electricity.

**Cyclic polymerisation.** Three ethyne molecules close into a single ring over a red-hot iron tube at 873 K:

$$
\ce{3CH#CH ->[Fe, 873 K] C6H6}
$$

This is the route from an aliphatic compound to an aromatic one, and benzene made this way is the starting point for many dyes and drugs.

<KeyIdeas>
- The C–H of a terminal alkyne is acidic because its sp carbon has 50% s character, so the bonding pair sits close to carbon. Sodium and sodamide remove that hydrogen, and this separates alkynes from alkenes and alkanes.
- Acidity follows the s character: $\ce{HC#CH > H2C=CH2 > CH3-CH3}$. Internal alkynes have no acidic hydrogen.
- An alkyne adds two molecules of $\ce{H2}$, a halogen or a hydrogen halide in two steps. Two Markovnikov HX additions give a gem-dihalide.
- Water with $\ce{Hg^2+}$ and dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$ at 333 K gives an enol that rearranges to a carbonyl compound: ethanal from ethyne, a ketone from other alkynes.
- Three ethyne molecules over red-hot iron at 873 K close into benzene. Linear polymerisation gives polyacetylene.
</KeyIdeas>
