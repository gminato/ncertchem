<Hook>
Mothballs in a wardrobe give off a sharp smell for weeks. Many of them are made of naphthalene, $\ce{C10H8}$, a white solid that slowly turns into vapour at room temperature. Naphthalene has ten carbons and a ring structure with several double bonds, so you might expect it to react as eagerly as an alkene. It does not. Why is a hydrocarbon this unsaturated so calm, and what is different about its rings?
</Hook>

## Arenes (9.5)

Hydrocarbons that contain a benzene ring are called arenes. Many of them have a pleasant smell, and that is how the whole class came to be called aromatic. The smell is not the reason for the name any more. Aromatic now means something about the electrons in a ring, and it applies to some compounds that have no benzene ring at all.

Compounds that contain a benzene ring are called benzenoid. Naphthalene, $\ce{C10H8}$, is benzenoid: it is two benzene rings sharing a bond. Some aromatic compounds are non-benzenoid. The cyclopentadienyl anion, $\ce{C5H5^-}$, and the tropylium ion, $\ce{C7H7^+}$, have no benzene ring, yet they share the electron pattern that makes benzene special. Section 9.5.3 gives that pattern a name.

## Nomenclature and isomerism (9.5.1)

All six hydrogen atoms of benzene are equivalent. So there is only one way to put a single substituent on the ring: chlorobenzene, $\ce{C6H5Cl}$, is the only monosubstituted product, and so is methylbenzene (toluene), $\ce{C6H5CH3}$.

Two substituents give three position isomers. Number the ring from one substituent. If the second sits at position 2, the pair is ortho (o-); at position 3, it is meta (m-); and at position 4, it is para (p-). Positions 1 and 6 are the same ortho pair as 1 and 2, so the ring has only three distinct arrangements. The xylenes, $\ce{C6H4(CH3)2}$, show all three: o-xylene is 1,2-dimethylbenzene, m-xylene is 1,3-dimethylbenzene and p-xylene is 1,4-dimethylbenzene.

## Structure of benzene (9.5.2)

Michael Faraday isolated benzene in 1825, and it took decades to work out its structure. Its formula, $\ce{C6H6}$, has far fewer hydrogens than the alkane with six carbons, so the molecule is very unsaturated. Yet it is stable, and it reacts by substitution far more readily than by addition.

In 1865 Kekulé proposed a ring of six carbons with alternate single and double bonds, one hydrogen on each carbon. This explained the formula, but it ran into a problem. If the bonds alternate, the two Br atoms of an ortho-dibromobenzene could sit on the two carbons of a double bond, or on the two carbons of a single bond. That predicts two isomers. Benzene gives only one.

Valence bond theory explains the ring with resonance. Benzene is a hybrid of Kekulé's structures A and B, and neither drawing on its own is the molecule. <Step>
Look at the orbitals. Each carbon is sp² hybridised, so its σ bonds lie in the plane of the ring. Each carbon keeps one unhybridised p orbital, pointing straight up or down out of that plane. Neighbouring p orbitals overlap sideways, and the six of them merge into one π cloud, with a doughnut of electron density above the ring and another below it.
</Step>
<Step>

Because the six electrons spread over the whole ring, benzene is more stable than a hypothetical cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene with three localised double bonds.
</Step>

The benzene bond is shorter than a single bond and longer than a double bond. That is the evidence that the six π electrons belong to the whole ring and not to any one pair of carbons.

## Aromaticity (9.5.3)

Benzene is the parent aromatic compound, and the name now applies to any ring that has three properties:

1. It is planar.
2. Its π electrons are completely delocalised round the ring.
3. It has $(4n + 2)$ π electrons, where $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$

This is Hückel's rule, and the count is the important part. Each C=C double bond in a Kekulé form supplies two π electrons. A ring atom outside any double bond still counts if it sits in the π system: a lone pair on an N, O or S atom in the ring adds two, as in pyrrole and furan, and an empty p orbital adds none. The total must be of the form $4n + 2$.

<Scrolly animation="huckel-count">
<Step>
Benzene is the first test. Its Kekulé form has three double bonds, and each gives two π electrons, so the count is six. Six is $4 \times 1 + 2$, so $n = 1$ and the ring is aromatic. The circle in the hexagon shows the same six electrons as a hybrid, so the count can be taken from any Kekulé form.
</Step>
<Step>
Naphthalene is two benzene rings sharing a bond. Its Kekulé form has five double bonds, so the count is ten. Ten is $4 \times 2 + 2$, and all ten carbons, including the two that are shared, are in one flat π system. Naphthalene is aromatic, with $n = 2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Anthracene is three rings fused in a straight row, with fourteen carbons. Its Kekulé form has seven double bonds, so the count is fourteen. Fourteen is $4 \times 3 + 2$, so $n = 3$. The electrons spread over the whole flat system, and anthracene is aromatic too.
</Step>
<Step>
Cyclooctatetraene has eight carbons and four double bonds, so the count is eight. Eight is $4 \times 2$, which is not of the form $4n + 2$. A planar ring with $4n$ electrons would be antiaromatic, but this ring is tub-shaped and not flat. So its electrons cannot be fully delocalised, and the molecule is non-aromatic.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Preparation of benzene (9.5.4)

Benzene is mostly isolated from coal tar, but three laboratory preparations are worth knowing, and each one starts from a compound you already know.

**Cyclic polymerisation of ethyne.** Three molecules of ethyne join in a red-hot iron tube at 873 K to close a six-membered ring. This is the reaction from Section 9.4.4, and it is the most direct route from a simple alkyne to benzene:

$$
\ce{3C2H2 ->[{red-hot iron tube}, 873 K] C6H6}
$$

**Decarboxylation of a sodium aromatic salt.** Heating sodium benzoate with soda lime removes the carboxyl group as sodium carbonate and replaces it with hydrogen. Soda lime is a mixture of NaOH and CaO, and NCERT prints CaO above the arrow:

$$
\ce{C6H5COONa + NaOH ->[CaO, \Delta] C6H6 + Na2CO3}
$$

**Reduction of phenol.** Phenol vapour passed over heated zinc dust loses its –OH group, which leaves as zinc oxide. The ring is left as benzene:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH + Zn -> C6H6 + ZnO}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- Benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, has six equal C–C bonds of 139 pm. That length is between a single bond (154 pm) and a double bond (134 pm), so no bond in the ring is a pure double bond.
- Kekulé's two forms A and B are resonance contributors. The hybrid is drawn as a hexagon with a circle, and the six π electrons are delocalised over the six carbons.
- Hückel's rule: a flat ring with completely delocalised π electrons is aromatic when it has $4n + 2$ of them. A flat ring with $4n$ electrons is antiaromatic, and a non-flat one is non-aromatic.
- Naphthalene (10 π electrons), anthracene (14), the cyclopentadienyl anion (6) and the tropylium ion (6) are aromatic. Cyclooctatetraene (8) is not, because its tub shape keeps the ring from being flat.
- Benzene is made by cyclic polymerisation of ethyne at 873 K, by heating sodium benzoate with soda lime, or by passing phenol over zinc dust.
</KeyIdeas>
