<Hook>
A label on a drum says $\ce{C5H12}$ and nothing more. Three different substances fit that formula, and they boil at three different temperatures, from about 282 K to about 309 K. The formula has told you the atoms, but not how they are joined. To read the joints, you count, carbon by carbon, how many other carbons each one touches.
</Hook>

The alkanes are the simplest family in organic chemistry: open chains in which every carbon is joined to its neighbours by single bonds and carries as many hydrogens as it can. Methane, $\ce{CH4}$, is the first member. If you replace one hydrogen of methane by a $\ce{CH3}$ group, you get ethane, $\ce{C2H6}$. Replace a hydrogen of ethane by another $\ce{CH3}$ and you get propane, $\ce{C3H8}$, and the pattern continues. Each new member adds one $\ce{CH2}$ unit to the one before it, and a family like this, whose members differ by a fixed unit, is called a homologous series.

## The general formula and the tetrahedron

The series has one general formula, $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$. Choose $n$ and you have one member: $n = 1$ gives methane, and $n = 9$ gives an alkane with 20 hydrogens. Check the arithmetic against the carbons: a chain of $n$ carbons has $n - 1$ bonds between them, each carbon has four bonds in all, and the hydrogens fill the rest.

The carbon of methane is $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybridised. Its four bonds point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron, and every H–C–H angle is $109.5^\circ$. In an alkane, each carbon is a small tetrahedron, and the tetrahedra are joined corner to corner along the chain. The bond lengths are 154 pm for a C–C bond and 112 pm for a C–H bond, the figures this unit uses throughout. Their bonds are made of head-on overlaps of $\mathrm{sp}^3$ hybrid orbitals with the 1s orbitals of hydrogen, and each bond is a σ bond.

## Chain isomers: the same formula, a different chain

The first three alkanes, methane, ethane and propane, have only one possible structure each. From butane onwards the carbons can be joined in more than one way. Four carbons can form a single unbranched chain, or a chain with a carbon at a branch point. Both have the formula $\ce{C4H10}$, and they are chain isomers: molecules with the same molecular formula whose carbon skeletons differ.

Their properties differ, too. Butane boils at 272.4 K and 2-methylpropane at 261.0 K. Five carbons give three chain isomers, all $\ce{C5H12}$: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane. The boiling points from Table 9.2 are 309.1 K, 300.9 K and 282.5 K. The more branched the molecule, the lower the boiling point, because a compact molecule touches its neighbours over less surface, and so attracts them less strongly.

The number of chain isomers grows fast: hexane's formula $\ce{C6H14}$ has five chain isomers, and $\ce{C7H16}$ has nine. The Scrolly below walks through the three isomers of $\ce{C5H12}$, colouring each carbon by its class.

## Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary carbons

Classify each carbon by the number of other carbons attached to it. A carbon bonded to no other carbon or to one other carbon is **primary**, $1^\circ$, and so is the end of any chain. A carbon bonded to two carbons is **secondary**, $2^\circ$. A carbon bonded to three is **tertiary**, $3^\circ$, and one bonded to four is **quaternary**, $4^\circ$. A hydrogen takes the class of the carbon it sits on, so the hydrogens of a methyl group are primary hydrogens.

<Step>
Move one carbon off the chain, and the isomer becomes 2-methylbutane. The carbon at the branch point touches three other carbons, so it is tertiary, and it still carries one hydrogen. The $\ce{CH2}$ between the branch and the end is secondary, and the three methyl ends are primary. Three primary carbons, one secondary and one tertiary: the formula has not changed, so this is a chain isomer.
</Step>
<Step>
Gather all the branching onto one carbon, and you have 2,2-dimethylpropane. Its central carbon touches four others, so it is quaternary, and it carries no hydrogen. The four methyl groups are primary. All twelve of its hydrogens sit on primary carbons, and the molecule has the most compact shape of the three.
</Step>

## Naming alkanes: the rules from Unit 8

The naming rules are the ones from Section 8.5: take the longest chain as the parent, number it so the side chains get the lowest locants, and name the side chains in alphabetical order. Apply them to a molecule of your own. Take the chain $\ce{CCC(CC)C(C)C(C)C}$, which has ten carbons. The longest chain has six carbons, and it carries an ethyl group and two methyl groups. Numbered from the end that gives the lower set of locants, the name is 4-ethyl-2,3-dimethylhexane, with the formula $\ce{C10H22}$. The ethyl comes first alphabetically, which is why it is cited before the methyls.

Alkyl groups follow the same rules. An alkyl group is an alkane with one hydrogen removed, so its general formula is $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}}$. The four groups with the formula $\ce{C4H9-}$ are the butyl group, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2-}$, the sec-butyl group, $\ce{CH3CH2CH(CH3)-}$, the isobutyl group, $\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2-}$, and the tert-butyl group, $\ce{(CH3)3C-}$. In the last one, the attached carbon is tertiary.

<KeyIdeas>
- The alkanes form a homologous series with the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, and every carbon is $\mathrm{sp}^3$ with bond angles of $109.5^\circ$.
- Chain isomers share a molecular formula but differ in their carbon skeleton. Five carbons give three of them: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane.
- Each carbon is primary, secondary, tertiary or quaternary according to the number of other carbons it touches, and each hydrogen takes the class of its carbon.
- The more branched the chain, the lower the boiling point, because the compact molecule has less surface in contact with its neighbours.
- Name an alkane with the Unit 8 rules: the longest chain is the parent, the lowest locants go to the side chains, and the side chains are cited in alphabetical order.
</KeyIdeas>
