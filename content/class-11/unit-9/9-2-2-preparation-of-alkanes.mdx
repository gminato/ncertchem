<Hook>
Somewhere on a farm, a biogas tank bubbles away on cattle dung and kitchen waste, and the gas it gives off burns with a clean blue flame. Most of that gas is methane. Nobody feeds the tank sodalime, zinc or an electric current. Microbes make the methane with no laboratory recipe at all, so the chemist's routes to alkanes have to earn their place by being predictable.
</Hook>

Alkanes come mostly from petroleum and natural gas, so a chemist rarely needs to make one from scratch. The routes are still worth learning. Each one builds a bond in a known place, and each one changes the carbon count in a known way, which makes them a good test of whether you understand the reaction or only remember its name.

## 9.2.2 Preparation of alkanes

The six steps below follow the four families of preparation. Watch the carbon count under each species: it tells you what the route can and cannot make.

<Scrolly animation="alkane-routes">
<Step>
Begin with the simplest route: add hydrogen across a multiple bond. Propyne has one triple bond, and two molecules of hydrogen saturate it, one bond at a time, to give propane. Finely divided platinum, palladium or nickel makes the reaction go, because the metal surface holds the hydrogen molecules and splits the bond between their atoms. The carbon count does not change, which is the signature of an addition.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, an alkyl halide. Zinc and dilute acid reduce 1-chloropropane: the carbon–chlorine bond is replaced by a carbon–hydrogen bond, and the chlorine leaves as hydrogen chloride. Three carbons go in and three come out, so the skeleton is untouched. Only the halogen is swapped for a hydrogen.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the Wurtz reaction, with one halide used twice. Bromoethane and sodium in dry ether couple the two ethyl groups at the carbons that lost their bromine. Two halides give one butane and two molecules of sodium bromide. The carbon count doubles, two plus two gives four, and the ether must be dry, because water would destroy the sodium before it could react.
</Step>
<Step>
Mix two different halides in the Wurtz reaction and the product is no longer one alkane. Bromomethane and 1-bromobutane give three alkanes, one from each pairing of alkyl groups: ethane, pentane and octane. Pentane is among them, but only as one product of three. A single halide cannot make an odd-carbon alkane this way, because its two partners always carry the same number of carbons.
</Step>
<Step>
Heat the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid with soda lime, a mixture of sodium hydroxide and calcium oxide, and the carboxyl carbon leaves as carbonate, $\ce{Na2CO3}$. Sodium butanoate gives propane and sodium carbonate. The alkane has one carbon fewer than the acid, which is the reason this route is called decarboxylation.
</Step>
<Step>
Electrolyse an aqueous solution of sodium ethanoate and the two alkyl groups couple at the anode, where carbon dioxide bubbles off, while hydrogen forms at the cathode. Ethane results. The electrode reactions are the heart of the method, and they explain why each product is an even count of carbons. There is no alkyl group in methanoate, so this cell cannot make methane.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

### From unsaturated hydrocarbons

Alkenes and alkynes gain hydrogen across their multiple bonds in the presence of finely divided platinum, palladium or nickel. This is hydrogenation. Platinum and palladium work at room temperature, while nickel needs a higher temperature and pressure. A triple bond takes up two molecules of hydrogen and a double bond takes up one, so the number of hydrogen molecules you need is fixed by the number of multiple bonds.

### From alkyl halides

Alkyl halides, except the fluorides, give alkanes when they are reduced by zinc and dilute hydrochloric acid. The halogen is removed and replaced by hydrogen. The skeleton is kept, so the carbon count does not change.

Alkyl halides also react with sodium in dry ether, and this is the Wurtz reaction. Two alkyl halides lose their halogens and their alkyl groups join at the carbon atoms that were bonded to the halogen. Two identical halides give one alkane with twice the carbons of each partner, so the Wurtz reaction is a route to alkanes with an even number of carbon atoms.

Why is the Wurtz reaction not preferred for alkanes with an odd number of carbons? Take pentane as the example. Its two halves would have to be an ethyl and a propyl group, or a methyl and a butyl group, and whichever pair you choose, the two different halides give the two self-couplings as well. So pentane always comes out as one part of a mixture. The reaction is useful for a clean product only when the two alkyl groups are the same.

### From carboxylic acids

A sodium salt of a carboxylic acid, heated with soda lime, gives an alkane with one carbon fewer than the acid. The carboxyl carbon leaves as carbonate, $\ce{Na2CO3}$, and the bond it made to the chain is replaced by a bond to hydrogen. This decarboxylation is the route to reach for when you want one carbon fewer than an acid. It is also how methane is made, from sodium ethanoate.

<Check n={2} options={["Butane", "Pentane", "Propane", "Ethane"]} answer={0} explanation="Pentanoic acid has five carbons, and the carboxyl carbon is one of them. Decarboxylation removes it, so the alkane has four carbons: butane.">
Sodium pentanoate is heated with soda lime. Which alkane forms?
</Check>

Kolbe's electrolytic method starts from an aqueous solution of a sodium or potassium salt of a carboxylic acid. At the anode, each carboxylate loses an electron and then breaks into carbon dioxide and an alkyl free radical. Two alkyl free radicals pair up to form an alkane with twice the alkyl carbons. At the cathode, water is reduced to hydrogen. The overall equation for sodium ethanoate is

$$
\ce{2CH3COONa + 2H2O ->[electrolysis] CH3CH3 + 2CO2 + H2 + 2NaOH}
$$

Because each product is two alkyl groups joined together, the Kolbe method gives an alkane with an even number of carbons, and it cannot make methane. Methanoate has no alkyl group at all, so there is no methyl radical to pair. Hydrogen, not methane, is what would come off.

<KeyIdeas>
- Hydrogenation adds hydrogen across a C=C or C≡C bond with Pt, Pd or Ni, and the carbon count stays the same.
- Zinc and dilute acid replace the halogen of an alkyl halide with hydrogen. Fluorides are not reduced this way.
- The Wurtz reaction couples two alkyl groups with sodium in dry ether, so two identical halides give an alkane with an even number of carbons.
- Decarboxylation with soda lime removes the carboxyl carbon, so the alkane has one carbon fewer than the acid.
- Kolbe's electrolysis joins two alkyl radicals made at the anode, giving twice the alkyl carbons; methane cannot be made this way because methanoate has no alkyl group.
</KeyIdeas>
