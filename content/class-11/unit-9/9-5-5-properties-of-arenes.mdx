## Physical properties

Aromatic hydrocarbons are non-polar molecules. Most of them are colourless liquids or solids with a strong, characteristic smell. The smell of the naphthalene balls kept in a cupboard is one you already know, and it is the reason those balls keep moths away from clothes.

They do not mix with water, but they mix readily with organic solvents. When they burn in air, they give a sooty flame, because their high carbon content leaves much of the carbon unburnt in the flame, as soot.

## Chemical properties

Arenes are characterised by electrophilic substitution reactions. Under special conditions they can also take part in addition and oxidation reactions, but the first thing to learn is the substitution. In it, a ring hydrogen is replaced by a group that carries a positive charge or a partial positive charge, which is called the electrophile, $\ce{E+}$.

## Electrophilic substitution reactions

Five substitution reactions of benzene are common in the laboratory. In each, one ring hydrogen is replaced and the ring stays intact.

**Nitration.** Heating benzene with concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulphuric acid, the nitrating mixture, gives nitrobenzene. The reaction is carried out between 323 and 333 K:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + HNO3 ->[conc. H2SO4, 323-333 K] C6H5NO2 + H2O}
$$

**Halogenation.** Chlorine does not react with benzene alone. A Lewis acid such as anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$, $\ce{FeCl3}$ or $\ce{FeBr3}$ is needed to make the chlorine electrophilic enough, and chlorobenzene and hydrogen chloride are formed:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + Cl2 ->[anhyd. AlCl3] C6H5Cl + HCl}
$$

**Sulphonation.** Heating benzene with fuming sulphuric acid, which contains free sulphur trioxide, replaces a hydrogen by an $\ce{-SO3H}$ group. The product is benzenesulphonic acid, and water is formed as well:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + H2SO4 ->[fuming H2SO4, heat] C6H5SO3H + H2O}
$$

**Friedel–Crafts alkylation.** Benzene and an alkyl chloride react in the presence of anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ to give an alkylbenzene. Chloromethane gives toluene, and chloroethane gives ethylbenzene:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + CH3Cl ->[anhyd. AlCl3] C6H5CH3 + HCl}
$$

Why does 1-chloropropane give isopropylbenzene rather than propylbenzene? The primary carbocation that it would give is unstable, so it changes into the more stable secondary carbocation before it attacks the ring. The carbon chain that reaches the ring is therefore the branched one.

**Friedel–Crafts acylation.** An acyl chloride or an acid anhydride, with anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ and heat, gives an acylbenzene. Ethanoyl chloride gives acetophenone:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + CH3COCl ->[anhyd. AlCl3, \Delta] C6H5COCH3 + HCl}
$$

When an excess of the electrophile is used, more than one hydrogen can be replaced. With excess chlorine in the presence of anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$, in the dark and cold, benzene becomes hexachlorobenzene:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + 6Cl2 ->[anhyd. AlCl3, dark, cold] C6Cl6 + 6HCl}
$$

## The mechanism in three steps

Every one of these reactions runs through the same three steps. The electrophile is made first, then the ring attacks it and forms a carbocation, and finally a proton leaves the carbocation to restore the ring. Step through the animation to see each one with its structures.

<Scrolly animation="eas-mechanism" layout="wide">
<Step>
Making the electrophile starts with the acid pair. Concentrated sulphuric acid is the stronger acid, so it passes a proton to nitric acid, which acts as a base here. The protonated nitric acid then loses a molecule of water, and what is left is the nitronium ion, $\ce{NO2+}$. Its nitrogen sits in the middle of two oxygens, so the ion is linear, and the nitrogen carries the positive charge.
</Step>
<Step>
The benzene ring has six π electrons in a cloud above and below its plane. One pair of these electrons reaches out to the nitrogen of $\ce{NO2+}$ and forms a new C–N bond. The ring carbon that receives the new bond is now $sp^3$ hybridised, and the other five carbons carry the positive charge that the pair has left behind.
</Step>
<Step>
The positive charge is spread over the carbons ortho and para to the $sp^3$ carbon, so the cation has three contributors that differ only in where the charge sits. Together they make the σ-complex, also called the arenium ion. Its four remaining π electrons are spread over five carbons, and the $sp^3$ carbon takes no part in that delocalisation. Because the ring's delocalised system is broken here, the σ-complex is not aromatic.
</Step>
<Step>
A base removes the proton from the $sp^3$ carbon. In nitration this base is the hydrogen sulphate ion, $\ce{HSO4-}$, which is the species left over from the acid pair. The two electrons of the C–H bond return to the ring, the π system is complete again, and nitrobenzene is formed. The sulphuric acid is regenerated in this step.
</Step>
<Step>
Friedel–Crafts alkylation uses the same three steps, but the electrophile is made differently. Anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ is a Lewis acid: it accepts a chloride ion from chloromethane, leaving the methyl cation, $\ce{CH3+}$, and the tetrachloroaluminate ion, $\ce{AlCl4-}$. The cation is then the electrophile that attacks the π cloud.
</Step>
<Step>
The σ-complex now loses its proton to $\ce{AlCl4-}$. The products are toluene, whose IUPAC name is methylbenzene, together with hydrogen chloride and aluminium chloride. The ring is restored, and aromaticity with it.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Addition reactions

Under vigorous conditions, benzene adds hydrogen. With nickel as the catalyst and heat, the three π bonds are reduced and cyclohexane is formed:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + 3H2 ->[Ni, heat] C6H12}
$$

Ultraviolet light makes benzene add chlorine instead. Three molecules of $\ce{Cl2}$ add across the ring to give benzene hexachloride, $\ce{C6H6Cl6}$, which is also known as BHC or gammaxane. It is an addition, not a substitution, and it needs ultraviolet light to start the reaction.

## Combustion

When benzene is heated in air it burns with a sooty flame. Two molecules of benzene need fifteen molecules of oxygen for complete combustion:

$$
\ce{2C6H6 + 15O2 -> 12CO2 + 6H2O}
$$

Any hydrocarbon burns in the same way. For a hydrocarbon $\ce{C_xH_y}$ the equation is

$$
\ce{C_xH_y + (x + y/4) O2 -> x CO2 + (y/2) H2O}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- Arenes undergo electrophilic substitution: a ring hydrogen is replaced and the aromatic ring is kept. The common reactions are nitration, halogenation, sulphonation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation and Friedel–Crafts acylation.
- The mechanism has three steps: generate the electrophile, form a σ-complex (arenium ion) with one $sp^3$ carbon, and remove a proton to restore the aromatic ring.
- Nitration uses the nitronium ion, $\ce{NO2+}$, from concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ and $\ce{H2SO4}$, and $\ce{HSO4-}$ removes the proton. Halogenation and the Friedel–Crafts reactions use a Lewis acid, $\ce{AlCl3}$, and $\ce{AlCl4-}$ removes the proton.
- Under vigorous conditions, benzene adds hydrogen over nickel to give cyclohexane, and ultraviolet light adds three chlorine molecules to give benzene hexachloride.
- Aromatic hydrocarbons burn with a sooty flame. Complete combustion of benzene is $\ce{2C6H6 + 15O2 -> 12CO2 + 6H2O}$.
</KeyIdeas>
