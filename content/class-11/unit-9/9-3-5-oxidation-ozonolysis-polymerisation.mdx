<Hook>
A polythene bag from the kirana shop is made of the same kind of molecule as the gas ethene, a colourless gas with a double bond between its two carbons. Many ethene molecules, joined end to end, make the flexible solid that carries vegetables home. So what happens to the double bond when the gas becomes the bag, and how could you tell that a bottle of liquid contains a double bond at all?
</Hook>

The double bond of an alkene is the part of the molecule that reacts most readily. Its π electrons are loosely held above and below the C=C axis, so an oxidising agent can attack them. Three reactions of this kind are studied in this Section: oxidation with potassium permanganate, cutting the double bond with ozone, and polymerisation. The first two are chemical tests as well as reactions. The third builds the large molecules of plastics.

The Scrolly below follows one alkene through the three reactions. It starts with a test tube, then cuts the bond, then runs the cut backwards and ends with the polymer.

<Scrolly animation="alkene-cleavage" layout="wide">
<Step>
Begin with cyclohexene and a dilute, cold solution of potassium permanganate, $\ce{KMnO4}$. This is Baeyer's reagent, and the solution is purple. NCERT gives 273 K as the temperature for this reaction, so the test tube is kept cold. Nothing has happened yet. The question is whether the reagent will find a double bond in the molecule to attack.
</Step>
<Step>
It does find one. The purple fades, and a brown solid, manganese(IV) oxide, settles out. Each carbon of the old C=C has gained an $\ce{-OH}$ group, so the product is cyclohexane-1,2-diol, $\ce{C6H12O2}$, a vicinal diol. The loss of the purple colour is the test for unsaturation: a saturated alkane leaves the solution purple.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, take 1-methylcyclohexene and treat it with ozone. Ozone adds across the double bond and forms an ozonide, a ring that holds three oxygen atoms. The dashed line marks where the molecule is about to be cut. Zinc and water then break the ozonide apart, so the ring opens at the place where the double bond was.
</Step>
<Step>
Each carbon of the old C=C now carries an oxygen. The methyl-bearing carbon becomes a ketone and the other becomes an aldehyde. The product is 6-oxoheptanal, $\ce{C7H12O2}$, a chain with a $\ce{CH3CO}$ group at one end and a $\ce{CHO}$ group at the other. Because the two carbonyl groups mark the two ends of the old bond, ozonolysis shows where the double bond was.
</Step>
<Step>
Now run the story backwards. The two carbonyl carbons of 6-oxoheptanal are joined by a double bond, and their oxygen atoms are removed. The chain closes into a ring and gives back 1-methylcyclohexene. A chemist who knows only the products can work out the alkene in this way, and the answer can be checked by running the forward reaction.
</Step>
<Step>
Ethene is the simplest monomer of a polymer. At high temperature and high pressure, with a catalyst, the π bond of each ethene molecule opens and the units join end to end. The result is polythene, $\ce{(CH2CH2)_{n}}$, with $n$ units in the chain. The atoms are the same before and after. Only the double bonds have been used up.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## Oxidation of alkenes

Potassium permanganate is a strong oxidising agent, and its colour is one of the most useful signals in organic chemistry. Its reaction with an alkene depends on the conditions, and the two conditions give different products. Read the two cases side by side.

**Cold, dilute, alkaline permanganate (Baeyer's reagent).** The alkene is oxidised to a vicinal diol, a molecule with two $\ce{-OH}$ groups on neighbouring carbons. The equation for cyclohexene, with the oxygen from the reagent written as $\ce{[O]}$, is

$$
\ce{C6H10 + H2O + [O] ->[cold, dilute aqueous KMnO4 (273 K)] C6H12O2}
$$

The purple colour of $\ce{MnO4-}$ is discharged and a brown precipitate of manganese(IV) oxide forms. This change is used as a test for unsaturation. A solution that stays purple shows that no C=C bond was present to react.

**Hot, acidic permanganate or acidic dichromate.** The conditions are stronger, and the double bond is broken, not only oxidised. NCERT lists acidic potassium permanganate and acidic potassium dichromate as the oxidants that give ketones and, depending on the alkene, carboxylic acids. For 2-methylbut-2-ene, the CH end becomes ethanoic acid and the end that carries two methyl groups becomes propanone:

$$
\ce{CH3CH=C(CH3)CH3 + 3[O] ->[acidic KMnO4] CH3COOH + CH3COCH3}
$$

The rule is simple. A carbon that has two alkyl groups gives a ketone. A carbon with one hydrogen gives an acid, and a $\ce{CH2}$ end gives carbon dioxide. The Section's Simulation lets you check that rule for yourself.

## Ozonolysis

Ozone is a more gentle way to cut a double bond, and it leaves the carbons at the two ends in a form that is easy to read. The reaction has two steps. In the first, ozone adds across the C=C bond and gives an ozonide. In the second, zinc and water break the ozonide into smaller molecules. These are aldehydes or ketones, and zinc oxide is formed as well.

$$
\ce{CH3C(CH3)=C(CH3)CH3 + O3 + Zn ->[ozone, then Zn and water] CH3COCH3 + CH3COCH3 + ZnO}
$$

Ozonolysis is used to locate a double bond. Cut the alkene, identify the carbonyl compounds, and the carbons that carried the double bond are the ones that now carry an oxygen. The same idea works for dienes. Buta-1,3-diene has two C=C bonds, and both of them are cut, giving three carbonyl products: two molecules of methanal and one of ethanedial.

## Polymerisation

A polymer is a very large molecule built from many small repeating units. The small molecule that forms the unit is the **monomer**. Polymerisation is the process that joins monomers into the polymer. Alkenes are well suited to it, because the π bond of each monomer can open and form a new σ bond to the next monomer in the chain.

For ethene, the equation uses the repeat unit, with the double bond used up:

$$
\ce{n CH2=CH2 ->[high temperature and pressure with a catalyst] (CH2CH2)_{n}}
$$

The conditions are high temperature, high pressure and a catalyst, as NCERT gives them. Polythene is used for plastic bags, squeeze bottles, toys and pipes, and the same kind of joining is used for other alkenes. The Scrolly above shows the process.

<KeyIdeas>
- Cold, dilute, alkaline potassium permanganate (Baeyer's reagent) gives a vicinal diol at 273 K. Losing the purple colour is the test for unsaturation.
- Hot acidic permanganate or acidic dichromate breaks the double bond to ketones and, depending on the alkene, to carboxylic acids or carbon dioxide.
- Ozonolysis is ozone addition to form an ozonide, followed by cleavage with zinc and water. The carbonyl products show where the double bond was.
- A diene has two C=C bonds, and ozonolysis cuts both, so there are more products.
- Polymerisation joins monomers into a polymer. Ethene at high temperature and pressure, with a catalyst, gives polythene.
</KeyIdeas>
