A kerosene lamp with its wick turned too high smokes, and the glass above the flame goes black. Trim the wick and the same fuel burns with a clean blue flame. The fuel has not changed, only the air reaching the flame. Why does a short supply of air leave black soot behind, when both flames are releasing a great deal of heat?
</Hook>

Alkanes are the most patient hydrocarbons. Strong acids, strong bases and the usual oxidising and reducing agents leave them alone. Yet they do react, and the reactions in this Section show how. Light starts a chain of radicals that puts a halogen on a carbon. Air burns them. A catalyst and high pressure let air turn them into alcohols and acids. Heat breaks them into smaller pieces.

## Physical properties

Alkanes are almost non-polar. The C–C and C–H bonds are covalent, and carbon and hydrogen differ very little in electronegativity. The only forces between alkane molecules are weak van der Waals forces. Because of that, the first four members, C₁ to C₄, are gases at 298 K. C₅ to C₁₇ are liquids, and those with 18 carbon atoms or more are solids. Alkanes are colourless.

Solubility follows the rule that like dissolves like. A non-polar alkane does not mix with polar water, so an alkane layer floats on water instead of dissolving in it. The grease on a pan is a mixture of higher alkanes, which is why it does not wash off with water alone. Petrol, a mixture of lighter alkanes, dissolves it, and that is why petrol and the lighter fractions of petroleum are used for dry-cleaning grease stains.

The boiling point climbs steadily as the molecule grows. Each extra $\ce{CH2}$ group adds to the surface that touches a neighbour, so the van der Waals attraction grows too. Table 9.2 of NCERT gives the values. Octane boils at 398.7 K and heptane at 371.4 K, a rise of 27.3 K for one extra $\ce{CH2}$ group.

Branching works the other way. Pentane, a straight chain, boils at 309.1 K. Its branched isomers boil lower: 2-methylbutane at 300.9 K and 2,2-dimethylpropane at 282.5 K. The more branched the molecule, the closer it comes to a sphere, which touches its neighbours at fewer points and so needs less energy to leave them for the vapour.

## Chemical properties

### Substitution reactions

One or more hydrogen atoms of an alkane can be replaced by a halogen. Nitration and sulphonation need more reactive conditions and do not work for the lower alkanes. Halogenation happens at 573 to 773 K, or in diffused sunlight or ultraviolet light. The chlorination of methane gives four products, one after another:

$$
\ce{CH4 + Cl2 ->[h\nu] CH3Cl + HCl}
$$

Chloromethane reacts again to give dichloromethane, which gives trichloromethane, and finally tetrachloromethane. The four products mix, so the reaction is hard to stop after one substitution.

The halogens do not all react at the same speed. The rate of reaction with alkanes falls in the order $\ce{F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2}$. Fluorination is too violent to control. Iodination is very slow and reversible. An oxidising agent such as iodic acid turns the hydrogen iodide into iodine and so pulls the reaction forward:

$$
\ce{CH4 + I2 <=> CH3I + HI}
$$

$$
\ce{HIO3 + 5HI -> 3I2 + 3H2O}
$$

Within one alkane, the hydrogens are not all alike. Chlorination and bromination replace them at rates in the order 3° > 2° > 1°. Once a halogen is attached, you can read the rest from the mechanism below, which is the free-radical chain that makes the reaction run.

<Scrolly animation="radical-chain">
<Step>
Methane and chlorine stay almost unchanged in the dark at room temperature. Put them into diffused sunlight or ultraviolet light and the reaction begins. The first event is the homolysis of the Cl–Cl bond: each electron of the bond goes to a different chlorine atom. Each chlorine atom now carries one unpaired electron and is a chlorine free radical, $\ce{Cl^{.}}$. The Cl–Cl bond is the easiest to break, since it is weaker than the C–C and C–H bonds.
</Step>
<Step>
A chlorine radical is very reactive and attacks a methane molecule. It pulls one hydrogen away from carbon, which leaves a methyl radical, $\ce{CH3^{.}}$, and makes hydrogen chloride. The chlorine's electron pairs with the hydrogen to make H–Cl, and the electron left in the C–H bond stays with carbon. That is two fishhook arrows, one for each single-electron move. The methyl radical is not used up. It is handed on to the next step.
</Step>
<Step>
The methyl radical meets a chlorine molecule and takes one chlorine atom from it. That gives chloromethane and a new chlorine radical, which goes back to the first propagation step. The two steps form a loop: one $\ce{Cl^{.}}$ goes in and one comes out. Each turn of the loop makes one molecule of product, so a single initiation event can lead to many molecules before the chain stops.
</Step>
<Step>
The chain stops when two radicals meet and pair their electrons. There are three ways to do this. Two chlorine radicals give chlorine back. A methyl and a chlorine radical give chloromethane. Two methyl radicals give ethane, $\ce{CH3CH3}$. That last pairing is the reason ethane shows up as a by-product of chlorinating methane, even though the main product is chloromethane.
</Step>
<Step>
Shape matters beyond reactions. The three pentanes share the formula $\ce{C5H12}$ but not the shape. The straight chain of pentane lies along its whole length against its neighbours, so the contact is large. 2,2-Dimethylpropane is nearly a sphere that touches its neighbours at few points, and 2-methylbutane lies between the two. Table 9.2 gives their boiling points, and the order follows the contact.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

The drawing follows the chain through its four stages: initiation, the first propagation step, the loop that closes the chain, and termination. The chain runs as long as there are radicals and reactants, and the chain-ending pairings explain the by-products.

### Combustion

Alkanes burn in air or oxygen, and complete combustion gives carbon dioxide and water with a large release of heat. Methane has an enthalpy of combustion of −890 kJ mol⁻¹, and butane, $\ce{C4H10}$, releases 2875.84 kJ mol⁻¹, both as NCERT gives them:

$$
\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}, \qquad \Delta_cH^\circ = -890\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

$$
\ce{C4H10(g) + 13/2 O2(g) -> 4CO2(g) + 5H2O(l)}, \qquad \Delta_cH^\circ = -2875.84\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

The general equation for any alkane, $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, is

$$
\ce{C_nH_{2n+2} + (3n+1)/2 O2 -> nCO2 + (n+1)H2O}
$$

so one mole of alkane needs $\frac{3n+1}{2}$ mol of oxygen and gives $n$ mol of carbon dioxide and $(n+1)$ mol of water. Pentane, as an example:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3 + 8O2 -> 5CO2 + 6H2O}
$$

Because so much heat is released, alkanes are the fuels of our lives: the LPG in a kitchen is mostly propane and butane, and petrol is mostly alkanes. When air or oxygen is short, the combustion is incomplete and carbon black is formed. The black particles are the soot on the glass above the lamp, and carbon black is also used in printer ink and black pigments:

$$
\ce{CH4 + O2 ->[insufficient O2] C + 2H2O}
$$

### Controlled oxidation

Alkanes resist oxidation, but a regulated supply of oxygen at high pressure, with a suitable catalyst, gives a few useful oxidation products. NCERT gives four examples:

$$
\ce{2CH4 + O2 ->[Cu, 523 K, 100 atm] 2CH3OH}
$$

$$
\ce{CH4 + O2 ->[Mo2O3, heat] HCHO + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{2CH3CH3 + 3O2 ->[(CH3COO)2Mn, heat] 2CH3COOH + 2H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{(CH3)3CH ->[KMnO4] (CH3)3COH}
$$

The last one works only on an alkane with a tertiary hydrogen. Potassium permanganate turns 2-methylpropane, which has one, into 2-methylpropan-2-ol.

### Isomerisation

Heating an n-alkane with anhydrous aluminium chloride and hydrogen chloride gas gives branched alkanes. n-Hexane, for example, gives 2-methylpentane and 3-methylpentane as its main products:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 ->[anhyd. AlCl3, HCl] CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH3 + CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH3}
$$

### Aromatisation

Heating n-alkanes with six or more carbon atoms to 773 K at 10 to 20 atmospheres, over oxides of vanadium, molybdenum or chromium on alumina, gives benzene and its homologues. This is called aromatisation, or reforming. n-Hexane becomes benzene, with four molecules of hydrogen as the by-product:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 ->[773 K, oxides of V, Mo or Cr] C6H6 + 4H2}
$$

### Reaction with steam

Methane reacts with steam at 1273 K over a nickel catalyst. The products are carbon monoxide and hydrogen, and this route is the industrial way to make hydrogen gas:

$$
\ce{CH4 + H2O ->[1273 K, Ni] CO + 3H2}
$$

### Pyrolysis

Higher alkanes break into smaller alkanes and alkenes when they are heated strongly. This is called pyrolysis, or cracking. Oil gas and petrol gas are made from kerosene and petrol by this principle. Dodecane, a constituent of kerosene, heated to 973 K over platinum, palladium or nickel gives a mixture of heptane and pentene:

$$
\ce{C12H26 ->[973 K, Pt/Pd/Ni] C7H16 + C5H10}
$$

The other products are not shown in NCERT's equation, so they are left out here.

<KeyIdeas>
- Alkanes are non-polar and held together only by weak van der Waals forces. Their boiling points rise with molar mass and fall with branching, because a compact molecule has less surface in contact.
- Halogenation is a free-radical chain of three stages: initiation splits Cl₂ into two radicals, propagation runs a loop of two steps, and termination pairs radicals. Two methyl radicals give ethane, the by-product.
- The C–H bonds react in the order 3° > 2° > 1°, so the statistical count of hydrogens does not give the product mix on its own.
- Complete combustion of CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ gives CO₂ and H₂O with (3n + 1)/2 mol of O₂ per mole of alkane. Too little air leaves carbon black.
- Isomerisation, aromatisation, steam reforming and pyrolysis use heat, pressure or a catalyst. Each one is a named reaction with its own NCERT condition.
</KeyIdeas>
