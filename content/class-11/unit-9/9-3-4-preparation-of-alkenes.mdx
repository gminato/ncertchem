## Four routes to a C=C bond

An alkene has one C=C double bond, so a preparation has to create that π bond. Chemists do it in four ways. Three remove atoms from two neighbouring carbons: a hydrogen and a halogen, two halogens together, or a hydrogen and an –OH group as water. The fourth starts from an alkyne and adds just enough hydrogen to stop at the alkene.

Each route leaves something behind, and the reagent tells you what. The Scrolly below follows one example of each, in the order the Section takes them, and ends with the alkyne reduction, where the same starting compound gives a cis or a trans alkene.

<Scrolly animation="elimination">
<Step>
Begin with bromoethane, $\ce{CH3CH2Br}$. The carbon that carries bromine is the α-carbon. The bond between it and bromine is polar, because bromine holds the shared pair more strongly, so the α-carbon is δ+ and bromine is δ−. The carbon on the other side is the β-carbon, and its three hydrogens are the β-hydrogens. Those are the hydrogens that can leave.
</Step>
<Step>
Heat the bromide with alcoholic potassium hydroxide, and the base removes a β-hydrogen. The lone pair on the hydroxide ion forms a bond to that hydrogen, and the hydrogen leaves the molecule as part of water. Only a hydrogen on a carbon next to the α-carbon can leave this way, which is why the reaction is called β-elimination.
</Step>
<Step>
The next shift happens in the same moment. The electron pair of the C–H bond moves into the space between the α-carbon and the β-carbon, and it forms the new π bond. The electron pair of the C–Br bond moves onto bromine, which leaves as the bromide ion. The base, the C–H pair and the C–Br pair all move at once, so there is no intermediate to see.
</Step>
<Step>
Once the hydrogen and the bromide have gone, the two carbons that remain form a double bond. Ethene is the product, with potassium bromide and water as the other products. The halide is the one that leaves, the hydrogen is the one that is taken, and the π bond is what is new.
</Step>
<Step>
A vicinal dihalide has its two halogens on neighbouring carbons, as in 1,2-dibromoethane. Zinc metal takes both halogens away as zinc bromide, and the two carbons that they were bonded to join into a double bond. A single C–X bond gives no alkene this way, because zinc has no second halogen on a neighbouring carbon to take.
</Step>
<Step>
Ethanol has an –OH group on its α-carbon. Hot concentrated sulphuric acid makes that group leave as water, and a β-hydrogen leaves with it. The double bond forms between the two carbons, and ethene is the product. Because water is removed in the presence of an acid, the reaction is called acidic dehydration of alcohols.
</Step>
<Step>
An alkyne can be partly reduced to an alkene by adding one molecule of hydrogen. With Lindlar's catalyst, both hydrogens add to the same side of the triple bond, so the alkene is cis. With sodium in liquid ammonia, electrons are added first and the hydrogens end on opposite sides, so the alkene is trans. The same alkyne gives the two geometries, and only the reagent decides which one you get.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

## From alkynes

An alkyne has two π bonds, and hydrogen can be added across them one at a time. The first addition needs a catalyst, and the catalyst decides where the hydrogens go. Palladium on charcoal is the usual catalyst, but it is so active that it would add a second molecule of hydrogen and give the alkane. To stop at the alkene, the catalyst is partly deactivated with a poison such as a sulphur compound or quinoline, and the hydrogen is measured out, not taken in excess. This modified catalyst is called Lindlar's catalyst.

Take but-2-yne as the example. Lindlar's catalyst gives the cis alkene:

$$
\ce{CH3C#CCH3 + H2 ->[Lindlar catalyst] CH3CH=CHCH3}
$$

Sodium dissolved in liquid ammonia works by a different route. It adds electrons to the triple bond one at a time, and the anion that forms settles into the arrangement with the two larger groups on opposite sides. Protons from the ammonia then complete the reduction, and the result is the trans alkene. Both reagents start from the same alkyne, so the choice of reagent is the whole difference between the two products.

## From alkyl halides

When an alkyl halide is heated with alcoholic potassium hydroxide, the molecule loses a hydrogen and a halogen acid, and an alkene forms. Chemists call this dehydrohalogenation. The hydrogen comes from a carbon next to the one that carries the halogen, so the step is a β-elimination.

The rate depends on two things. For the halogen, the rate follows the order iodine > bromine > chlorine, because the weaker the C–X bond, the easier it breaks. For the alkyl group, the order is tertiary > secondary > primary. Some alkyl halides have more than one β-carbon, so more than one alkene can form. Take 2-bromobutane:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CHBrCH3 + KOH ->[\Delta] CH3CH=CHCH3 + KBr + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CHBrCH3 + KOH ->[\Delta] CH2=CHCH2CH3 + KBr + H2O}
$$

Both alkenes can form, and NCERT gives the rate order but no major product, so neither is named major here. The simulation below shows every alkene that each starting compound can give.

## From vicinal dihalides

A vicinal dihalide has two halogens on adjacent carbons. Zinc metal takes both halogens and leaves as zinc halide, and the two carbons that they were bonded to form the double bond. This reaction is called dehalogenation:

$$
\ce{CH2BrCH2Br + Zn -> CH2=CH2 + ZnBr2}
$$

The rule is strict. The halogens must be on neighbouring carbons, because the two carbons have to be able to meet. A dihalide with the halogens far apart does not give an alkene with zinc.

## From alcohols by acidic dehydration

Alcohols are hydroxy derivatives of alkanes. When an alcohol is heated with concentrated sulphuric acid, water is removed and an alkene forms. Because the water leaves in the presence of an acid, the process is called acidic dehydration. It is a β-elimination too: the –OH group leaves from the α-carbon and a β-hydrogen goes with it.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2OH ->[conc. H2SO4][\Delta] CH2=CH2 + H2O}
$$

Ethanol gives ethene in this way, which is the same gas that ripens fruit in the Hook. An alcohol with more than one β-carbon, such as 2-methylbutan-2-ol, can give more than one alkene.

<KeyIdeas>
- Every alkene from a halide or an alcohol is made by removing atoms from two neighbouring carbons, so a β-hydrogen is needed. A carbon with no hydrogen next to the leaving group cannot give an alkene by this route.
- In dehydrohalogenation, the hydrogen leaves from a β-carbon and the halogen from the α-carbon, and the π bond forms between them. Iodine leaves faster than bromine, and bromine faster than chlorine; tertiary halides react faster than primary ones.
- A vicinal dihalide with zinc gives an alkene with both halogens removed. The halogens must be on adjacent carbons.
- Acidic dehydration removes water from an alcohol with concentrated sulphuric acid and heat. Ethanol gives ethene.
- Partial reduction of an alkyne with Lindlar's catalyst gives the cis alkene. Sodium in liquid ammonia gives the trans alkene. The reagent, not the starting compound, decides the geometry.
</KeyIdeas>
