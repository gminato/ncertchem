## Alkynes (9.4)

Alkynes are unsaturated hydrocarbons, like alkenes, but they carry a triple bond. Every alkyne has at least one carbon–carbon triple bond, and the general formula is $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}$. Each triple bond removes two more hydrogen atoms than a double bond does, so an alkyne has fewer hydrogens than the alkane with the same number of carbons.

The first member of the series is ethyne, $\ce{HC#CH}$, which is known more widely as acetylene. Its flame is the reason it matters outside the laboratory. When acetylene is mixed with oxygen and burnt, the oxyacetylene flame is hot enough to cut and join metal, and gas welders still use it. Alkynes are also starting materials for many other organic compounds, so the chemistry of the triple bond is worth learning in its own right.

## Nomenclature and isomerism (9.4.1)

In the common system, alkynes are named as derivatives of acetylene. In the IUPAC system, they take the name of the alkane with the same carbons, and the ending *-ane* is replaced by *-yne*. The position of the triple bond is given by the lower number of its two carbons. Ethyne and propyne have one structure each, while $\ce{C4H6}$ has two: but-1-yne, with the triple bond at the end of the chain, and but-2-yne, with it in the middle.

The two butynes are position isomers, because the carbon skeleton is the same and only the place of the triple bond changes. Next come the $\ce{C5H8}$ alkynes. There are three of them, and the list below gives each structure with its IUPAC name.

- $\ce{HC#C-CH2-CH2-CH3}$: pent-1-yne, with the triple bond at the end of a straight chain.
- $\ce{CH3-C#C-CH2-CH3}$: pent-2-yne, with the triple bond one carbon in from the end.
- $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-C#CH}$: 3-methylbut-1-yne, with a methyl branch on the chain.

Pent-1-yne and pent-2-yne share a five-carbon straight chain and differ only in the triple bond, so they are position isomers. The branched 3-methylbut-1-yne has only four carbons in its longest chain, so it is a chain isomer of the other two. ## Structure of the triple bond (9.4.2)

Ethyne is the simplest alkyne, and its structure follows from hybridisation. Each carbon uses two of its orbitals as sp hybrids. One sp hybrid of each carbon overlaps head on with the other carbon to form a C–C σ bond, and the other overlaps with a 1s orbital of hydrogen to form a C–H σ bond. The two sp hybrids on each carbon point in opposite directions, so the bond angle H–C–C is 180°, and the molecule is linear.

Ethyne is $\ce{H-C#C-H}$. Each carbon has two sp hybrids, one for the C–C σ bond and one for the C–H σ bond, so the molecule is a straight line. Each carbon also keeps two unhybridised p orbitals. They are perpendicular to the C–C axis and to each other, so they point in two directions at right angles. Sideways overlap of one pair of p orbitals gives one π bond, and the other pair gives a second π bond.
</Step>
<Step>
Both π bonds now sit on the same axis. Their electron density joins into a cylinder that wraps the C–C line. Seen from the end, the cloud looks the same in every direction round the axis, so it is cylindrically symmetrical. A triple bond is therefore one σ bond and two π bonds, and this exposed π cloud is the part that electrophiles attack.
</Step>
<Step>

## Preparation of alkynes (9.4.3)

There are two standard routes to ethyne and its relatives, and both are worth knowing.

**From calcium carbide.** On an industrial scale, ethyne is made by treating calcium carbide with water. Calcium carbide is prepared from quicklime, which is produced by heating limestone:

$$
\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}
$$

$$
\ce{CaO + 3C -> CaC2 + CO}
$$

Water then reacts with the carbide to give ethyne and calcium hydroxide:

$$
\ce{CaC2 + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + C2H2}
$$

Calcium carbide is an ionic compound that holds the carbide ion $\ce{C2^{2-}}$, and water hydrolyses it to ethyne. This is also why a wet lump of carbide releases a gas as soon as it meets moisture in a sack of fruit.

**From vicinal dihalides.** A vicinal dihalide has its two halogen atoms on neighbouring carbons. The route takes two eliminations, and each one needs a different reagent. Take 2,3-dibromobutane, $\ce{CH3CHBrCHBrCH3}$, as the example.

In the first step, alcoholic potassium hydroxide removes one molecule of hydrogen bromide. The hydrogen comes from the carbon that carries the second bromine, so that bromine stays on the carbon of the new double bond. The product is an alkenyl (vinylic) bromide, 2-bromobut-2-ene:

$$
\ce{CH3CHBrCHBrCH3 + KOH -> CH3CH=CBrCH3 + KBr + H2O}
$$

In the second step, sodamide, $\ce{NaNH2}$, removes the second molecule of hydrogen bromide. The hydrogen comes from the carbon that is already double-bonded to the vinylic carbon, which turns the double bond into a triple bond and gives but-2-yne:

$$
\ce{CH3CH=CBrCH3 + NaNH2 -> CH3C#CCH3 + NaBr + NH3}
$$

The alkyne is therefore reached in two steps, and the intermediate is the alkenyl halide that NCERT names. Each equation is balanced, and the reagents change from alcoholic KOH to NaNH₂ between the two steps.

<KeyIdeas>
- Alkynes have the general formula $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}$. A triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds, and its carbons are sp hybridised, so the H–C≡C angle is 180°.
- Name an alkyne by replacing *-ane* with *-yne* and give the position of the triple bond by its first carbon. Position isomers differ in that position, and chain isomers differ in the carbon skeleton.
- The C≡C bond (120 pm, 823 kJ mol⁻¹) is shorter and stronger than C=C (134 pm, 681 kJ mol⁻¹), which is shorter and stronger than C–C (154 pm, 348 kJ mol⁻¹).
- Calcium carbide and water give ethyne: $\ce{CaC2 + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + C2H2}$.
- Vicinal dihalides lose two molecules of HX in two steps: alcoholic KOH gives an alkenyl halide, and then $\ce{NaNH2}$ gives the alkyne.
</KeyIdeas>
