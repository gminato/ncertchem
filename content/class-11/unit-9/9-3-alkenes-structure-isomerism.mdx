<Hook>
Leave a green banana in a paper bag next to a ripe one, and within a day or two the green one softens and turns yellow. Nobody has touched it. The ripe fruit gives off a gas, and that gas is the reason. It is ethene, the simplest alkene, and it is the same molecule that the first chemists could turn into an oily liquid with chlorine. The same small double bond is doing both jobs.
</Hook>

Alkenes are hydrocarbons with at least one carbon–carbon double bond. Give an alkane with the same number of carbons and an alkene has two hydrogen atoms fewer, so the general formula is $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$. Ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$, is the first stable member of the family. Its old name, *ethylene*, and the family name, *olefins* (oil-formers), both come from its reaction with chlorine to give an oily liquid.

## 9.3.1 Structure of the double bond

The double bond is made of two different kinds of bond. One is a σ bond, formed by head-on overlap of $\ce{sp^2}$ orbitals. The other is a π bond, formed by sideways overlap of the two $\ce{2p}$ orbitals that are left over. NCERT gives the bond enthalpies as about 397 kJ mol⁻¹ for the σ part and about 284 kJ mol⁻¹ for the π part, which together make the double bond's 681 kJ mol⁻¹. The single bond in ethane is 348 kJ mol⁻¹, so the double bond is stronger than a single bond, but not twice as strong.

The π bond is the weak point. Sideways overlap is poorer than head-on overlap, so the π electrons are held loosely and sit above and below the plane of the molecule. Reagents that are looking for electrons find them easily. This is why alkenes are reactive in a way that alkanes are not, and why a reagent that seeks electrons is called electrophilic. The double bond is also shorter than a single bond: NCERT gives 134 pm for C=C, against 154 pm for C–C.

Begin with the framework. Each carbon in ethene is $\ce{sp^2}$-hybridised, so it has three equal hybrid orbitals lying in one plane, 120° apart. Two of them on each carbon form σ bonds with the other carbon and with hydrogen. What is left on each carbon is a plain $\ce{2p}$ orbital, pointing straight up out of that plane, and that is the orbital the π bond will use.
</Step>
<Step>
Now bring the two upright $\ce{2p}$ orbitals together. Sideways, they overlap above and below the plane of the nuclei, and the lobes merge into one cloud on each face of the molecule. That sideways overlap is the π bond. It is the weaker of the two bonds in the double bond, and this drawing is the one that gives the π electrons their loose hold.
</Step>
<Step>
Look straight down the C=C axis and the picture simplifies. Here the two $\ce{2p}$ orbitals are parallel, and they overlap sideways. The carbons cannot turn freely while the overlap holds, because the overlap is exactly what keeps the two halves of the molecule in step with each other.
</Step>
<Step>
Now turn one end through 90°. The two orbitals are perpendicular and no longer overlap, so the π bond is broken. NCERT's figure for the π part is about 284 kJ mol⁻¹, a large sum to pay for a turn. That restriction is the origin of cis and trans isomers, which you will meet in the last subsection.
</Step>
<Step>
Count the alkenes with the formula $\ce{C4H8}$. Three arrangements of four carbons fit the one-double-bond rule: but-1-ene, with the double bond at the end of the chain; but-2-ene, with it in the middle; and 2-methylprop-1-ene, with a branch. The names come from the library, and each one has the same formula.
</Step>
<Step>
In but-2-ene, each doubly bonded carbon carries a hydrogen and a methyl group. The double bond locks the two methyls in place, so they can lie on the same face of the molecule (cis) or on opposite faces (trans). Both have the same atoms bonded in the same order. Only the arrangement in space differs. The cis form has a net dipole, and the trans form has none.
</Step>

## 9.3.2 Nomenclature

Alkene names follow the alkane rules, with one change. The longest chain that contains the double bond is the parent, and the ending *-ene* replaces *-ane*. The chain is numbered from the end nearer to the double bond, so the double bond gets the lowest possible locant. When the double bond sits in the middle of a chain, the locant is written in front of the ending.

Take $\ce{CH3CH=C(CH3)CH2CH3}$, which the library names as 3-methylpent-2-ene. Numbered from the left, the double bond takes locant 2. Numbered from the right, it would take locant 3, and that is not allowed. The same rule applies to molecules with two double bonds. Penta-1,3-diene, $\ce{CH2=CH-CH=CH-CH3}$, has a diene ending, and both double bonds get the lowest locants the chain allows.

NCERT notes that $n = 1$ would give a molecule called *methene*, $\ce{CH2}$, which exists only for a moment and is not a stable alkene. Ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$, is the first stable member.

## 9.3.3 Isomerism

Alkenes have two kinds of isomerism. The first is structural isomerism, which is the same as in alkanes: the atoms are joined in different orders. Ethene and propene have only one structure each. From four carbons upward there is more than one. The three $\ce{C4H8}$ alkenes in the animation are chain isomers or position isomers of each other, and you can tell which is which by asking whether the chain or only the double bond has moved.

The second kind is geometrical isomerism, and it needs a double bond. Each doubly bonded carbon has two more bonds to make, and if the two groups on one carbon are the same, there is only one way to place them. If they are different on both carbons, the two arrangements are two different molecules. The arrangement with the two identical groups on the same side is the *cis* isomer, and the one with them on opposite sides is the *trans* isomer.

This is why but-1-ene has no geometrical isomers. Its first carbon carries two hydrogen atoms, so there is no way to place them that differs from another. But-2-ene has a hydrogen and a methyl on each carbon, so it has both.

Geometrical isomers can also differ in their physical properties. NCERT lists melting point, boiling point, dipole moment and solubility as the properties that change between them. For the dipole moment, NCERT gives 0.33 D for cis-but-2-ene, and says the trans form is almost zero, because in the trans isomer the two C–CH₃ bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel. In the cis isomer both point to the same side, so they add up. For solids, NCERT gives one comparison: the trans isomer melts at a higher temperature than the cis isomer. The section does not give boiling points for the pair, so none are quoted here.

<KeyIdeas>
- An alkene has the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$. The double bond is one σ bond, from head-on $\ce{sp^2}$ overlap, and one weaker π bond, from sideways overlap of the leftover $\ce{2p}$ orbitals.
- The π electrons are loosely held, so alkenes are attacked by electrophilic reagents. The double bond is 134 pm long, shorter than the 154 pm C–C single bond.
- The parent chain contains the double bond, the numbering gives the double bond the lowest locant, and the ending *-ene* replaces *-ane*.
- Structural isomers of $\ce{C4H8}$ are but-1-ene, but-2-ene and 2-methylprop-1-ene. Only a doubly bonded carbon that carries two different groups can give geometrical isomers.
- The cis isomer of but-2-ene is polar (0.33 D, per NCERT). The trans isomer has no net dipole because its two C–CH₃ dipoles cancel.
</KeyIdeas>
