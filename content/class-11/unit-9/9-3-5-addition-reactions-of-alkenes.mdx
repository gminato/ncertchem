## Physical properties

Alkenes resemble alkanes in most physical properties. The difference lies in the double bond, which shows up in their chemistry and in the isomers they can form. The first three members, ethene, propene and but-1-ene, are gases at room temperature. The next fourteen are liquids, and the higher members are solids.

Ethene is a colourless gas with a faint, sweet smell. The heavier alkenes are colourless and odourless. They are insoluble in water, but most dissolve well in non-polar solvents such as benzene and petroleum ether. As in the alkanes, the boiling point rises with every $\ce{CH2}$ group added to the chain, and a straight-chain alkene boils higher than its branched isomer, because the branched shape has less surface for van der Waals forces.

## Chemical properties: the π bond is the reactive part

The π electrons of a C=C bond are held more loosely than the σ bond beneath them. They are therefore the part of an alkene that a reagent reaches for. Most reactions of alkenes are **electrophilic additions**: an electron-poor reagent takes a share of the π pair, and the two parts of the reagent end up on the two carbons that were joined by the double bond. Some reagents add by a free-radical route instead.

### Addition of dihydrogen

Alkenes take up one molecule of hydrogen in the presence of finely divided platinum, palladium or nickel, and the product is the alkane with the same carbon skeleton. Nickel needs a higher temperature and pressure than the other two.

$$
\ce{CH2=CHCH3 + H2 ->[Pt, Pd or Ni] CH3CH2CH3}
$$

### Addition of halogens

Chlorine and bromine add across the double bond to give a vicinal dihalide, a compound with one halogen on each of two neighbouring carbons. Iodine does not add under ordinary conditions. Bromine in carbon tetrachloride is a reddish-orange solution. When it meets an alkene, the colour is discharged, which makes the reaction a simple test for unsaturation.

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 ->[CCl4] CH2BrCH2Br}
$$

### Addition of hydrogen halides and Markovnikov's rule

The order of reactivity is HI > HBr > HCl. Hydrogen halides add to alkenes by the same electrophilic mechanism as the halogens. A symmetrical alkene, one whose two double-bond carbons carry the same groups, gives one product. Ethene, for example, gives bromoethane:

$$
\ce{CH2=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CH2Br}
$$

An unsymmetrical alkene such as propene can give two products. Markovnikov's rule decides between them: the negative part of the adding molecule goes to the carbon that already carries fewer hydrogens. The Scrolly below follows that rule through its mechanism, one carbocation at a time.

<Scrolly animation="markovnikov" layout="wide">
<Step>
Propene has a C=C bond with a loosely held π pair. HBr supplies the electrophile, H⁺, because the hydrogen end of the H–Br bond is δ+. The π pair reaches for that proton. It forms a new C–H bond on the terminal CH₂ carbon, which had two hydrogens and now has three. The other carbon of the double bond is left with a positive charge.
</Step>
<Step>
The carbon left with the charge is the middle carbon. It is bonded to two alkyl carbons, so this is a secondary carbocation, a 2° cation. The six C–H bonds on its two neighbouring methyl groups can donate a little electron density into the empty p orbital. That donation spreads the charge, so this cation is the more stable of the two possible.
</Step>
<Step>
The other possibility puts the positive charge on the end carbon, which is bonded to only one alkyl carbon. That is a primary, 1° carbocation. It has fewer C–H bonds next to the charge to stabilise it, so it forms more slowly and is the minor route. The same H⁺ addition has two outcomes, and the rule is that the more stable cation wins.
</Step>
<Step>
The bromide ion, Br⁻, is the nucleophile in the next step. It pairs with the positive carbon of the 2° cation, and the product is 2-bromopropane. This is the major product. The 1° route would give 1-bromopropane, but only in small amounts. The step that forms the carbocation is the slow one, so the more stable cation is the one that forms faster.
</Step>
<Step>
Add a peroxide, such as benzoyl peroxide, and HBr behaves differently. The peroxide breaks into radicals, which take a hydrogen from HBr and leave a bromine radical, Br•. The Br• adds to the terminal CH₂ carbon, so the unpaired electron lands on the middle carbon. This secondary radical is more stable than a primary one. It takes a hydrogen from another HBr, which gives 1-bromopropane and regenerates Br•.
</Step>
</Scrolly>

### The peroxide effect

In 1933, M. S. Kharasch and F. R. Mayo found that a peroxide reverses the Markovnikov product when HBr adds to an unsymmetrical alkene. The reaction is called the peroxide effect, or the Kharasch effect, and it proceeds by a free-radical chain. For propene, the product is 1-bromopropane, the anti-Markovnikov product.

$$
\ce{CH2=CHCH3 + HBr ->[benzoyl peroxide] CH3CH2CH2Br}
$$

The peroxide effect is seen only with HBr. HCl does not show it, because the H–Cl bond is stronger, so the radical chain does not carry on. HI does not show it either, because its iodine radicals combine to form I₂ rather than adding to the alkene. This is why a peroxide changes the outcome for HBr and for no other hydrogen halide.

### Addition of sulphuric acid and water

Cold concentrated sulphuric acid adds to an alkene by the Markovnikov rule and gives an alkyl hydrogen sulphate. Propene gives propan-2-yl hydrogen sulphate, in which the group is on the middle carbon:

$$
\ce{CH3CH=CH2 + H2SO4 -> CH3CH(OSO3H)CH3}
$$

In the presence of a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid, alkenes react with water. The elements of water add across the double bond. The hydrogen goes to the carbon with more hydrogens, and the –OH goes to the other carbon, the one that is more substituted. The result is an alcohol. Ethene gives ethanol, and 2-methylpropene gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol.

<KeyIdeas>
- The π bond of an alkene is the reactive part. Most of its reactions are electrophilic additions: the reagent's two parts join the two carbons of the double bond, which becomes a single bond.
- Hydrogen adds over Pt, Pd or Ni to give the alkane. Chlorine and bromine add to give vicinal dihalides, and the disappearing bromine colour is the test for unsaturation.
- In a Markovnikov addition, H⁺ goes to the carbon with more hydrogens, so the more stable, more substituted carbocation forms and the halide joins it.
- A peroxide gives the anti-Markovnikov product with HBr only, through a radical chain, because the more stable radical is the secondary one.
</KeyIdeas>
