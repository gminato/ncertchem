<Hook>
Iron tablets prescribed for anaemia carry their iron as $\ce{Fe^2+}$, while the rust on a garden gate holds the same metal as $\ce{Fe^3+}$. Meanwhile the sodium in table salt is always $\ce{Na+}$, however it is packed. Why can a transition metal change its charge so readily?
</Hook>

## One metal, many charges

The first row of transition metals, from scandium to zinc, is known for its variable oxidation states. Table 4.3 gathers the states each metal shows in its compounds. Read the table column by column and the metals behave very differently. Scandium settles on a single $+3$. Manganese goes through six states, from $+2$ up to $+7$. The whole Section turns on explaining that shape, and the grid below draws it element by element.

## Why the middle of the row wins

Scandium and zinc are at the two ends, and each has only one state for a reason you can read off the electrons. The highest state a metal reasonably reaches follows the number of electrons in its 4s and 3d orbitals taken together:

$$
\text{highest state} = n_s + n_d \qquad (\text{Sc to Mn})
$$

Scandium gives $+3$, titanium $+4$, vanadium $+5$, chromium $+6$ and manganese $+7$, which are the electron counts of the neutral atoms. Past manganese the count keeps rising, but the reachable states drop away. Cobalt and nickel top out at $+4$, and copper and zinc at $+2$. Iron's highest state, $+6$, lies well below its eight 4s and 3d electrons, so it belongs to the same abrupt fall-off after manganese. The count is a ceiling rather than a promise: $\ce{Sc^2+}$ is virtually unknown, and $\ce{Ti^4+}$ is more stable than $\ce{Ti^3+}$ or $\ce{Ti^2+}$.

Each state is reached by removing electrons, so the metals with the most states are those whose d orbitals are partly filled. Manganese, with five 3d electrons and two 4s electrons, has the most to give. Zinc's d set is full and cannot share electrons in the same way, so it stops at $+2$. The gap between neighbouring states in the transition metals is one unit, as in $\ce{V^2+}$, $\ce{V^3+}$, $\ce{V^4+}$ and $\ce{V^5+}$. Many non-transition elements step by two, as tin does with $+2$ and $+4$.

The common species that follow are the states that remain easy to make and keep: $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, $\ce{Co^2+}$ and $\ce{Co^3+}$, $\ce{Ni^2+}$, $\ce{Cu^+}$ and $\ce{Cu^2+}$, and $\ce{Zn^2+}$. The states above them appear mainly in oxides and fluorides, where oxygen and fluorine are small and pull electrons strongly. The highest manganese oxide is $\ce{Mn2O7}$ (Table 4.6), the highest chromium oxide is $\ce{CrO3}$ (Table 4.6), and the highest chromium fluoride is $\ce{CrF6}$ (Table 4.5). The reason for that pull belongs to Section 5.

## Low states and the wider picture

Not every state is positive. Carbon monoxide can bind to a metal and hold it in a state of zero, as in nickel tetracarbonyl, $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$, and iron pentacarbonyl, $\ce{Fe(CO)5}$. Such a ligand can accept electron density back from the metal as well as donate to it. The point is only that low states exist in these complexes; the bonding is for a later Section.

The direction of change down a group depends on the block. Among the p-block elements, the heavier members prefer their lower states. In groups 4 to 10 of the d-block, the heavier members lean the other way, toward higher states. Group 6 shows this sharply: chromium's $+6$, as dichromate in acid, is a strong oxidant, whereas the $+6$ oxides $\ce{MoO3}$ and $\ce{WO3}$ are not. The $+6$ state grows steadier as you move down, and that matters again when you reach the dichromate chemistry in Section 8.

Finally, treat the electron count as a guide rather than a guarantee. It tells you the most a metal could give up, but which of those states actually survives depends on what the metal is bonded to. Table 4.3 records what happens in practice, and the sections that follow explain why.

<KeyIdeas>
- First-row transition metals show between one and six oxidation states: scandium and zinc have one each, and manganese has the most, from $+2$ to $+7$.
- The highest state a metal reasonably reaches follows its 4s and 3d electron count, up to manganese: $+3$ for scandium rising to $+7$ for manganese.
- Neighbouring states of a transition metal differ by one unit, whereas many non-transition elements, tin among them, step by two ($+2$ and $+4$).
- Down group 6 the $+6$ state becomes more stable: $\ce{Cr(VI)}$ is a strong oxidant, while $\ce{MoO3}$ and $\ce{WO3}$ are not.
- The highest states appear mainly in oxides and fluorides, such as $\ce{Mn2O7}$ and $\ce{CrO3}$; low states occur with $\pi$-acceptor ligands such as CO, as in $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$.
</KeyIdeas>
