<Hook>
Potassium permanganate, $\ce{KMnO4}$, is the deep purple crystal a careful household might use to treat a bucket of well water. Pour a little of its solution into a school lab bottle of manganese(II) salt and the liquid is almost colourless, because that metal now carries $\ce{Mn^2+}$. The same element sits at both ends of the story. How can one metal wear charges that differ so much?
</Hook>

## One metal, many charges

The first row of transition metals, from scandium to zinc, is known for its variable oxidation states. Table 4.3 in the lib gathers the states each metal shows in its compounds. Read the table column by column and the metals behave very differently. Scandium settles on a single $+3$. Manganese goes through six states, from $+2$ up to $+7$. The whole Section turns on explaining that shape, and the grid below draws it element by element.

## Why the middle of the row wins

Scandium and zinc are at the two ends, and each has only one state for a reason you can read off the electrons. The highest state a metal reasonably reaches follows the number of electrons in its 4s and 3d orbitals taken together:

$$
\text{highest state} = n_s + n_d \qquad (\text{Sc to Mn})
$$

Scandium gives $+3$, titanium $+4$, vanadium $+5$, chromium $+6$ and manganese $+7$, which are the electron counts of the neutral atoms. Past manganese the count keeps rising, but the reachable states drop away. Cobalt and nickel top out at $+4$, and copper and zinc at $+2$. Iron's highest state, $+6$, lies well below its eight 4s and 3d electrons, so it belongs to the same abrupt fall-off after manganese.

Each state is reached by removing electrons, so the metals with the most states are those whose d orbitals are partly filled. Manganese, with five 3d electrons and two 4s electrons, has the most to give. Zinc's d set is full and cannot share electrons in the same way, so it stops at $+2$. The gap between neighbouring states in the transition metals is one unit, as in $\ce{V^2+}$, $\ce{V^3+}$, $\ce{V^4+}$ and $\ce{V^5+}$. The non-transition elements step by two.

The common species that follow are the states that remain easy to make and keep: $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, $\ce{Co^2+}$ and $\ce{Co^3+}$, $\ce{Ni^2+}$, $\ce{Cu^+}$ and $\ce{Cu^2+}$, and $\ce{Zn^2+}$. The states above them appear mainly in oxides and fluorides, where oxygen and fluorine are small and pull electrons strongly. In Table 4.5 and Table 4.6 the highest entries are $\ce{Mn2O7}$ (manganese $+7$), $\ce{CrO3}$ (chromium $+6$) and $\ce{CrF6}$ (chromium $+6$). The reason for that pull belongs to Section 5.

## Low states and the wider picture

Not every state is positive. Carbon monoxide can bind to a metal and hold it in a state of zero, as in nickel tetracarbonyl, $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$, and iron pentacarbonyl, $\ce{Fe(CO)5}$. Such a ligand can accept electron density back from the metal as well as donate to it. The point is only that low states exist in these complexes; the bonding is for a later Section.

Going down a group the picture shifts. In the p-block the lower states become more typical for the heavier elements, but in groups 4 to 10 of the d-block it is the reverse. Chromium's $+6$ is a powerful oxidant in dichromate, while the $+6$ oxides $\ce{MoO3}$ and $\ce{WO3}$ are not oxidants: the $+6$ state becomes more stable going down the group. This trend will matter when you reach the dichromate chemistry in Section 8.

Finally, treat the electron count as a guide rather than a guarantee. It tells you the most a metal could give up, but which of those states actually survives depends on what the metal is bonded to. Table 4.3 records what happens in practice, and the sections that follow explain why.

<KeyIdeas>
- First-row transition metals show between one and six oxidation states: scandium and zinc have one each, and manganese has the most, from $+2$ to $+7$.
- The highest state a metal reasonably reaches follows its 4s and 3d electron count, up to manganese: $+3$ for scandium rising to $+7$ for manganese.
- Neighbouring states of a transition metal differ by one unit, unlike the two-unit steps of tin ($+2$ and $+4$).
- Down group 6 the $+6$ state becomes more stable: $\ce{Cr(VI)}$ is a strong oxidant, while $\ce{MoO3}$ and $\ce{WO3}$ are not.
- The highest states appear mainly in oxides and fluorides, such as $\ce{Mn2O7}$ and $\ce{CrO3}$; low states like $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$ occur only with carbonyl ligands.
</KeyIdeas>
