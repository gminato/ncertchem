<Hook>
Your bicycle frame, the cell in a torch and the polythene bag from the vegetable shop all owe their existence to the same middle band of the periodic table. What do these metals do that sodium or sulphur cannot, and which of their properties does each job rely on?
</Hook>

## Steel first

Iron is the workhorse. Most of the iron we mine is turned into steel, and steel is iron with small amounts of other metals stirred in. Chromium, manganese and nickel are the usual partners. Each one changes the metal in a useful way, and the change is possible for a simple reason. The atoms are almost the same size.

Table 4.2 gives the metallic radii: 126 pm for iron, 129 pm for chromium, 137 pm for manganese and 125 pm for nickel. None of these differs from iron by more than about 15 %. A chromium or nickel atom can slip into the iron lattice in place of an iron atom without bending it out of shape, so the alloy forms easily.

## Coins and the metals that do not corrode

The group 11 metals copper, silver and gold have been used for coins for thousands of years, and they still deserve the name coinage metals. They last because they are hard to attack. Copper shows the reason most clearly. Its standard electrode potential, $E^\ominus_{\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{Cu}}$, is $+0.34\ \text{V}$, the only positive value in the 3d series, so dilute acid cannot dissolve it to release hydrogen. Coins are usually alloys of these metals, not the pure metals, so the metal you hold is a mixture chosen for wear and cost.

## Pigment, batteries and the oxide that is not coloured

Titanium dioxide, $\ce{TiO2}$, is the white pigment in paints and paper. Its colour is not the usual d–d story. The titanium ion in the oxide is $\ce{Ti^4+}$, which is $[\text{Ar}]$ with no d electrons at all, so there is no d–d transition to absorb visible light.

Manganese dioxide, $\ce{MnO2}$, is the active material of many dry cells, where manganese moves between oxidation states as the cell delivers charge. Zinc and nickel–cadmium chemistry also serve the battery industry.

## Catalysts that change oxidation state

Some catalysts work by a metal that can exist in more than one oxidation state. In the Contact process, vanadium(V) oxide, $\ce{V2O5}$, passes oxygen to sulphur dioxide and is itself reduced to vanadium(IV), then reoxidised by air. The overall change is

$$
\ce{2SO2 + O2 -> 2SO3}
$$

The Wacker process uses palladium(II) chloride to oxidise ethene to ethanal. Palladium(II) takes electrons from the organic molecule and is reduced to palladium(0). The overall equation is

$$
\ce{2C2H4 + O2 -> 2CH3CHO}
$$

## Catalysts that hold on to molecules

Other catalysts work at a surface. Finely divided iron in the Haber process adsorbs nitrogen and hydrogen molecules on its surface, and the bonds inside them weaken enough to react. The overall change is

$$
\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}
$$

Nickel does the same job for hydrogen in the hydrogenation of fats. The Ziegler catalysts, made from titanium(IV) chloride with trimethylaluminium, bind ethene at a titanium site and add the molecules one after another to a growing chain. Each unit is $\ce{C2H4}$, and the polymer is written as $\ce{(C2H4)_{n}}$ with no fixed stoichiometry. Nickel complexes are also used to polymerise alkynes.

## The film in the camera

Photography uses a property of a different kind. Silver bromide, $\ce{AgBr}$, is light-sensitive: light breaks it down to silver where the film was exposed, and that silver forms the image. Here the solid is changed by the light, so the film is not a catalyst.

<KeyIdeas>
- Some transition metals change oxidation state during a reaction, so they can pass electrons along: V₂O₅ in the Contact process, PdCl₂ in the Wacker process and MnO₂ in dry cells.
- Other metal surfaces adsorb reactant molecules and weaken their bonds: finely divided iron in the Haber process, nickel in the hydrogenation of fats.
- Metals with radii within about 15 % of one another form alloys easily, so Cr, Mn and Ni can take the place of iron atoms in steel.
- Copper's standard electrode potential is the only positive value in the 3d series, which is why dilute acid does not dissolve it to release hydrogen.
- Ti⁴⁺ is d⁰, so the white pigment TiO₂ has no d–d transitions to absorb visible light.
</KeyIdeas>
