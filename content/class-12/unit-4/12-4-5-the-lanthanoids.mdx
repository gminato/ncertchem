The flint in some gas lighters is a magnesium alloy that holds mischmetal, a metal about 95 per cent lanthanoid. That row of fourteen metals sits apart at the bottom of the table, and they look and behave so alike that telling them apart is a real problem. Why should fourteen neighbouring elements be so hard to separate?
</Hook>

## Filling the hidden 4f shell

Lanthanum is followed by fourteen elements, cerium to lutetium, in which the 4f subshell fills. Every atom keeps its 6s pair, while the number of 4f electrons changes from one element to the next. A 5d electron also appears in a few of them: lanthanum, cerium, gadolinium and lutetium.

We write Ln for any one lanthanoid, and La is usually counted in. The ions tell the cleaner story. The tripositive ion of every lanthanoid is the most stable form, and in each one the 4f level holds a count that rises one step at a time. Across the row $\ce{Ce^3+}$ carries $[\ce{Xe}]\,4f^{1}$, $\ce{Gd^3+}$ carries $[\ce{Xe}]\,4f^{7}$ and $\ce{Lu^3+}$ carries $[\ce{Xe}]\,4f^{14}$. The lanthanum ion keeps $4f^{0}$, while the ytterbium ion has $4f^{13}$, one electron short of full.

## A steady shrinkage

Measure the ions rather than the atoms and a clear pattern appears. The $\ce{Ln^3+}$ radius falls from $106\ \text{pm}$ for lanthanum to $86\ \text{pm}$ for ytterbium, a total fall of $20\ \text{pm}$ across thirteen steps. NCERT does not print a radius for lutetium, so a plot stops at ytterbium. The metal radii follow the same downward drift, from $187\ \text{pm}$ to $173\ \text{pm}$, but europium's metal radius, $199\ \text{pm}$, breaks the trend.

The reason is shielding. Each extra proton pulls the electron cloud inward, and the electrons of the same subshell only partly hold that pull back. For a 4f electron that partial shielding is weaker than it is for a d electron, so the shrinkage keeps building as the series runs. This cumulative fall is the lanthanoid contraction.

## What the contraction does to the third series

The contraction is what makes the third transition series look like the second. Zirconium has a radius of $160\ \text{pm}$ and hafnium of $159\ \text{pm}$. The two atoms end up almost the same size, which is why they are found together in nature and why pulling them apart is so difficult. 

## Oxidation states beyond +3

Most lanthanoids settle into the +3 state. Now and then a +2 or +4 ion appears, and that happens mainly when the 4f level gains extra stability by being empty, half-filled or full.

Several exceptions are worth knowing. Cerium(IV), with an empty 4f level, is a strong oxidant. Its couple has $E^\ominus_{\ce{Ce^4+}/\ce{Ce^3+}} = +1.74\ \text{V}$, enough in principle to oxidise water, yet the reaction is so slow that $\ce{Ce^4+}$ is a useful analytical reagent. Europium(II), with $4f^{7}$, and ytterbium(II), with $4f^{14}$, are powerful reductants that readily drift back to the common +3 state. Terbium(IV), with its half-filled 4f⁷ level, is an oxidant. Praseodymium, neodymium, terbium and dysprosium also reach +4, but only in oxides such as $\ce{MO2}$. Samarium shows both +2 and +3.

## General character

The fourteen lanthanoids resemble one another far more closely than the transition metals do. Most show one stable oxidation state, +3, and their ions shrink so steadily that neighbours end up nearly the same size. Ordinary chemistry therefore separates them only with difficulty.

Each lanthanoid is a soft metal with a silvery sheen that dulls fast when exposed to air. They get harder as the atomic number rises, and samarium is steel hard. NCERT gives a melting range of $1000$ to $1200\ \text{K}$ for the group. Samarium is the exception that NCERT singles out, with a higher melting point. Density and most other properties change smoothly, except for europium and ytterbium and, now and then, samarium and thulium.

Trivalent ions of many lanthanoids carry colour, in solids and in solutions alike. NCERT attributes that colour to the 4f electrons, and the absorption bands are narrow. Lanthanum(III) and lutetium(III) show no colour at all. Apart from the $f^{0}$ and $f^{14}$ types, every lanthanoid ion is paramagnetic.

Energetically, the first ionisation enthalpy sits near $600\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ and the second near $1200\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, close to calcium's values. The third is unusually low for lanthanum, gadolinium and lutetium, because empty, half-filled and full f levels carry extra stability. The standard potentials of the $\ce{Ln^3+}/\ce{Ln}$ couples lie between $-2.2$ and $-2.4\ \text{V}$, with europium at $-2.0\ \text{V}$. Calcium is the nearer comparison for the early members, and the later ones lean toward aluminium.

## Reactions with common reagents

Heated gently with hydrogen, a lanthanoid combines with it. Heated with carbon, it forms carbides such as $\ce{LnC2}$. The metal burns in halogens to give $\ce{LnX3}$, and it reacts with sulphur and nitrogen to give $\ce{Ln2S3}$ and $\ce{LnN}$. Its oxide is $\ce{Ln2O3}$ and its hydroxide is $\ce{Ln(OH)3}$. Each hydroxide is a well-defined compound, not a loose hydrate of the oxide, and its basicity matches that of the alkaline earth hydroxides. Dilute acids release hydrogen from the metal.

$$
4\ce{Ln} + 3\ce{O2} \rightarrow 2\ce{Ln2O3}
$$

$$
2\ce{Ln} + 3\ce{X2} \rightarrow 2\ce{LnX3}
$$

$$
2\ce{Ln} + 6\ce{H2O} \rightarrow 2\ce{Ln(OH)3} + 3\ce{H2}
$$

## Uses

NCERT calls alloy steels for plates and pipes the best single use of the lanthanoids. Mischmetal, which NCERT describes as about 95 per cent lanthanoid metal and about 5 per cent iron, with traces of sulphur, carbon, calcium and aluminium, is another. A magnesium-based alloy containing mischmetal goes into bullets, shells and lighter flints. Mixed lanthanoid oxides act as catalysts in petroleum cracking, and some single oxides serve as phosphors in television screens.

<KeyIdeas>
- The 4f level fills one electron at a time, and every tripositive ion has $4f^{n}$ with $n$ rising from $0$ at La³⁺ to $14$ at Lu³⁺.
- The Ln³⁺ radii fall steadily, about 20 pm from La to Yb, because a 4f electron shields its neighbours only weakly. This is the lanthanoid contraction.
- Because of the contraction, Zr (160 pm) and Hf (159 pm) are almost the same size, so they occur together and are hard to separate.
- The +3 state dominates; Ce⁴⁺ (f⁰) and Tb⁴⁺ (f⁷) are oxidants, while Eu²⁺ (f⁷) and Yb²⁺ (f¹⁴) are reductants.
- The metals are soft and silvery, and their chief use, as NCERT notes, is in alloys such as steels and mischmetal.
</KeyIdeas>
