<Hook>
Inside an old incandescent bulb, a thin tungsten filament glows white-hot for hours and never melts. Yet the bright silver bead in an old fever thermometer is mercury, a metal that is liquid at room temperature. Both come from the same wide middle band of the periodic table. How can one block hold metals at opposite ends of the temperature scale?
</Hook>

Most transition metals behave the way you expect a metal to. They are strong and tough, they can be hammered into sheets or drawn into wire, they carry heat and electricity well, and they shine with a metallic lustre. Hardness and high melting points are typical too, with one exception family: zinc, cadmium and mercury break the pattern. They are soft and far more volatile than their neighbours, and manganese also departs from the usual metallic lattices at ordinary temperatures.

Two patterns explain most of what follows. The first is a bonding pattern set by the d electrons that are not paired up. The second is a size pattern set by how poorly a d electron shields the nucleus.

<Scrolly animation="dblock-melting-radii">
  <Step>
    **Electrons that can bond:** Count the 3d electrons that are unpaired in each neutral atom, from scandium to zinc. Sc has one, Ti two, V three, Cr five, Mn five, Fe four, Co three and Ni two. Cu, with its full $3d^{10}$ set, and Zn have none. Each unpaired electron can take part in the bonding between neighbouring atoms, so the metal holds together more tightly as the count climbs toward the middle of the row.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A hill across the row:** Plot the enthalpy of atomisation for each element, using the values in Table 4.2. It climbs from 326 $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ at Sc to a peak of 515 at V. It then drops to 281 at Mn, recovers to 430 at Ni and falls away to 339 at Cu and 126 at Zn. The curve joins points within the 3d series only, and the Mn dip stands out against its neighbours.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **The full shell at the far end:** Zinc has $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^{2}$, so its d set holds no unpaired electron to contribute. Its enthalpy of atomisation is the lowest in the row, and the point sits at the bottom of the curve. Cadmium and mercury show the same full $d^{10}$ pattern. This is why zinc, cadmium and mercury are not counted as transition metals, even though they are studied alongside them.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Ions that shrink:** Look at the radii of the $\ce{M^3+}$ ions in Table 4.2. They fall from 73 pm for $\ce{Sc^3+}$ to 67 pm for $\ce{Ti^3+}$, 64 pm for $\ce{V^3+}$ and 62 pm for $\ce{Cr^3+}$, with a small rise at $\ce{Mn^3+}$ (65 pm), and then reach 60 pm at $\ce{Ni^3+}$. Each extra d electron sits in a shell that shields the nucleus only weakly, so the pull on the outer electrons grows. Metallic radii follow the same trend, falling from 164 pm to 125 pm by Co and Ni.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Two points almost on top of each other:** Moving down a group you would expect each element to be bigger than the one above it. The 4d and 5d metals break that rule. Zirconium has a radius of 160 pm and hafnium 159 pm, almost the same. The 4f electrons fill before the 5d set begins, and their poor shielding pulls the whole 5d set inward. This cancels most of the size increase you would expect, so zirconium and hafnium behave alike. The full story waits for the lanthanoids later in this unit.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Packing more mass into the same space:** Density climbs across the row as the atoms get smaller and heavier. From V at 6.07 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ it rises through Cr (7.19) and Mn (7.21) to Fe (7.8), Co (8.7) and Ni (8.9). Copper ties Ni at 8.9 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$, so the rise levels off at the end of the series rather than continuing.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Unpaired 3d electrons take part in metallic bonding, so the enthalpy of atomisation in Table 4.2 peaks at V (515 $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$) and falls to 126 $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ at Zn, where the 3d set is full.
- Transition metals are hard and strong, and their high melting and boiling points follow from the same bonding; Zn, Cd and Hg are the soft exceptions.
- The radius of $\ce{M^3+}$ falls from 73 pm for $\ce{Sc^3+}$ to 60 pm for $\ce{Ni^3+}$ because d electrons shield the nucleus poorly, while the metallic radius levels off near Co and Ni.
- Zr (160 pm) and Hf (159 pm) are almost the same size because the lanthanoid contraction cancels the expected increase down the group.
- Density rises from V (6.07 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$) to Ni (8.9 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$) and levels off at Cu.
</KeyIdeas>
