<Hook>
Inside an old incandescent bulb, a tungsten filament glows white-hot for hours without melting, because tungsten melts only at about 3400 °C. Yet the silver bead in an old fever thermometer is mercury, a metal that is liquid at room temperature and melts at about −39 °C. Both come from the same wide middle band of the periodic table. How can one block hold metals at opposite ends of the temperature scale?
</Hook>

Most transition metals behave the way you expect a metal to. They are strong and tough, they can be hammered into sheets or drawn into wire, they carry heat and electricity well, and they shine with a metallic lustre. Many are also hard and melt at high temperatures. Zinc, cadmium and mercury break that pattern: they are soft and melt far below their neighbours. Manganese also departs from the usual metallic lattices at ordinary temperatures.

Two patterns explain most of what follows. The first is a bonding pattern set by the d electrons that are not paired up. The second is a size pattern set by how poorly a d electron shields the nucleus.

<Scrolly animation="dblock-melting-radii">
  <Step>
    **Three hills, one valley at each end:** Plot the melting points of the 3d, 4d and 5d series, each in its own tone. Every row climbs to a maximum near its middle: 1910 °C at V in the 3d row, 2623 °C at Mo in the 4d row and 3422 °C at W in the 5d row. Zinc, cadmium and mercury sit at the low end, at 420 °C, 321 °C and −39 °C. Join the points within each series only, so the three rows never blur together.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Electrons free to bond:** Count the 3d electrons that are unpaired in each neutral atom, from Sc to Zn: 1, 2, 3, 5, 5, 4, 3, 2, 0, 0. Every unpaired d electron can help hold an atom to its neighbours, so the count is highest near the middle of the row. Cu and Zn, with a full $3d^{10}$ set, have none. This bonding explains why melting points and atomisation enthalpies climb toward the middle of each row.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Atomisation enthalpy follows the same hill:** Table 4.2 gives 326 kJ mol⁻¹ at Sc, a peak of 515 at V, a dip to 281 at Mn, 430 at Ni and 126 at Zn. The 4d and 5d curves, read from NCERT's Figure 4.2, lie above the 3d curve mid-row: ≈ 720 kJ mol⁻¹ near Nb and ≈ 845 kJ mol⁻¹ near W. Their low ends are ≈ 110 for Cd and ≈ 60 for Hg. These are approximate readings, so treat them as shape, not exact data.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Ions and atoms that shrink:** Table 4.2 gives the 3d $\ce{M^3+}$ radii as 73 pm for $\ce{Sc^3+}$, 67 pm for $\ce{Ti^3+}$, 64 pm for $\ce{V^3+}$ and 62 pm for $\ce{Cr^3+}$, with a small rise to 65 pm at $\ce{Mn^3+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, then 60 pm at $\ce{Ni^3+}$. The metallic radius falls from 164 pm at Sc to 125 pm at Co and Ni, then rises to 137 pm at Zn. Poorly shielding d electrons let the nuclear pull grow, so the radius falls and levels off late in the row.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Two sizes almost on top of each other:** A 4d metal is bigger than the 3d metal above it: zirconium measures 160 pm and titanium 147 pm. One row further down, hafnium (5d) measures 159 pm, almost exactly the size of zirconium. The 4f electrons fill before the 5d set begins, and their poor shielding pulls the 5d atoms inward. This lanthanoid contraction cancels most of the size increase you would expect, and its full story waits for the lanthanoids later in this unit.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Packing more mass into each cubic centimetre:** Table 4.2 gives the density of the 3d metals from V to Zn: 6.07 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ for V, 7.19 for Cr, 7.21 for Mn, 7.8 for Fe, 8.7 for Co, 8.9 for both Ni and Cu, and 7.1 for Zn. The density rises steadily and levels off at Ni and Cu before Zn falls back. Sc and Ti, at the start of the row, are much lighter and are described here only qualitatively.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Melting points peak near the middle of each series: V (1910 °C) in the 3d row, Mo (2623 °C) in the 4d row and W (3422 °C) in the 5d row. Zn, Cd and Hg sit at the low end, with Hg at −39 °C.
- Unpaired d electrons take part in metallic bonding, so the enthalpy of atomisation in Table 4.2 peaks at V (515 $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$) and falls to 126 $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ at Zn, where the 3d set is full. The 4d and 5d metals lie higher.
- The metallic radius falls from 164 pm at Sc to 125 pm at Co and Ni because d electrons shield the nucleus poorly, then rises to 137 pm at Zn.
- Zr (160 pm) and Hf (159 pm) are almost the same size because the lanthanoid contraction cancels the expected increase down the group.
- Density along the 3d series rises from V (6.07 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$) to Ni and Cu (8.9 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$) and falls back to 7.1 $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ at Zn.
</KeyIdeas>
