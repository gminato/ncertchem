<Hook>
An old breathalyser tube changed from orange to green when a driver had been drinking, and a few crystals of lal dawa turn a bucket of well water pink. Both tricks depend on a metal sitting at the top of its oxidation range. What lets chromium and manganese hold such high states, and why does the colour tell you what has happened?
</Hook>

## Oxides that turn into acids

Oxygen brings out the highest oxidation states of the 3d metals. A low state gives a basic oxide, such as MnO or CrO. Raise the state and the oxide becomes less ionic and more covalent, so it stops behaving as a base. Cr₂O₃ is amphoteric, but CrO₃ is acidic and dissolves to give H₂CrO₄ and H₂Cr₂O₇. Manganese follows the same path: MnO is basic, while Mn₂O₇, where manganese is +7, is a covalent green oil that gives HMnO₄.

The pattern matters for this Section, because both of our oxidants are oxoanions: chromium and manganese in their top states, wrapped in oxygen.

## Potassium dichromate from chromite

Chromite, FeCr₂O₄, is the ore. Fused with sodium carbonate in a steady supply of air, it gives yellow sodium chromate, and iron leaves as Fe₂O₃. The equation is long enough that it needs its own line.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{4FeCr2O4 + 8Na2CO3 + 7O2} \\
&\qquad\to \ce{8Na2CrO4 + 2Fe2O3 + 8CO2}
\end{aligned}
$$

Filter off the yellow solution and acidify it with sulphuric acid. Orange sodium dichromate, more soluble than the potassium salt, crystallises from the acid. Potassium dichromate is then made by adding potassium chloride, because the potassium salt is the less soluble one and it crystallises out as orange crystals.

$$
\ce{2Na2CrO4 + 2H+ -> Na2Cr2O7 + 2Na+ + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{Na2Cr2O7 + 2KCl -> K2Cr2O7 + 2NaCl}
$$

## One chromium, two forms

Chromate and dichromate are not separate substances to be memorised. They are the same chromium(VI) in two forms, and the pH decides which one you get. Add acid to yellow chromate and it joins up into orange dichromate; add alkali to the orange and it splits back.

$$
\ce{2CrO4^2- + 2H+ <=> Cr2O7^2- + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 2OH- -> 2CrO4^2- + H2O}
$$

Look at the shapes. The chromate ion is a single tetrahedron. The dichromate ion is two such tetrahedra sharing one corner oxygen, so the Cr–O–Cr angle at that shared oxygen is 126°. Picture the bridge as one oxygen that belongs to both halves.

## Dichromate as an oxidant in acid

In acid, dichromate is a strong oxidant. Each Cr₂O₇²⁻ takes six electrons, and each chromium drops from +6 to +3. The standard electrode potential for this step is 1.33 V.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O} \\
&\qquad E^\ominus = +1.33\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

In acid, then, dichromate can take electrons from iodide (giving iodine), from hydrogen sulphide (giving sulphur), from tin(II) (giving tin(IV)) and from iron(II) (giving iron(III)). Each reductant contributes its own half-equation, and the full equation is the sum. For iron(II), the six electrons from six Fe²⁺ ions are exactly what one dichromate can take.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6Fe^2+} \\
&\qquad\to \ce{2Cr^3+ + 6Fe^3+ + 7H2O}
\end{aligned}
$$

Dichromate is also used as a primary standard in volumetric analysis, which means it can be weighed out and trusted as a pure, stable reference. The orange solution turns green as chromium(III) species form in the acid, which is why the change is so easy to see.

## Potassium permanganate from manganese dioxide

Going from MnO₂ to MnO₄⁻ takes two stages. Fusing MnO₂ with KOH in the presence of air or potassium nitrate gives dark green K₂MnO₄. That manganate then splits, in neutral or acidic solution, into permanganate and a brown MnO₂ precipitate.

$$
\ce{2MnO2 + 4KOH + O2 -> 2K2MnO4 + 2H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{3MnO4^2- + 4H+ -> 2MnO4- + MnO2 + 2H2O}
$$

On a factory scale the same manganate is turned into permanganate by electrolytic oxidation in alkaline solution. In a laboratory a manganese(II) salt can be oxidised directly by peroxodisulphate, S₂O₈²⁻, and the two-stage route is then not needed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{2Mn^2+ + 5S2O8^2- + 8H2O} \\
&\qquad\to \ce{2MnO4- + 10SO4^2- + 16H+}
\end{aligned}
$$

## Colour, magnetism and a few stable facts

Potassium permanganate crystallises as nearly black crystals with a deep purple sheen, and it shares its crystal lattice with potassium perchlorate. Water takes up only about 6.4 g per 100 g at 293 K. On heating to 513 K it breaks down and releases oxygen.

$$
\ce{2KMnO4 -> K2MnO4 + MnO2 + O2}
$$

Both ions are tetrahedral, so the difference lies in the electrons. Count the unpaired ones first: manganate has one, which makes it paramagnetic and green, while permanganate has none and is diamagnetic and purple. The intense purple of permanganate is not a d–d colour. NCERT leaves the full explanation to molecular orbital theory, which is beyond this course.

## Permanganate as an oxidant

The hydrogen ion concentration decides which product forms. In strong acid, MnO₄⁻ accepts five electrons and becomes Mn²⁺, a pale pink ion in Table 4.8. In neutral or faintly alkaline solution it accepts three electrons and ends as brown MnO₂; the +1.69 V below is for the acid form. Reduction only to manganate takes one electron, with E° = +0.56 V.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O} \\
&\qquad E^\ominus = +1.52\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{MnO4- + 4H+ + 3e- -> MnO2 + 2H2O} \\
&\qquad E^\ominus = +1.69\ \text{V}
\end{aligned}
$$

On the potentials alone, permanganate in acid should oxidise water, yet the reaction is extremely slow unless Mn²⁺ is present or the solution is warmed. Here kinetics, not only the potentials, decides what you see.

In acid, permanganate oxidises iodide to iodine and iron(II) to iron(III). It also oxidises oxalate, which needs about 333 K for the reaction to go at a useful rate. In neutral or faintly alkaline solution it oxidises iodide to iodate and thiosulphate to sulphate. Titrations with permanganate are run in acid, but not in hydrochloric acid, because HCl is itself oxidised to chlorine.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{10I- + 2MnO4- + 16H+} \\
&\qquad\to \ce{2Mn^2+ + 8H2O + 5I2}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\ce{5Fe^2+ + MnO4- + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O + 5Fe^3+}
$$

$$
\ce{2MnO4- + H2O + I- -> 2MnO2 + 2OH- + IO3-}
$$

Chemists reach for permanganate when they want to oxidise an organic molecule. The same strength explains why it can bleach fabrics such as wool, cotton and silk, and why a little of it discolours oils.

    **One pH, two shapes:** Start with a yellow solution of $\ce{CrO4^2-}$, a single tetrahedron of chromium surrounded by four oxygens. Slide the pH arrow towards acid. Two chromate ions link through one shared oxygen, and the solution turns orange as $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ forms. Slide back to alkali and the bridge breaks, so the yellow returns. The same chromium(VI) is present in both forms.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Six electrons, counted one by one:** Bring in one $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ and the 14 $\ce{H+}$ that go with it. Six electrons enter, and the two chromium atoms each fall from +6 to +3. Seven water molecules appear on the right. The orange turns green as chromium(III) species form, and six $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ions give up exactly the six electrons needed.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Purple in acid:** Permanganate is a tetrahedron with manganese at +7 and four oxygens around it, and its solution is deep purple. In strongly acidic solution, one $\ce{MnO4-}$ takes five electrons and becomes $\ce{Mn^2+}$, a pale pink ion. Watch the purple fade as the electrons arrive, leaving only the faint pink of the product.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Brown in neutral solution:** Now move the same ion into neutral solution. It accepts three electrons and ends as $\ce{MnO2}$, a brown solid that settles out of the liquid. Iodide and thiosulphate both give this brown solid when they are oxidised in neutral solution, so the brown precipitate tells you which route the reaction took.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Green or purple, one electron decides:** Compare manganate, $\ce{MnO4^2-}$, which is green with one unpaired electron, with permanganate, $\ce{MnO4-}$, which is purple with none. A sample of manganate is drawn slightly into a magnetic field, while permanganate is diamagnetic and is pushed out. Both ions are tetrahedral. The purple of permanganate is not a d–d colour.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Titration: counting electrons to find a concentration

Permanganate titrations are self-indicating. Near the end point, the first lasting excess of MnO₄⁻ keeps the flask faintly pink, because permanganate is so intensely coloured that a trace still shows against the pale pink Mn²⁺. Dichromate gives no colour end point of its own, so a titration with it needs an indicator.

Count electrons to find the ratio. Each MnO₄⁻ takes five electrons from five Fe²⁺ ions, so five Fe²⁺ are oxidised per MnO₄⁻. Each Cr₂O₇²⁻ takes six electrons, so it oxidises six Fe²⁺ ions. Take a worked case: 25.00 mL of an iron(II) solution needs 12.50 mL of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄.

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\text{MnO}_4^-) &= 0.0200 \times 0.01250 = 2.50 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol} \\
n(\text{Fe}^{2+}) &= 5 \times 2.50 \times 10^{-4} = 1.25 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol} \\
c(\text{Fe}^{2+}) &= \frac{1.25 \times 10^{-3}}{0.02500} = 0.0500\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Try the same calculation on screen. Fill the flask with 25.00 mL of iron(II) solution, set the burette to 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ KMnO₄ and run it in drop by drop. Add the oxidant in small increments. Each one decolourises until the first lasting pink appears, and the burette reading at that moment is your titre. Then switch the burette to dichromate and see how the volume changes for the same iron solution. The default values are 25.00 mL of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ Fe²⁺, which gives a titre of 12.50 mL with permanganate.

<KeyIdeas>
- Chromium(VI) forms chromate in alkali and dichromate in acid. The dichromate ion is two tetrahedra sharing a corner oxygen, with a Cr–O–Cr angle of 126°.
- Acidified dichromate takes six electrons (Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O, E° = +1.33 V), so one dichromate oxidises six Fe²⁺.
- Permanganate is made by fusing MnO₂ with alkali and oxidising to manganate, which disproportionates to MnO₄⁻ and MnO₂.
- MnO₄⁻ takes five electrons in acid (E° = +1.52 V), three to give MnO₂ (E° = +1.69 V for the acid half-equation), and one to give manganate (E° = +0.56 V). The hydrogen ion concentration decides which.
- The purple of MnO₄⁻ is not a d–d colour, and MnO₄⁻ is diamagnetic while manganate is paramagnetic with one unpaired electron.
</KeyIdeas>
