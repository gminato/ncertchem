## Three ways a substance answers a magnet

Bring a sample near the poles of a strong magnet and it responds in one of three broad ways. Some samples are drawn into the field, some are pushed slightly out of it, and a few are pulled so hard that they cling to the magnet. These are the paramagnetic, diamagnetic and ferromagnetic responses. The difference lies in the electrons, not in the metal as a lump.

Everything that matters comes from the arrangement of electrons inside each atom or ion. The next Scrolly follows the three responses, the origin of the pull, and the formula that links the pull to a count of electrons.

  <Step>
    **Three responses:** A diamagnetic sample has all its electrons paired and is weakly pushed out of the field. A paramagnetic sample has unpaired electrons and is drawn into the field. A ferromagnetic sample is drawn in very strongly, and it is best thought of as an extreme case of paramagnetism. Many transition metal ions are paramagnetic.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Why an electron acts as a magnet:** Every electron spins, and the spin makes it a tiny magnet. Where two electrons occupy one orbital, their spins point opposite ways and the two magnets null each other, so the ion keeps a net moment only from its unpaired electrons. Orbital motion is the other possible source of a moment, but in a compound it is largely switched off for these first-row 3d ions, which leaves the spins to decide the response.
  </Step>

    **Counting into a number:** Take $\ce{Cr^3+}$, with configuration $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{3}$. Its three d electrons sit in three separate orbitals, so $n = 3$. The spin-only moment is $\mu = \sqrt{3 \times 5} = \sqrt{15} = 3.87\ \text{BM}$. A single unpaired electron gives $\sqrt{3} = 1.73\ \text{BM}$, so the moment grows as the count grows.
  </Step>

## Counting the unpaired electrons

The spin-only formula is written

$$
\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}
$$

where $n$ is the number of unpaired electrons and $\mu$ is the moment in Bohr magnetons. The unit BM is a fixed unit of magnetic moment, so the number that comes out can be compared directly from one ion to another. The formula does not ask which metal the ion came from; it asks only how many electrons are unpaired.

To use it, first write the configuration of the ion, remove the $4s$ electrons first when the ion is formed, and then fill the $3d$ orbitals one electron at a time, obeying Hund's rule. Count the singly occupied orbitals. That count is $n$.

Try the count on a simple case. A $\ce{Ti^3+}$ ion has $3d^{1}$, with one unpaired electron, so $\mu = \sqrt{1 \times 3} = 1.73\ \text{BM}$.

## Reading the moment from the ion

The count of unpaired electrons does not depend on the metal alone, but on how many d electrons the ion holds. For a 3d ion in water (a high spin aqua ion) the rule is simple. For $d^{n}$ with $n \le 5$ every electron enters its own orbital first, so the number of unpaired electrons equals $n$. For $d^{n}$ with $n \ge 5$ the orbitals fill up, and the number unpaired equals $10 - n$. The count therefore rises to five at $d^{5}$ and falls to zero at $d^{10}$, which gives the hill you saw in the Scrolly.

This is why a zinc ion, with a full $3d^{10}$ shell, is diamagnetic, while iron(II) with $3d^{6}$ carries four unpaired electrons and is paramagnetic. The same count also explains why $\ce{Ni^2+}$ and $\ce{Cu^2+}$ sit near the bottom of the hill.

## Measured moments and the gap

The moments in Table 4.7 are measured, mostly on hydrated ions in solution or in solid salts. For many 3d ions the measured value is close to the spin-only one. The titanium(III) value is 1.75 BM against 1.73 BM calculated, and the copper(II) range of 1.8 to 2.2 BM lies just above 1.73 BM. The nickel(II) range is 2.9 to 3.4 BM, which sits above the calculated 2.83 BM for two unpaired electrons.

Some ions drift further away. The iron(II) range of 5.3 to 5.5 BM is well above the spin-only 4.90 BM. The formula is therefore a clear first estimate, and the gap tells you that it is not the whole story. Use the measured values to check your count, not to replace it.

<KeyIdeas>
- Diamagnetic substances have all electrons paired and are weakly pushed out of a field; paramagnetic substances have unpaired electrons and are drawn in; ferromagnetism is an extreme form of paramagnetism.
- Each unpaired electron's spin gives a small magnetic moment, and paired electrons cancel one another out.
- The spin-only formula $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}$ turns the count $n$ into a moment: one unpaired electron gives $1.73\ \text{BM}$.
- High spin 3d aqua ions have $n$ unpaired electrons for $d^{n}$ with $n \le 5$ and $10 - n$ for $n \ge 5$, so the moment rises to $5.92\ \text{BM}$ at $d^{5}$ and falls to zero at $d^{10}$.
- Measured moments are close to the spin-only values for 3d ions, but not identical; the $\ce{Ni^2+}$ range of 2.9 to 3.4 BM sits above the calculated 2.83 BM.
</KeyIdeas>
