## A colour made by one electron's jump

Shine white light through a solution of a transition metal salt and part of it goes missing. The eye sees whatever is left over. For a transition metal ion the missing light is usually taken up by a single d electron that moves from one d orbital to another, slightly higher, d orbital. The energy gap between the two is small enough that visible light supplies it, so the ion absorbs one band of the spectrum and passes the rest.

The colour you see is therefore not the colour that was absorbed. It is the complementary colour, the hue that sits opposite the absorbed one on a colour wheel. A solution that looks blue has taken orange light out of the beam.

## What the ligand changes

The size of the energy gap depends on what surrounds the metal ion. Table 4.8 lists the colours of ions that are surrounded by water molecules. The same metal in a different surrounding gives a different gap, and so a different absorbed band and a different colour. This is why one metal can appear in a whole range of colours depending on the salt you make.

The table is a good place to see the pattern: $\ce{Ti^3+}$ is purple, $\ce{V^3+}$ is green, $\ce{V^2+}$ is violet, $\ce{Cr^2+}$ is blue, $\ce{Mn^2+}$ is pink, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ is yellow, $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is green, $\ce{Ni^2+}$ is green, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ is blue. For cobalt, $\ce{Co^3+}$ is blue and $\ce{Co^2+}$ is pink.

## When an ion stays colourless

Two cases take the colour away. An ion with no d electrons at all, such as $\ce{Sc^3+}$ or $\ce{Ti^4+}$, has nothing to jump, so every colour passes through. An ion with a completely full d set, such as $\ce{Zn^2+}$, has no empty level to jump into, and the result is the same. Both are colourless in water.

Permanganate and dichromate ions are also strongly coloured, but their colour does not come from the d–d jumps described here, so this account does not explain it.

## Catalysts that change their minds

Transition metals speed up many reactions, and the d shell helps in two ways. First, a metal surface binds reactant molecules by using its 3d and 4s electrons. This brings the reactants close together on the surface and loosens the bonds inside them. Second, a transition metal ion can change its oxidation state, so it can hand electrons from one reactant to another. Vanadium(V) oxide does this work in the Contact process, finely divided iron in the Haber process, and nickel in the hydrogenation of oils.

A clear example is the reaction of iodide with peroxodisulphate:

$$
\ce{2I^- + S2O8^2- -> I2 + 2SO4^2-}
$$

This reaction is slow when the two ions meet alone. Iron(III) provides a faster route, in two steps that each pass an electron along:

$$
\ce{2Fe^3+ + 2I^- -> 2Fe^2+ + I2}
$$

$$
\ce{2Fe^2+ + S2O8^2- -> 2Fe^3+ + 2SO4^2-}
$$

The iron ion goes from +3 to +2 and back to +3, so the catalyst is not used up. The standard potential of the $\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}$ couple is +0.77 V (Table 4.2), which is the number that makes iron a convenient go-between for electrons.

## Complexes, in brief

Transition metal ions readily bind anions or neutral molecules. Their small size and high charge pull ligands close, and their empty d orbitals can accept lone pairs from those ligands. The result is a complex, and many of the colours above belong to complexes of this kind. How complexes are built and named is the subject of Unit 5, so here it is enough to notice the link: the ligand sets the colour.

## Interstitial compounds

A metal lattice has small holes between its atoms. When hydrogen, carbon or nitrogen is heated with a metal, those small atoms can sit in the holes without pushing the metal atoms apart. The compounds that result are called interstitial compounds, and examples include $\ce{TiC}$, $\ce{Mn4N}$, $\ce{Fe3H}$, $\ce{VH_{0.56}}$ and $\ce{TiH_{1.7}}$.

Their composition is not fixed, and the ratios do not correspond to any ordinary oxidation state of the metal. They also have properties that set them apart from the metals they came from. They melt at higher temperatures, they are very hard, they still conduct electricity like metals, and they are chemically inert.

## Alloys

An alloy is a blend of metals, usually made by melting the components together. In many alloys the atoms of one metal are scattered at random among those of the other, forming a solid solution. Such mixing happens most easily when the atoms are of similar size, with metallic radii within about 15 % of each other. Transition metals are often close in size, which is why they blend so readily.

Table 4.2 shows the point. Iron has a metallic radius of 126 pm and manganese 137 pm, a difference of about 9 % from the iron value. Iron, chromium, vanadium, tungsten, molybdenum and manganese go into steels and stainless steel. Alloys of transition metals with non-transition metals, such as brass (copper and zinc) and bronze (copper and tin), are also important in industry.

<KeyIdeas>
- A d–d jump absorbs one band of visible light in a transition metal ion, and the colour you see is the complementary colour of that band.
- The energy gap depends on the ligands, so the same metal can show different colours in different surroundings.
- Ions with no d electrons (such as $\ce{Ti^4+}$) or a full d shell (such as $\ce{Zn^2+}$) have no d–d jump and look colourless in water.
- Variable oxidation states and metal surfaces let transition metals catalyse reactions; iron(III) carries electrons between iodide and peroxodisulphate.
- Small atoms fit into metal lattices as interstitial compounds, and metals of similar size (within about 15 %) form alloys.
</KeyIdeas>
