The reactors at Kudankulam, in Tamil Nadu, run on fuel made from uranium. Uranium can show an oxidation state as high as +6, yet most lanthanoids stop at +3. Why does the row just below the lanthanoids behave so differently?

## The row below the lanthanoids

The actinoids are the fourteen elements from thorium ($Z = 90$) to lawrencium ($Z = 103$). They fill the second inner transition row, just as the lanthanoids fill the first. The lanthanoid row adds electrons to the $4f$ subshell. The actinoid row adds them to $5f$, and that one change in shell brings a surprising amount of new chemistry.

<Scrolly animation="actinoid-comparison">
  <Step>
    **Every member is radioactive:** None of the fourteen is stable. The early members decay slowly enough to be handled in bulk, while the later ones decay so quickly that they have been made only in tiny amounts in the laboratory. That makes the late actinoids very hard to study, and it is one reason their chemistry is still thin.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Electrons shared out between 5f and 6d:** Every actinoid keeps a $7s^{2}$ pair in its ground state. Thorium is the one case with no $5f$ electron, $6d^{2}7s^{2}$. From protactinium onwards the $5f$ subshell takes the new electrons, as in $5f^{2}6d^{1}7s^{2}$ for Pa and $5f^{3}6d^{1}7s^{2}$ for U. Americium is $[\ce{Rn}]\,5f^{7}7s^{2}$, and curium picks up a $6d$ electron as well. The $5f$ subshell is complete at lawrencium.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Radii that shrink:** Plot the $\ce{M^3+}$ radii from Table 4.10. The actinium ion is $111\ \text{pm}$ across and the californium ion is $98\ \text{pm}$. The textbook calls this fall the actinoid contraction, and it says the fall is steeper from one element to the next than the lanthanoid contraction. Check the printed figures: from Ce to Yb the lanthanoid $\ce{M^3+}$ radii fall by about $1.4\ \text{pm}$ per step, while from U to Cf the actinoid radii fall by about $0.8\ \text{pm}$ per step.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A wider ladder of states:** The lanthanoids sit almost entirely at +3, and only five of them, Ce, Pr, Nd, Tb and Dy, reach +4. The actinoids climb further. Thorium stops at +4, protactinium reaches +5, and uranium reaches +6. Neptunium and plutonium both reach +7. Past plutonium the ceiling drops: americium reaches +6, curium and berkelium stop at +4, and from californium on the only state is +3.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Exposed 5f electrons:** The $5f$ orbitals look much like the $4f$ orbitals in shape, but they reach further out. Because they penetrate the inner core less, they feel less of the nuclear charge and are held less firmly, so they are more available for bonding. That helps explain the extra oxidation states. Early actinoid ionisation enthalpies are lower than those of the early lanthanoids, although the actinoid values are not known precisely. The $\ce{M^3+}$ and $\ce{M^4+}$ ions tend to hydrolyse in water.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Metals and reactions

The actinoid metals are all silvery in appearance, yet they adopt several different crystal structures, because their metallic radii vary irregularly. Finely divided, they are very reactive. Boiling water gives a mixture of oxide and hydride, and most non-metals combine with them at moderate temperatures. Hydrochloric acid attacks all of them. Most are only slightly affected by nitric acid, because a protective oxide layer forms, and alkalis have no effect at all.

The magnetic behaviour is harder to read than in the lanthanoids. Susceptibility follows the number of unpaired $5f$ electrons in a similar pattern, but the lanthanoid values are the larger ones. Where the two rows look alike, the resemblance comes late in the actinoid series. Even the early actinoids share the gradual, steady change in properties that the lanthanoids show, and that change does not involve a shift in oxidation state.

<KeyIdeas>
- Every actinoid keeps a $7s^{2}$ pair. Thorium is the only one whose ground state has no $5f$ electron, $6d^{2}7s^{2}$, and the $5f$ and $6d$ levels lie close enough that the actinoids share electrons between them.
- The $\ce{M^3+}$ radii fall from $111\ \text{pm}$ for actinium to $98\ \text{pm}$ for californium, and the printed values alone do not settle how steep that fall is compared with the lanthanoids.
- The actinoids reach $+7$ in the first half of the series (neptunium and plutonium), whereas the lanthanoids top out at $+4$ (cerium, praseodymium, neodymium, terbium and dysprosium). Both rows prefer $+3$.
- The $5f$ electrons are less buried than the $4f$ electrons, so they take part in bonding more readily. The $\ce{M^3+}$ and $\ce{M^4+}$ ions hydrolyse in water.
</KeyIdeas>
