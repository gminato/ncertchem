<Hook>
The reactors at Kudankulam, in Tamil Nadu, run on fuel made from uranium. Uranium can show an oxidation state as high as +6, yet most lanthanoids stop at +3. Why does the row just below the lanthanoids behave so differently?
</Hook>

## The row below the lanthanoids

The actinoids are the fourteen elements from thorium ($Z = 90$) to lawrencium ($Z = 103$). They fill the second inner transition row, just as the lanthanoids fill the first. The lanthanoid row adds electrons to the $4f$ subshell. The actinoid row adds them to $5f$, and that one change in shell brings a surprising amount of new chemistry.

<Scrolly animation="actinoid-comparison">
  <Step>
    **Every member is radioactive:** None of the fourteen is stable. The earlier members decay comparatively slowly, whereas the later ones decay so fast that only minute quantities of them have ever been made in the laboratory, which makes those later members hard to work with.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Electrons shared out between 5f and 6d:** Every actinoid keeps a $7s^{2}$ pair in its ground state. Thorium is the one case with no $5f$ electron, $6d^{2}7s^{2}$. From protactinium onwards the $5f$ subshell takes the new electrons, as in $5f^{2}6d^{1}7s^{2}$ for Pa and $5f^{3}6d^{1}7s^{2}$ for U. Americium is $[\ce{Rn}]\,5f^{7}7s^{2}$, and curium picks up a $6d$ electron as well. The $5f$ subshell is complete at lawrencium.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Radii that shrink:** Plot the $\ce{M^3+}$ radii from Table 4.10. The actinium ion has a radius of $111\ \text{pm}$ and the californium ion one of $98\ \text{pm}$. This fall is called the actinoid contraction. The textbook holds that the contraction is greater from element to element than the lanthanoid contraction, and it attributes this to the poor shielding of the nuclear charge by the $5f$ electrons. The printed radii do not clearly show that claim.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A wider ladder of states:** The 5f, 6d and 7s levels sit at comparable energies, so the electrons can be shared among them in several ways, and that is what gives the actinoids their wide range of oxidation states. The lanthanoids sit almost entirely at +3, and only five of them, Ce, Pr, Nd, Tb and Dy, reach +4. The actinoids climb further. Thorium stops at +4, protactinium reaches +5, and uranium reaches +6. Neptunium and plutonium both reach +7. Past plutonium the ceiling drops: americium reaches +6, curium and berkelium stop at +4, and from californium on the only state is +3.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Exposed 5f electrons:** The $5f$ orbitals look much like the $4f$ orbitals in shape, but they reach further out. Because they penetrate the inner core less, they feel less of the nuclear charge and are held less firmly, so they are more available for bonding. That helps explain the extra oxidation states. The first few actinoids need less energy to lose an electron than the first few lanthanoids do, but the actinoid figures are not known with any precision. The $\ce{M^3+}$ and $\ce{M^4+}$ ions tend to hydrolyse in water.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Metals and reactions

Irregular metallic radii mean that the actinoid metals, though alike in colour, do not share one crystal structure. Ground into fine powder, they react readily. Acids and water sort them out: hydrochloric acid attacks every one, nitric acid barely touches most because an oxide skin shields them, and alkalis leave them untouched. Heat changes the picture again, since boiling water turns them into a mixture of oxide and hydride and most non-metals react with them at moderate temperatures.

Magnetic susceptibility is harder to interpret than in the lanthanoids. The lanthanoid values are the larger of the two, and the actinoids resemble the lanthanoids only towards the end of their row.

<KeyIdeas>
- Every actinoid keeps a $7s^{2}$ pair. Thorium is the only one whose ground state has no $5f$ electron, $6d^{2}7s^{2}$, and the $5f$ and $6d$ levels lie close enough that the actinoids share electrons between them.
- The $\ce{M^3+}$ radii fall from $111\ \text{pm}$ for actinium to $98\ \text{pm}$ for californium, and the printed values alone do not settle how steep that fall is compared with the lanthanoids.
- The actinoids reach $+7$ in the first half of the series (neptunium and plutonium), whereas the lanthanoids top out at $+4$ (cerium, praseodymium, neodymium, terbium and dysprosium). Both rows prefer $+3$.
- The $5f$ electrons are less buried than the $4f$ electrons, so they take part in bonding more readily. The $\ce{M^3+}$ and $\ce{M^4+}$ ions hydrolyse in water.
</KeyIdeas>
