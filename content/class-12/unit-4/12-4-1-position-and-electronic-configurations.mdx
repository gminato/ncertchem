<Hook>
Your steel tiffin, a copper kalash and a gold earring all come from one wide band in the middle of the periodic table. Why does that band behave so differently from sodium or sulphur?
</Hook>

## A wide band in the middle

Find the centre of the periodic table and you will see ten columns, groups 3 to 12, sitting between the s-block on the left and the p-block on the right. These are the d-block elements. Their atoms are filling the d orbitals of the shell just inside the outermost one, so the band comes in four rows: the 3d, 4d, 5d and 6d series. Every row holds ten elements, forty in all.

The f-block is kept in a separate panel below. Its two rows fill the 4f and 5f orbitals. The lanthanoids run from cerium to lutetium, and the actinoids from thorium to lawrencium. Lanthanum and actinium, the first members of the 5d and 6d rows, sit in group 3 above that panel, so they count as d-block elements here.

## What makes a metal a transition metal

The word "transition" is an old one. It was chosen because these metals seemed to bridge the chemistry of the s-block and the p-block. The modern test, set by IUPAC, is about electrons instead: a metal is a transition metal when it has an incomplete d subshell, either in the neutral atom or in one of its ions.

By that test, scandium counts, since its atom is $3d^{1}4s^{2}$. Titanium counts too, because $\ce{Ti^3+}$ still keeps one d electron. Zinc, cadmium and mercury do not. Their d subshells are full, $3d^{10}$, $4d^{10}$ and $5d^{10}$, both in the atom and in their usual oxidation states. They close off their series, so their chemistry is studied alongside the transition metals, but they are not called transition metals themselves.

## The general rule and its exceptions

The outer electrons of a d-block atom follow a general pattern, written as

$$
(n-1)d^{1\text{–}10}\,ns^{1\text{–}2}
$$

The $(n-1)d$ subshell is the inner d set, and $ns$ is the outermost s pair. Table 4.1 breaks the pattern once, at palladium, whose configuration is $4d^{10}$ with no 5s electron at all.

The pattern is more than a list. Add the d and s electrons of any neutral d-block atom and you get its group number. Chromium gives 5 + 1 = 6 and copper gives 10 + 1 = 11. Palladium gives 10 from its d set alone, with no s electrons. The same sum holds for all forty rows of Table 4.1.

The pattern is not a filling order, though. Chromium is $3d^{5}4s^{1}$ and copper is $3d^{10}4s^{1}$, not the $3d^{4}4s^{2}$ and $3d^{9}4s^{2}$ that a plain order would give. The 3d and 4s levels are so close in energy that one electron prefers to move into the d set. A half-filled d⁵ set and a completely filled d¹⁰ set are also especially stable, and chromium and copper both gain from that. Table 4.1 shows the same kind of shift in the heavier rows, for example $4d^{5}5s^{1}$ for molybdenum and $5d^{9}6s^{1}$ for platinum.

## Why d electrons change the chemistry

The d orbitals sit toward the outer edge of the atom, so they feel and act on the atoms and ions around them. Once a d set is only partly filled, the element can show several oxidation states, form coloured ions, bind ligands to form complexes, behave as a paramagnet and act as a catalyst. Each of these gets a Section of its own later in this Unit. Here, notice that the elements of one row resemble each other far more than the elements of one group do. That is why the 3d row is studied first.

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- The d-block is groups 3 to 12, four series of ten elements (3d, 4d, 5d and 6d); La and Ac lead the 5d and 6d rows, and the f-block sits in its own panel.
- A transition metal has an incomplete d subshell in its atom or in one of its ions, so Zn, Cd and Hg are not transition metals, although they are studied with them.
- The outer configuration follows $(n-1)d^{1\text{–}10}\,ns^{1\text{–}2}$, and the d and s electrons together give the group number; palladium, $4d^{10}$, is the one break in the pattern.
- Cr ($3d^{5}4s^{1}$) and Cu ($3d^{10}4s^{1}$) fill against the plain order because the 3d and 4s levels are close and half and full d sets are stable.
- Partly filled d subshells are why these elements show several oxidation states, coloured ions, complexes, paramagnetism and catalysis.
</KeyIdeas>
