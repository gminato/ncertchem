<Hook>
Iron is comfortable at the +3 state, as the rust on an old gate shows. Manganese, its close neighbour, pays noticeably more energy for the same third step. Why should two metals side by side differ so much when the third electron is pulled away?
</Hook>

## Each step costs more than the last

Ionisation enthalpy is the energy needed to pull an electron off a gaseous atom. Pull a second electron off the resulting ion and you need the second ionisation enthalpy, and so on. For a metal $\ce{M}$ the first step is

$$
\ce{M(g) -> M+(g) + e-}
$$

Each element of the 3d row, from scandium to zinc, has three values in the textbook's Table 4.2. Read across the row and the first value only creeps upward, from 631 kJ mol⁻¹ at scandium to 906 at zinc. Across a period of main-group elements the first value can swing by roughly a thousand kJ mol⁻¹, but here the added electrons enter an inner d set that shields the nuclear charge, so the change is much smaller. The second and third values climb far faster. Over the same stretch IE₂ gains 499 kJ mol⁻¹ and IE₃ gains 1444. Each electron you remove leaves a more positive ion, and the next electron is held more tightly, so the gap widens with every step.

## The first electron: a small, bumpy rise

The first values are not a smooth ramp. Manganese at 717 kJ mol⁻¹ stands well above chromium at 653, and zinc at 906 jumps clear of copper at 745. The first electron leaves from the 4s level, and the 3d electrons lie closer to the nucleus, so they block only part of the growing nuclear pull on it. The atoms shrink slowly across the row, and the first values wobble on top of a slow rise. Chemically the wobble matters little. The steep climb in the later steps matters much more.

## Exchange energy and the stable d shells

Removing an electron from an ion is hard when the ion is especially stable. Much of that stability comes from exchange energy: the more pairs of d electrons that share a spin direction, the more of it the ion holds. Count those pairs and the pattern follows. In the ion $\ce{Mn^2+}$, with 3d⁵, there are 10 parallel-spin pairs. Taking away one electron leaves $\ce{Mn^3+}$ with 3d⁴, which has only 6, so a great deal of stability is lost in that step. Now take $\ce{Fe^2+}$, with 3d⁶ and also 10 pairs. Its third electron leaves $\ce{Fe^3+}$ with a half-filled 3d⁵ set, which still has 10 pairs. Nothing is lost, and the step is cheaper.

## Where the pattern breaks

The steady rise in the second and third values breaks in two places. The second value dips at manganese, because the electron removed from $\ce{Mn^+}$ is still a 4s electron, not a 3d one. The third value dips at iron, for the reason you saw in the Scrolly. The pattern peaks at the end of the row: copper's second value is the highest second value in the row, and zinc's third value is the highest third value in the row. Copper, nickel and zinc all have high third values (3556, 3402 and 3837 kJ mol⁻¹), which is one reason these metals seldom go beyond +2.

Ionisation enthalpies are only part of the story of which oxidation states are stable. Other energy terms, such as hydration enthalpy, also matter, and the oxidation states themselves are taken up later in this unit.

<KeyIdeas>
- The first ionisation enthalpy creeps up across the 3d row, from 631 to 906 kJ mol⁻¹, while IE₂ and IE₃ climb much faster, by 499 and 1444 kJ mol⁻¹.
- The 3d electrons block only part of the nuclear pull on the outgoing 4s electron, so the first values rise slowly and wobble.
- A larger count of parallel-spin pairs in the d set means more exchange energy; a step that breaks a stable set costs more, and one that keeps the same number of pairs is cheap.
- The second value dips at Mn (Mn⁺ still has a 4s electron) and the third dips at Fe (Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ keeps ten pairs), while Cu's second value and Zn's third value are the highest in the row.
- High third values for Cu (3556), Ni (3402) and Zn (3837) explain why these metals seldom form states above +2.
</KeyIdeas>
