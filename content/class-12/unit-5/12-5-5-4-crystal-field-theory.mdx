<Hook>
Two complexes of the same metal ion in the same oxidation state can differ sharply: one is strongly attracted into a magnetic field and the other is barely affected. Valence bond theory could label such pairs but could not say why the ligands behave differently. What property of a ligand decides how a metal ion arranges its d electrons?
</Hook>

## Ligands as a field of negative charge

Crystal field theory (CFT) treats the bond between metal and ligand as purely electrostatic. The metal ion is a positive centre. An anionic ligand is a point of negative charge, and a neutral ligand such as $\ce{NH3}$ or $\ce{H2O}$ is a point dipole whose negative end faces the metal. Nothing is shared and no hybrid orbitals are built.

In a free gaseous ion the five d orbitals have the same energy, so we call them degenerate. If the ion sat inside a perfectly spherical shell of negative charge, all five would rise together and stay degenerate. A set of ligands at fixed positions is not a sphere, so the d orbitals feel different amounts of repulsion and the degeneracy is lifted. This is crystal field splitting. How the orbitals split depends on the geometry of the field.

## Octahedral splitting

Place six ligands on the three axes, one on each side of the metal. The orbitals $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$ point straight along those axes, towards the ligands, so an electron in them is repelled strongly and their energy rises. The orbitals $d_{xy}$, $d_{yz}$ and $d_{xz}$ point between the axes, away from the ligands, so they feel less repulsion and sit lower, relative to the spherical-field average.

The result is two groups. The three lower orbitals form the $t_{2g}$ set and the two upper orbitals form the $e_g$ set. The gap between them is written $\Delta_o$, where the subscript o stands for octahedral. Measured from the spherical-field average energy, called the barycentre, the shifts are fixed fractions of that gap:

$$
E(e_g) = +\tfrac{3}{5}\,\Delta_o \qquad E(t_{2g}) = -\tfrac{2}{5}\,\Delta_o
$$

These fractions keep the average unchanged: $3 \times (-\tfrac{2}{5}) + 2 \times (+\tfrac{3}{5}) = 0$.

## What sets the size of the gap

The gap $\Delta_o$ depends on two things: the field the ligands produce, and the charge on the metal ion. Some ligands produce a strong field and a large gap. Others produce a weak field and a small gap. Experiments on how complexes absorb light let chemists line the ligands up in order of rising field strength. This line is the spectrochemical series:

$$
\ce{I^- < Br^- < SCN^- < Cl^- < S^2- < F^- < OH^- < C2O4^2- < H2O < NCS^- < edta^4- < NH3 < en < CN^- < CO}
$$

Iodide gives the smallest gap in the list and carbon monoxide gives the largest. Water lies in the middle, and ammonia and ethane-1,2-diamine (en) produce stronger fields than water. The series is an experimental ordering, not a derived one, so it is learnt rather than predicted. Note the pair $\ce{SCN^-}$ and $\ce{NCS^-}$: the same ion bound through different atoms sits at two different places.

## Filling the orbitals: weak and strong fields

Electrons enter the lower $t_{2g}$ level first. One electron goes into one $t_{2g}$ orbital. The second and third electrons also enter $t_{2g}$ orbitals singly, following Hund's rule, so $d^1$, $d^2$ and $d^3$ have only one possible arrangement.

The fourth electron has a real choice. It can pair up with an electron already in a $t_{2g}$ orbital, which costs the pairing energy $P$, the energy needed to squeeze two electrons into one orbital. Or it can jump to an $e_g$ orbital, which costs the gap $\Delta_o$. It takes whichever is cheaper.

- If $\Delta_o < P$, the electron goes up into $e_g$, giving $t_{2g}^{3}\,e_g^{1}$. Ligands that give this outcome are weak field ligands, and the complexes are high spin.
- If $\Delta_o > P$, the electron pairs in $t_{2g}$, giving $t_{2g}^{4}\,e_g^{0}$. Ligands that give this outcome are strong field ligands, and the complexes are low spin.

The same choice appears for $d^5$, $d^6$ and $d^7$ ions. It disappears from $d^8$ upward, because by then the $t_{2g}$ set is full and the remaining electrons must go to $e_g$ either way. For $d^4$ to $d^7$, calculations show that the strong field arrangement is the more stable one in a strong field and the weak field arrangement is the more stable one in a weak field.

## Tetrahedral splitting

With four ligands in a tetrahedron the picture is turned around. The ligands now lie between the axes, so they approach the $d_{xy}$, $d_{yz}$ and $d_{xz}$ orbitals more closely than the other two. The three-orbital set ($t_2$) is now the higher one, raised by $\tfrac{2}{5}\Delta_t$ above the barycentre, and the two-orbital set ($e$) is the lower one, lowered by $\tfrac{3}{5}\Delta_t$. The splitting is inverted compared with the octahedral case.

It is also smaller. For the same metal, the same ligands and the same metal to ligand distances,

$$
\Delta_t = \tfrac{4}{9}\,\Delta_o
$$

Four ligands instead of six and a less direct approach to the orbitals both contribute. Because $\Delta_t$ is so small, it is rarely larger than the pairing energy. Electrons spread out rather than pair, and low spin tetrahedral complexes are rarely found.

A note on labels: the subscript g marks orbitals in species that have a centre of symmetry, such as octahedral and square planar complexes. A tetrahedron has no centre of symmetry, so tetrahedral levels are written $e$ and $t_2$, without g.

<KeyIdeas>
- Crystal field theory treats ligands as point charges or point dipoles; the five d orbitals, degenerate in the free ion, split once the ligands arrive.
- In an octahedral field the $e_g$ pair rises by $\tfrac{3}{5}\Delta_o$ and the $t_{2g}$ triple falls by $\tfrac{2}{5}\Delta_o$; the size of $\Delta_o$ depends on the ligand and on the metal ion charge.
- The spectrochemical series, from $\ce{I^-}$ at the weak end to $\ce{CO}$ at the strong end, ranks ligands by field strength and is found by experiment.
- For $d^4$ to $d^7$ ions, $\Delta_o < P$ gives a weak field, high spin complex and $\Delta_o > P$ gives a strong field, low spin complex.
- Tetrahedral splitting is inverted and smaller, $\Delta_t = \tfrac{4}{9}\Delta_o$, so tetrahedral complexes are almost always high spin.
</KeyIdeas>
