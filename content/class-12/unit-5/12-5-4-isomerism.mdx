<Hook>
Solid chromium chloride hexahydrate comes in a violet form and a grey-green form. Both analyse to the same atoms in the same ratio, yet dissolve to give different colours and different amounts of free chloride. If the formula is identical, what exactly is different?
</Hook>

## What isomers are, and how to sort them

Isomers are compounds with the same formula but a different arrangement of atoms, and that difference shows up in at least one physical or chemical property. Coordination compounds are rich in isomers because a metal ion holds several ligands in a fixed geometry, and there is often more than one way to place them.

NCERT sorts the possibilities into two principal types. In **stereoisomers** the same atoms are joined by the same bonds, and only the arrangement in space differs. In **structural isomers** the bonds themselves differ. Each type splits further, giving six kinds that this section covers in turn.

| Type | Kind | What differs |
|---|---|---|
| Stereoisomerism | Geometrical (5.4.1) | Relative positions of ligands around the metal |
| Stereoisomerism | Optical (5.4.2) | Mirror-image arrangement that cannot be superimposed |
| Structural | Linkage (5.4.3) | Which donor atom of an ambidentate ligand binds |
| Structural | Coordination (5.4.4) | Which metal carries which ligands in a salt of two complex ions |
| Structural | Ionisation (5.4.5) | Which species is inside the sphere and which is the counter ion |
| Structural | Solvate (5.4.6) | Whether a solvent molecule is a ligand or free in the lattice |

<Scrolly animation="isomer-family-tree">
  <Step>
    **One formula, two branches:** Every pair of isomers starts from the same chemical formula but differs in arrangement. The family tree splits into two principal branches: stereoisomers keep the same bonds and differ only in three-dimensional space, while structural isomers differ in the bonds themselves.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Stereo branch:** Stereoisomers split into two kinds: geometrical isomerism (5.4.1), where ligands sit in different relative positions such as cis/trans or fac/mer, and optical isomerism (5.4.2), where mirror-image complexes form non-superimposable d and l pairs.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Structural branch:** Structural isomers split into four kinds (5.4.3 to 5.4.6), each defined by a different bond change: linkage swaps the donor atom, coordination swaps ligands between two metals, ionisation swaps a ligand with a counter ion, and solvate moves water between the coordination sphere and the crystal lattice.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Geometrical isomerism (5.4.1)

Geometrical isomerism appears in heteroleptic complexes, those carrying more than one kind of ligand, because the unlike ligands can be arranged in different ways around the metal. It matters mainly for coordination numbers 4 and 6.

**Square planar, $\ce{[MX2L2]}$.** Take a square planar complex with two ligands X and two ligands L, all unidentate. The two X ligands can sit next to each other, giving the **cis** isomer, or on opposite corners, giving the **trans** isomer. The classic case is $\ce{[Pt(NH3)2Cl2]}$, drawn in NCERT as Fig. 5.2. The type $\ce{[MABXL]}$, where A, B, X and L are four different unidentate ligands, gives three isomers, two cis and one trans. You can check this by fixing A in one corner and counting the distinct partners that can sit opposite it.

**Tetrahedral complexes show none.** In a tetrahedron every corner is adjacent to every other corner, so any two ligands are always at the same relative position. Swapping them gives nothing new, and a tetrahedral complex with two kinds of unidentate ligand has no geometrical isomers.

**Octahedral, $\ce{[MX2L4]}$.** The octahedron has two X positions that can be adjacent (cis) or opposite (trans), as in $\ce{[Co(NH3)4Cl2]+}$ (Fig. 5.3). The same idea carries over when didentate ligands are present. For $\ce{[MX2(L-L)2]}$, with $\ce{L-L}$ such as ethane-1,2-diamine (en), the two X ligands are again cis or trans, as in $\ce{[CoCl2(en)2]+}$ (Fig. 5.4).

**Facial and meridional, $\ce{[Ma3b3]}$.** An octahedral entity with three of one ligand and three of another, such as $\ce{[Co(NH3)3(NO2)3]}$, has a second kind of arrangement. If the three identical ligands occupy the three corners of one triangular face, the isomer is **facial (fac)**. If they lie around the octahedron's middle, in a line that follows a meridian, the isomer is **meridional (mer)** (Fig. 5.5).

<Scrolly animation="geometrical-isomer-builder">
  <Step>
    **Square planar cis and trans:** In square planar $\ce{[MX2L2]}$ such as $\ce{[Pt(NH3)2Cl2]}$, placing the two X ligands on adjacent corners at $90^\circ$ gives the cis isomer, while placing them on opposite corners at $180^\circ$ gives the trans isomer. The dashed line highlights the angle between the two X ligands in each form.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Three isomers of $\ce{[MABXL]}$:** With four different unidentate ligands around a square planar metal, fixing A at the top-left corner leaves three distinct partners that can sit opposite it: X, B or L. That produces three geometrical isomers in total, two cis and one trans.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Tetrahedron has no cis or trans:** Rotating a tetrahedral $\ce{[MX2L2]}$ complex brings it back to the same relative arrangement. As the right panel shows, all six pairs of corners are adjacent at $109.5^\circ$, so no cis or trans isomers exist.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Octahedral cis and trans:** In octahedral $\ce{[MX2L4]}$, the two X ligands can sit on adjacent vertices at $90^\circ$ (cis) or on opposite vertices at $180^\circ$ (trans). The same cis and trans pair appears in $\ce{[MX2(en)2]}$, where each didentate en ligand bridges two adjacent vertices as a curved arc.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Fac and mer:** In octahedral $\ce{[Ma3b3]}$ such as $\ce{[Co(NH3)3(NO2)3]}$, placing the three identical a ligands on the corners of one triangular face gives the facial (fac) isomer, shown by the shaded triangle. Placing them along a meridian through the metal gives the meridional (mer) isomer.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Optical isomerism (5.4.2)

Optical isomers are mirror images that cannot be placed one on top of the other. Such a pair is called a pair of **enantiomers**, and an entity that cannot be superimposed on its mirror image is **chiral**. Your hands show the idea: each is the mirror image of the other, yet a left glove will never fit the right hand.

The two forms are labelled **dextro (d)** and **laevo (l)** by what they do to plane-polarised light in a polarimeter. The d form rotates the plane to the right, the l form to the left, by equal amounts.

Optical isomerism is common in octahedral complexes with didentate ligands. The standard example is $\ce{[Co(en)3]^3+}$ (Fig. 5.6): three en rings wrapped round the metal in a propeller-like way, and the two propellers twist in opposite senses. Geometry decides which isomer is chiral. For $\ce{[PtCl2(en)2]^2+}$, only the cis isomer is optically active (Fig. 5.7). The trans isomer has a mirror plane through it, so it matches its mirror image and shows no activity.

> **Common Misconception:** Chiral does not mean the complex has no symmetry at all. It means it has no mirror plane and no centre of inversion, so it cannot be brought on top of its mirror image. A quick test is to ask whether the entity and its reflection are the same object.

<Scrolly animation="chirality-mirror">
  <Step>
    **A mirror between two propellers:** Across the central mirror line, the three didentate en rings of octahedral $\ce{[Co(en)3]^3+}$ wrap round cobalt in opposite senses: clockwise in the dextro (d) form on the left and anticlockwise in the laevo (l) form on the right.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Try to superimpose:** Sliding a translucent copy of the d isomer across onto the l isomer shows why the pair is chiral. No matter how the copy is rotated, its chelate arcs clash with those of its mirror image, so the two forms cannot be superimposed.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Polarised light:** In a polarimeter, the dashed vertical line shows the incident plane of polarised light and the solid line shows the emergent plane. A d solution rotates the plane to the right by $+\theta$, an l solution rotates it to the left by $-\theta$, and an equal mixture of d and l cancels out with zero net rotation.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Cis versus trans of $\ce{[PtCl2(en)2]^2+}$:** In the cis isomer (top), the complex and its mirror image cannot be superimposed, so it forms a chiral, optically active d and l pair. In the trans isomer (bottom), a dashed mirror plane cuts through both $\ce{Cl}$ ligands and the platinum centre, mapping the complex onto its own reflection, so it is achiral.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Linkage isomerism (5.4.3)

Some ligands can bind through more than one donor atom, and these are called ambidentate. When such a ligand attaches through different atoms in different samples, the result is **linkage isomerism**. The thiocyanate ion, $\ce{NCS^-}$, can bind through nitrogen, giving $\ce{M-NCS}$, or through sulphur, giving $\ce{M-SCN}$.

Jørgensen found the famous case in $\ce{[Co(NH3)5(NO2)]Cl2}$. It exists as a **red** form, where the nitrite ion binds through oxygen as $\ce{-ONO}$, and a **yellow** form, where it binds through nitrogen as $\ce{-NO2}$. The two forms have different colours because the donor atom changes the metal's surroundings.

## Coordination isomerism (5.4.4)

This kind needs a salt in which both the cation and the anion are complex ions of different metals. The ligands can then be exchanged between the two metal centres. In $\ce{[Co(NH3)6][Cr(CN)6]}$ the ammonia is on cobalt(III) and the cyanide is on chromium(III). In the coordination isomer $\ce{[Cr(NH3)6][Co(CN)6]}$ the ammonia is on chromium(III) and the cyanide is on cobalt(III).

## Ionisation isomerism (5.4.5)

Here the counter ion of a complex salt can itself act as a ligand, and it swaps places with a ligand that then becomes the counter ion. The pair $\ce{[Co(NH3)5(SO4)]Br}$ and $\ce{[Co(NH3)5Br]SO4}$ shows it. In the first, sulphate is inside the coordination sphere and bromide is free. In the second, bromide is inside and sulphate is free. In water the two give different ions, so different precipitation tests tell them apart: only the free ion responds.

## Solvate isomerism (5.4.6)

When the solvent is water, this is also called **hydrate isomerism**, and it resembles ionisation isomerism. The isomers differ in whether the solvent molecule is bonded to the metal or sits as free solvent in the crystal lattice. The aqua complex $\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]Cl3}$ is violet. Its solvate isomer $\ce{[Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2.H2O}$ is grey-green. One water has moved out of the sphere into the lattice, and one chloride has moved in.

<Scrolly animation="structural-isomer-swaps">
  <Step>
    **Linkage: flip the nitrite:** Around cobalt with five $\ce{NH3}$ ligands, the ambidentate nitrite ligand can attach through its oxygen atom to give the red $\ce{-ONO}$ form, $\ce{[Co(NH3)5(ONO)]Cl2}$, or flip to attach through its nitrogen atom to give the yellow $\ce{-NO2}$ form, $\ce{[Co(NH3)5(NO2)]Cl2}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Coordination: swap between two metals:** In the top pair, $\ce{Co}$ holds six $\ce{NH3}$ ligands and $\ce{Cr}$ holds six $\ce{CN^-}$ ligands. Exchanging the two ligand sets gives the bottom pair, $\ce{[Cr(NH3)6][Co(CN)6]}$, while keeping the overall atom count unchanged.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Ionisation: swap a ligand with the counter ion:** In $\ce{[Co(NH3)5(SO4)]Br}$ (left), sulphate sits inside the coordination bracket and $\ce{Br^-}$ is the free counter ion that precipitates $\ce{AgBr}$. Swapping them gives $\ce{[Co(NH3)5Br]SO4}$ (right), where $\ce{SO4^2-}$ is now free outside the bracket and precipitates $\ce{BaSO4}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Solvate: water moves across the bracket:** Violet $\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]Cl3}$ (left) holds all six water molecules inside the coordination sphere with three free $\ce{Cl^-}$ ions outside. Moving one $\ce{H2O}$ into the crystal lattice and one $\ce{Cl^-}$ into the sphere gives grey-green $\ce{[Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2.H2O}$ (right), leaving two free $\ce{Cl^-}$ ions.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Isomers share a formula but differ in arrangement. Stereoisomers keep the same bonds and differ in space (geometrical, optical), while structural isomers differ in bonds (linkage, coordination, ionisation, solvate).
- Geometrical isomers give cis and trans in square planar $\ce{MX2L2}$ and octahedral $\ce{MX2L4}$, and fac and mer in $\ce{Ma3b3}$. $\ce{MABXL}$ square planar has three isomers, and tetrahedral complexes have none.
- Optical isomers are non-superimposable mirror images (d and l), common in octahedral complexes with didentate ligands: $\ce{[Co(en)3]^3+}$ and cis-$\ce{[PtCl2(en)2]^2+}$ are chiral, trans is not.
- Linkage isomers need an ambidentate ligand, as with $\ce{-ONO}$ (red) and $\ce{-NO2}$ (yellow) in $\ce{[Co(NH3)5(NO2)]Cl2}$.
- Coordination, ionisation and solvate isomers differ in what is inside the bracket: ligands swapped between two metals, a ligand swapped with a counter ion, or water inside versus in the lattice.
</KeyIdeas>
