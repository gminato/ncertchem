## Beyond Werner's coordination theory

Werner's model accounted for primary and secondary valences and correctly deduced the spatial arrangements of many complexes. Even so, it left three fundamental questions unanswered:

1. Why do only certain elements readily form coordination compounds?
2. Why do metal-ligand bonds point along fixed directions in space?
3. Why do coordination compounds display characteristic magnetic and optical properties?

Several bonding models were developed to answer these questions, including Valence Bond Theory (VBT), Crystal Field Theory (CFT), Ligand Field Theory (LFT) and Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT). We begin with an elementary treatment of Valence Bond Theory and see both what it explains well and where it reaches its limits.

## Hybridisation and geometry in Valence Bond Theory

In Valence Bond Theory, an approaching set of ligands influences the central metal atom or ion so that it mixes its vacant $(n-1)d$, $ns$ and $np$ orbitals, or its $ns$, $np$ and $nd$ orbitals, into a new set of equivalent hybrid orbitals. Each set of hybrid orbitals points along a definite geometry in space. A coordinate covalent bond then forms when a filled orbital on each ligand donates a pair of electrons to overlap with one of these empty metal hybrid orbitals.

NCERT Table 5.2 links the coordination number of a metal centre to the type of hybridisation and the resulting spatial geometry:

| Coordination number | Type of hybridisation | Distribution of hybrid orbitals in space |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $4$ | $sp^3$ | Tetrahedral |
| $4$ | $dsp^2$ | Square planar |
| $5$ | $sp^3d$ | Trigonal bipyramidal |
| $6$ | $sp^3d^2$ | Octahedral |
| $6$ | $d^2sp^3$ | Octahedral |

Notice that coordination number $4$ has two distinct hybridisation schemes ($sp^3$ and $dsp^2$) with different shapes, while coordination number $6$ has two distinct hybridisation schemes ($d^2sp^3$ and $sp^3d^2$) that both give an octahedral geometry. Which orbital set is used depends on whether the metal uses inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals or outer $nd$ orbitals.

One conceptual point is worth keeping in mind: hybrid orbitals do not physically exist as separate entities inside an atom. Hybridisation is a mathematical combination of the atomic orbital wave functions that provides a convenient description of directional bonding.

## Orbital schemes for octahedral and four-coordinate complexes

When you know the magnetic behaviour of a complex, VBT usually allows you to work out its hybridisation and shape. The Scrolly below walks through five classic complexes from NCERT Section 5.5.1, comparing inner-orbital and outer-orbital octahedral complexes as well as tetrahedral and square planar four-coordinate complexes.

<Scrolly animation="vbt-hybridisation">
  <Step>
    **Free $\ce{Co^3+}$ ion ($3d^6$):** A free $\ce{Co^3+}$ ion carries six electrons across its five $3d$ orbitals: one orbital holds a paired set and the other four hold single electrons under Hund's rule. The $4s$, $4p$ and $4d$ orbitals are empty, so four $3d$ electrons remain unpaired and no pair of vacant inner $3d$ orbitals is immediately available for octahedral bonding.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Inner orbital octahedral complex, $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$ ($d^2sp^3$):** Under the influence of six $\ce{NH3}$ ligands, the six $3d$ electrons of $\ce{Co^3+}$ pair up into three $3d$ orbitals, freeing two inner $3d$ orbitals. Those two $3d$ orbitals mix with the $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals to form six equivalent $d^2sp^3$ hybrid orbitals, each accepting an electron pair from an $\ce{NH3}$ molecule. With all electrons paired ($n = 0$), this octahedral complex is diamagnetic and is called an inner orbital (low spin or spin paired) complex.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Outer orbital octahedral complex, $[\ce{CoF6}]^{3-}$ ($sp^3d^2$):** With six $\ce{F-}$ ligands, the six $3d$ electrons of $\ce{Co^3+}$ stay in their original arrangement with four unpaired electrons. Instead of pairing the $3d$ electrons, the metal ion hybridises its $4s$, three $4p$ and two outer $4d$ orbitals into six $sp^3d^2$ hybrid orbitals that accept electron pairs from the six $\ce{F-}$ ions, leaving three $4d$ orbitals empty. This octahedral complex is paramagnetic ($n = 4$, $\mu = 4.90\ \text{BM}$) and is called an outer orbital (high spin or spin free) complex.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Tetrahedral complexes, $[\ce{NiCl4}]^{2-}$ and $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$ ($sp^3$):** Both four-coordinate complexes use one $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals to form four $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals in a tetrahedral arrangement, yet their magnetic behaviour differs. In $[\ce{NiCl4}]^{2-}$, the $\ce{Ni^2+}$ ion ($3d^8$) retains three paired $3d$ orbitals and two unpaired electrons while four $\ce{Cl-}$ ions donate electron pairs into the $sp^3$ hybrids, making the complex paramagnetic ($n = 2$, $\mu = 2.83\ \text{BM}$). In $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$, neutral nickel has a completely filled $3d^{10}$ subshell while four $\ce{CO}$ molecules donate pairs into the $sp^3$ hybrids, making it tetrahedral and diamagnetic ($n = 0$).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Square planar complex, $[\ce{Ni(CN)4}]^{2-}$ ($dsp^2$):** Here the $\ce{Ni^2+}$ ion ($3d^8$) pairs its two unpaired $3d$ electrons into a fourth $3d$ orbital, vacating one inner $3d$ orbital. That vacant $3d$ orbital combines with the $4s$ and two $4p$ orbitals to form four $dsp^2$ hybrid orbitals (leaving one $4p$ orbital unused), each receiving an electron pair from a $\ce{CN-}$ ion. The resulting complex adopts a square planar geometry and is spin paired and diamagnetic ($n = 0$).
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

Look closely at the two cobalt(III) complexes. Both are octahedral, yet $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$ uses inner $3d$ orbitals ($d^2sp^3$) and is called an **inner orbital**, **low spin** or **spin paired** complex, whereas $[\ce{CoF6}]^{3-}$ uses outer $4d$ orbitals ($sp^3d^2$) and is called an **outer orbital**, **high spin** or **spin free** complex.

Similarly, $[\ce{NiCl4}]^{2-}$ and $[\ce{Ni(CN)4}]^{2-}$ both contain $\ce{Ni^2+}$ ($3d^8$) with coordination number $4$, yet $[\ce{NiCl4}]^{2-}$ keeps two unpaired electrons and uses $sp^3$ hybridisation (tetrahedral), while $[\ce{Ni(CN)4}]^{2-}$ pairs its $3d$ electrons to free one $3d$ orbital and uses $dsp^2$ hybridisation (square planar). By contrast, $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$ has nickel in the zero oxidation state with all ten valence electrons paired in $3d^{10}$, so it is both tetrahedral ($sp^3$) and diamagnetic.

## Magnetic properties of coordination compounds

Magnetic susceptibility experiments measure the magnetic moment $\mu$ of a coordination compound. For first-row transition metal complexes, the spin-only formula connects the magnetic moment in Bohr magnetons ($\text{BM}$) to the number of unpaired electrons $n$:

$$
\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}
$$

Working backwards from an experimental magnetic moment gives $n$, which in turn reveals whether electron pairing has occurred and which hybridisation the complex adopts.

A close look at first-row octahedral complexes splits the metal ions into two regimes:

- **Ions with $d^1$, $d^2$ or $d^3$ configurations**, such as $\ce{Ti^3+}$ ($3d^1$), $\ce{V^3+}$ ($3d^2$) and $\ce{Cr^3+}$ ($3d^3$), already possess at least two vacant $3d$ orbitals. These two orbitals combine directly with $4s$ and $4p$ orbitals for octahedral $d^2sp^3$ hybridisation without any pairing. Consequently, their coordination entities have the same number of unpaired electrons as the free ions.
- **Ions with $d^4$, $d^5$ or $d^6$ configurations**, such as $\ce{Cr^2+}$ and $\ce{Mn^3+}$ ($d^4$), $\ce{Mn^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ($d^5$), and $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Co^3+}$ ($d^6$), have at least four $3d$ orbitals occupied under Hund's rule. A vacant pair of inner $3d$ orbitals is not directly available. Pairing the $3d$ electrons to free two inner orbitals leaves $2$, $1$ and $0$ unpaired electrons for $d^4$, $d^5$ and $d^6$ respectively.

Magnetic data show that while $d^6$ ions frequently exhibit maximum spin pairing, $d^4$, $d^5$ and $d^6$ ions can each form either inner-orbital ($d^2sp^3$) or outer-orbital ($sp^3d^2$) octahedral complexes depending on the ligand:

| Complex | Metal ion ($d^n$) | Orbital type | Hybridisation | Unpaired electrons ($n$) | Spin-only $\mu$ ($\text{BM}$) | Magnetic behaviour |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $[\ce{Mn(CN)6}]^{3-}$ | $\ce{Mn^3+}$ ($3d^4$) | Inner orbital | $d^2sp^3$ | $2$ | $2.83$ | Paramagnetic |
| $[\ce{MnCl6}]^{3-}$ | $\ce{Mn^3+}$ ($3d^4$) | Outer orbital | $sp^3d^2$ | $4$ | $4.90$ | Paramagnetic |
| $[\ce{Fe(CN)6}]^{3-}$ | $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ($3d^5$) | Inner orbital | $d^2sp^3$ | $1$ | $1.73$ | Paramagnetic |
| $[\ce{FeF6}]^{3-}$ | $\ce{Fe^3+}$ ($3d^5$) | Outer orbital | $sp^3d^2$ | $5$ | $5.92$ | Paramagnetic |
| $[\ce{Co(C2O4)3}]^{3-}$ | $\ce{Co^3+}$ ($3d^6$) | Inner orbital | $d^2sp^3$ | $0$ | $0.00$ | Diamagnetic |
| $[\ce{CoF6}]^{3-}$ | $\ce{Co^3+}$ ($3d^6$) | Outer orbital | $sp^3d^2$ | $4$ | $4.90$ | Paramagnetic |

Magnetic moments also settle geometry questions for four-coordinate complexes. Consider a $3d^5$ ion such as $\ce{Mn^2+}$ bonded to four ligands, as in $[\ce{MnBr4}]^{2-}$. Coordination number $4$ permits either tetrahedral ($sp^3$) or square planar ($dsp^2$) geometry. Measuring a spin-only magnetic moment of $5.9\ \text{BM}$ confirms the presence of $n = 5$ unpaired electrons ($\sqrt{5 \times 7} = \sqrt{35} \approx 5.92\ \text{BM}$). Because all five $3d$ orbitals are singly occupied, no empty $3d$ orbital is free for $dsp^2$ hybridisation, so the complex must adopt $sp^3$ hybridisation and a tetrahedral shape.

## Limitations of Valence Bond Theory

Although Valence Bond Theory accounts well for the formation, spatial geometries and qualitative magnetic behaviour of many coordination compounds, NCERT Section 5.5.3 identifies six major shortcomings:

1. **Reliance on assumptions:** The theory rests on several unverified assumptions, such as invoking electron pairing or outer $nd$ orbital participation after the fact to fit experimental observations.
2. **No quantitative magnetic predictions:** It links unpaired electron counts to paramagnetic or diamagnetic character qualitatively, but it does not give a quantitative interpretation of experimental magnetic data.
3. **No explanation of colour:** It offers no mechanism to explain why coordination compounds absorb visible light and exhibit vivid, ligand-dependent colours.
4. **No quantitative stability predictions:** It cannot quantitatively interpret either the thermodynamic stability or the kinetic stability of coordination compounds.
5. **No exact prediction for four-coordinate shapes:** Without prior magnetic measurements, VBT cannot reliably predict whether a four-coordinate complex will adopt a tetrahedral or a square planar structure.
6. **No distinction between weak and strong ligands:** It cannot explain why ligands such as $\ce{CN-}$ or $\ce{NH3}$ promote spin pairing in a given ion whereas $\ce{F-}$ or $\ce{Cl-}$ do not.

These six gaps motivate **Crystal Field Theory (CFT)** in the next Section, where the electrostatic splitting of the five $d$ orbitals explains ligand strength, spin state and colour from a single physical picture.

<KeyIdeas>
- In Valence Bond Theory, a metal centre hybridises $(n-1)d$, $ns$ and $np$ orbitals (or $ns$, $np$ and $nd$ orbitals) into directional hybrid orbitals ($sp^3$, $dsp^2$, $sp^3d$, $sp^3d^2$, $d^2sp^3$) that accept electron pairs from ligands; hybrid orbitals are mathematical combinations of atomic wave functions rather than physical objects.
- Octahedral complexes that use inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals ($d^2sp^3$) are inner orbital (low spin, spin paired) complexes, as in $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$; those using outer $nd$ orbitals ($sp^3d^2$) are outer orbital (high spin, spin free) complexes, as in $[\ce{CoF6}]^{3-}$.
- Four-coordinate complexes adopt tetrahedral geometry ($sp^3$, as in paramagnetic $[\ce{NiCl4}]^{2-}$ and diamagnetic $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$) or square planar geometry ($dsp^2$, as in diamagnetic $[\ce{Ni(CN)4}]^{2-}$).
- For octahedral complexes, $d^1$ to $d^3$ ions always have two empty inner $d$ orbitals available without pairing, whereas $d^4$, $d^5$ and $d^6$ ions can form either inner-orbital ($2$, $1$, $0$ unpaired) or outer-orbital ($4$, $5$, $4$ unpaired) complexes; experimental magnetic moments ($\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}$) distinguish between them.
- VBT suffers from six main limitations: it relies on assumptions, lacks quantitative magnetic and stability interpretations, cannot explain colour, cannot make exact predictions between tetrahedral and square planar shapes, and does not distinguish weak from strong ligands.
</KeyIdeas>
