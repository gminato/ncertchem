<Hook>
Two bottles on a shelf read $\ce{[Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2}$ and "pentaamminechloridocobalt(III) chloride". They describe the same purple solid, yet the formula starts with cobalt and the name ends with it. Why do the two run in opposite directions, and how does a name give away the metal's oxidation state before you have counted a single electron?
</Hook>

## Why a naming system is needed

A coordination compound can hold a metal ion, several kinds of ligand, and counter ions all in one formula. Common names such as "Prussian blue" tell you nothing about what is bonded to what. IUPAC therefore uses **additive nomenclature**: the name is built by adding pieces together, each ligand as a prefix to the name of the central atom. If you know the rules, any formula can be turned into a name and any name back into a formula. This Section covers mononuclear entities, which contain one central metal atom.

## Writing a formula: from the metal outward

Think of the coordination entity as a small cluster with the metal at the heart, so the formula begins there and moves outward to the ligands. The rules for the entity, in order, are these.

1. The central atom comes first.
2. Ligands follow in alphabetical order. The charge of a ligand plays no part in where it sits.
3. A polydentate ligand is placed alphabetically too. If it is written as an abbreviation such as en, the first letter of the abbreviation decides its place.
4. The whole entity goes in square brackets, charged or not. Polyatomic ligands go in parentheses, and so do ligand abbreviations.
5. No spaces appear between the metal and the ligands.
6. If you write the entity without its counter ion, put the charge outside the brackets as a superscript, number before sign: $\ce{[Co(CN)6]^3-}$ and $\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]^3+}$.
7. In a salt the cation is written first, and the charges of cations and anions must balance.

Take $\ce{K3[Co(C2O4)3]}$ as a pattern. The three potassium ions sit outside as counter ions. Inside the bracket, cobalt is first and the oxalate ligand is in parentheses because it is polyatomic. The entity has charge $3-$ to match the three $\ce{K+}$.

> [!NOTE]
> NCERT mentions that a 2004 IUPAC draft recommends sorting ligands alphabetically regardless of charge, and NCERT's own worked formulas list ligands in the same order their names would sort. This site follows that practice in examples.

## Naming: from the ligands inward

A name does the reverse of a formula. It lists the ligands first, then the metal, so the metal ends up last before any anion. The rules, in the order you apply them:

1. **Cation first.** In a salt, name the cation before the anion, whichever of the two holds the complex.
2. **Ligands in alphabetical order, before the metal.** Ignore the multiplying prefixes when sorting. Tetraammine sorts under "a", and dichlorido sorts under "c".
3. **Ligand names.** Anionic ligands end in **-o**, as in chlorido, bromido, cyanido, hydroxido and oxalato. Neutral and cationic ligands keep their ordinary names, except four special ones: **aqua** for $\ce{H2O}$, **ammine** for $\ce{NH3}$, **carbonyl** for $\ce{CO}$ and **nitrosyl** for $\ce{NO}$. When an ambidentate ligand can bind through two different donor atoms, the bound atom is shown after a hyphen, as in **nitrito-N** for $\ce{NO2-}$ bound through nitrogen and **thiocyanato-S** for $\ce{SCN-}$ bound through sulphur.
4. **How many.** Use mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa. If the ligand's own name already contains a number, as in ethane-1,2-diamine or triphenylphosphine, use **bis, tris, tetrakis** and put the ligand name in parentheses. For example, $\ce{[NiCl2(PPh3)2]}$ is dichloridobis(triphenylphosphine)nickel(II).
5. **Oxidation state.** Give the metal's oxidation state as a Roman numeral in parentheses straight after the metal name, with no space. Zero is written (0).
6. **Metal name.** In a cationic or neutral entity the metal keeps the element's name. In an anionic entity the name ends in **-ate**, and some metals use the Latin stem: ferrate for iron, argentate for silver and cuprate for copper. In a salt with two complex ions holding the same metal, such as $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2][Ag(CN)2]}$ (diamminesilver(I) dicyanidoargentate(I)), the metal takes its plain name in the cation and its -ate name in the anion.
7. **Neutral entities** are named exactly like cations.

> [!NOTE]
> NCERT also notes that the 2004 IUPAC draft changes the ending of anionic ligands from -o to -ido, so chloro becomes chlorido. NCERT's examples already use chlorido, and so does this site.

Never state the number of cations or anions in the name of a salt. $\ce{[Co(en)3]2(SO4)3}$ is named tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III) sulphate, with no "di" or "tri" for the ions outside.

## Reading the oxidation state

The name needs a Roman numeral, so you have to work out the metal's oxidation state. The entity's overall charge is the sum of the metal's state and the ligand charges:

$$
\text{charge of entity} = (\text{oxidation state of metal}) + \sum (\text{ligand charges})
$$

First find the entity's charge from the counter ions, then subtract the ligand charges. Take $\ce{K4[Fe(CN)6]}$. Four $\ce{K+}$ means the entity is $4-$. Six cyanide ligands contribute $6 \times (-1) = -6$. So the iron must supply $+2$, and the name is potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II). Notice that the metal is named ferrate because the entity is an anion, and that "potassium" comes first because the cation is named first.

For neutral ligands the sum is zero, so the metal's state equals the entity's charge. For $\ce{[Cr(NH3)3(H2O)3]Cl3}$, three chloride ions mean the entity is $3+$, both ligands are neutral, and chromium is $+3$. The name begins triammine, then triaqua, because ammine sorts before aqua.

## Worked name-building, step by step

Here is a full run on a heteroleptic complex: $\ce{[Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl}$.

1. The bracket holds the entity, and one $\ce{Cl-}$ sits outside. The entity is $1+$, so it is a cation.
2. Ligands: four neutral $\ce{NH3}$ (tetraammine) and two $\ce{Cl-}$ (dichlorido). Alphabetically, ammine comes before chlorido.
3. Oxidation state: $x + 0 + 2(-1) = +1$, so $x = +3$.
4. A cation keeps the metal's plain name: cobalt(III).
5. The anion outside is chloride.

The name is tetraamminedichloridocobalt(III) chloride.

  <Step>
    **The formula runs outward:** In $\ce{[Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl}$, the central cobalt atom comes first inside the square brackets, followed outward by the four $\ce{NH3}$ ligands and two $\ce{Cl-}$ ligands. The square brackets enclose only the coordination entity, while the single $\ce{Cl-}$ counter ion sits outside.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **The name runs inward:** Turning the same compound into a name reverses the order inside the entity: the ligands come first as tetraammine and dichlorido, followed by cobalt(III), with the outside chloride anion named last. The central metal moves from the start of the formula to the end of the complex cation's name.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Sorting ignores the count:** Ligands are ordered alphabetically by their own names, ignoring multiplying prefixes such as tetra, di, or bis. Thus tetraammine sorts under "a" before dichlorido under "c", and dichlorido sorts under "c" before bis(ethane-1,2-diamine) under "e".
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Ligand names change by charge:** Anionic ligands end in -o, giving chlorido ($\ce{Cl-}$), cyanido ($\ce{CN-}$), hydroxido ($\ce{OH-}$), and oxalato ($\ce{C2O4^2-}$). Four common neutral ligands take special names: aqua ($\ce{H2O}$), ammine ($\ce{NH3}$), carbonyl ($\ce{CO}$), and nitrosyl ($\ce{NO}$), while other neutral ligands keep their ordinary names.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Oxidation state by charge balance:** In $\ce{K4[Fe(CN)6]}$, four $\ce{K+}$ counter ions contribute $+4$, so the coordination entity must carry a $4-$ charge. Six $\ce{CN-}$ ligands supply $-6$, leaving iron at $+2$ so that $+2 - 6 = -4$ and the whole salt is electrically neutral.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Cation or anion decides the ending:** The sign of the coordination entity determines the metal's name without changing its Roman numeral. Iron(II) keeps its element name in the cation $\ce{[Fe(H2O)6]^2+}$ but becomes ferrate(II) in the anion $\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^4-}$; similarly, silver(I) in $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$ becomes argentate(I) in $\ce{[Ag(CN)2]-}$.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- A formula is written from the metal outward: metal first, then ligands in alphabetical order, with the entity in square brackets and the cation before the anion.
- A name runs the other way: ligands in alphabetical order (ignoring di, tri and so on), then the metal, then its oxidation state in Roman numerals. The cation is named first.
- Anionic ligands end in -o (chlorido, cyanido, oxalato). Neutral ligands keep their usual names, except aqua, ammine, carbonyl and nitrosyl. Use bis, tris and tetrakis when the ligand name already contains a number.
- The metal keeps its element name in a cation or neutral entity, and takes -ate (with Latin stems such as ferrate and argentate) in an anion.
- The oxidation state is found by charge balance: entity charge equals metal state plus ligand charges. Never include the number of ions in the name of the salt.
</KeyIdeas>
