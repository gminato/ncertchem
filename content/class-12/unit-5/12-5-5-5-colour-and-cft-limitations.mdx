<Hook>
Blue copper sulphate crystals turn to a white powder when you heat them, and turn blue again when water is dripped back on. The copper and the sulphate never left. Only the water did. What did the water molecules do to a copper ion to make it blue, and why does the colour vanish without them?
</Hook>

## White light with a band missing

White light is a mixture of every visible colour. When it passes through a sample, the sample may absorb one band of wavelengths and let the rest through. What reaches your eye is white light with a band taken out, and the eye reads that leftover mixture as a single colour. That colour is the complementary colour of the band that was absorbed.

Take a simple case. If a complex swallows green light, the light that emerges is red, so the complex looks red. Table 5.3 of the textbook pairs the wavelength absorbed with the colour seen for six real complexes, and the pattern is always the same: look up the colour absorbed, then read off its partner on the wheel.

| Coordination entity | Wavelength absorbed (nm) | Colour absorbed | Colour seen |
|---|---|---|---|
| $[\ce{CoCl(NH3)5}]^{2+}$ | 535 | Yellow | Violet |
| $[\ce{Co(NH3)5(H2O)}]^{3+}$ | 500 | Blue green | Red |
| $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$ | 475 | Blue | Yellow orange |
| $[\ce{Co(CN)6}]^{3-}$ | 310 | Ultraviolet (not visible) | Pale yellow |
| $[\ce{Cu(H2O)4}]^{2+}$ | 600 | Red | Blue |
| $[\ce{Ti(H2O)6}]^{3+}$ | 498 | Blue green | Violet |

The cyanide complex is the instructive row. Its absorption lies in the ultraviolet, outside what the eye can see, so nothing visible is removed and it is nearly colourless, with only a pale yellow tint.

## Where the missing light goes: a d to d jump

Crystal field theory (the model of Sections 5.5.1 to 5.5.4) explains the absorbing step. In an octahedral complex the five d orbitals split into a lower $t_{2g}$ set and a higher $e_g$ set, separated by the energy $\Delta_o$. A d electron sitting in the lower set can climb to the upper set if it receives exactly the right energy. A photon from the visible region can supply it. The photon is removed from the beam, and that is the absorbed band.

Follow $[\ce{Ti(H2O)6}]^{3+}$. The ion $\ce{Ti^3+}$ has a single d electron ($3d^{1}$). In the ground state that electron sits in $t_{2g}$, so the configuration is $t_{2g}^{1}e_g^{0}$. Absorbing a blue-green photon lifts it to give $t_{2g}^{0}e_g^{1}$. Blue green is taken out of white light, and the complementary violet is what you see.

$$
t_{2g}^{1}\,e_g^{0}\ \xrightarrow{\ h\nu\ (\text{blue-green})\ }\ t_{2g}^{0}\,e_g^{1}
$$

The photon energy must match the gap: $\Delta_o = h\nu = hc/\lambda$. A large gap needs a short wavelength, a small gap a long one. This is why the ligand matters so much for colour: a ligand that splits the d orbitals more shifts the absorption toward shorter wavelength, and the colour you see changes with it.

## Colour tracks the ligand

Because the colour comes from the gap, and the gap depends on the ligands, changing the ligands changes the colour. Table 5.3 already shows it: three cobalt(III) complexes carrying different mixes of $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ absorb at 535, 500 and 475 nm and look violet, red and yellow orange.

The textbook builds a clean series with nickel(II). Dissolve nickel(II) chloride in water to get green $[\ce{Ni(H2O)6}]^{2+}$, then add ethane-1,2-diamine (en) in the molar ratios en to Ni of 1:1, 2:1 and 3:1. Each bidentate en displaces two water molecules, and each new complex has a different colour.

| en : Ni ratio | Complex | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| none | $[\ce{Ni(H2O)6}]^{2+}$ | Green |
| 1 : 1 | $[\ce{Ni(H2O)4(en)}]^{2+}$ | Pale blue |
| 2 : 1 | $[\ce{Ni(H2O)2(en)2}]^{2+}$ | Blue or purple |
| 3 : 1 | $[\ce{Ni(en)3}]^{2+}$ | Violet |

The three steps are written as
$$
[\ce{Ni(H2O)6}]^{2+} + \ce{en} \rightleftharpoons [\ce{Ni(H2O)4(en)}]^{2+} + 2\,\ce{H2O}
$$
and the same pattern repeats twice more. Counting the waters left is a handy check: $6 - 2n$ remain after $n$ en molecules have bonded, so none are left in the violet complex.

## Why dehydrating a salt removes the colour

The d to d jump needs a crystal field, and a crystal field needs ligands. Without ligands the d orbitals stay degenerate and there is no gap for a photon to match. So a coloured hydrate can lose its colour when the ligands are taken away.

- Heating $[\ce{Ti(H2O)6}]\ce{Cl3}$ drives off the water, and the solid becomes colourless.
- Anhydrous $\ce{CuSO4}$ is white, while $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ is blue. This is the opening Hook: the water supplies the field that splits the copper d orbitals.

A full $d^{10}$ shell or an empty $d^{0}$ shell has the same effect from the electron side. There is no electron to promote, or no empty d level to promote it into, so there is no jump either. The Unit 4 colour lab on aqua ions made that point.

## Gem stones are d to d jumps in a crystal

The same physics colours gems. Ruby is aluminium oxide, $\ce{Al2O3}$, with about 0.5 to 1% of the $\ce{Al^3+}$ sites taken by $\ce{Cr^3+}$ ions ($d^{3}$). Each chromium sits in an octahedron of oxide ions, so you can treat it as an octahedral chromium(III) complex locked in the alumina lattice. Its d to d jumps give ruby its red.

Emerald is a different host, the mineral beryl, $\ce{Be3Al2Si6O18}$, but the colouring ion is again $\ce{Cr^3+}$ in octahedral sites. The surroundings differ slightly, and the absorption bands move to longer wavelength, into the yellow-red and the blue. With those two bands removed, the light that gets through is green. Same metal ion, different host, different colour.

## Where crystal field theory falls short

Crystal field theory is a strong model. It accounts, to a large extent, for the formation of complexes, their shapes, their colours and their magnetic behaviour, with nothing more than point charges and orbital energies. It has two honest weak spots, though.

First, the ligands are modelled as point charges, so an anionic ligand, with a full negative charge, ought to push on the d orbitals hardest and give the biggest splitting. Experiments say the opposite: anionic ligands such as $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ sit near the weak-field end of the spectrochemical series, below neutral $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{NH3}$. A model of pure charge cannot explain that order.

Second, the model ignores the covalent character of the bond between ligand and central metal, treating the interaction as purely electrostatic. Real metal to ligand bonds share electrons.

Both gaps are repaired by ligand field theory and by molecular orbital theory, which bring the overlap of orbitals back in. They go beyond this course, but it is worth knowing why they exist: the point-charge picture is a good start and not the final word.

<KeyIdeas>
- A complex looks the complementary colour of the band it absorbs: absorbing green gives red, and absorbing blue green in $[\ce{Ti(H2O)6}]^{3+}$ gives violet.
- Crystal field theory ascribes the colour to a d to d transition, where an electron moves from the lower $t_{2g}$ level to the upper $e_g$ level by absorbing a photon of energy $\Delta_o = hc/\lambda$.
- Changing the ligands changes the gap and the colour, as in the nickel(II) series with en (green, pale blue, blue or purple, violet). With no ligands there is no splitting, so the substance is colourless.
- Ruby and emerald both owe their colour to octahedral $\ce{Cr^3+}$ ($d^{3}$); the different host lattice moves the absorption bands and changes the colour.
- Crystal field theory treats ligands as point charges, so it cannot explain why anionic ligands are weak, and it ignores covalent bonding. Ligand field theory and molecular orbital theory address both.
</KeyIdeas>
