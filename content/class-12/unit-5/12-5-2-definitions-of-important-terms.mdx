## The coordination entity

A coordination entity has a central metal atom or ion, bonded to a fixed number of ions or molecules. Think of one metal at the hub and a set of attached partners around it. $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$ and $[\ce{Fe(CN)6}]^{4-}$ are charged entities, $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$ and $[\ce{PtCl2(NH3)2}]$ are neutral ones, and $[\ce{CoCl3(NH3)3}]$ is neutral as well, with three ammonia and three chloride attached to one cobalt.

## The central atom or ion

The atom or ion at the hub, to which a fixed number of groups are bound in a definite geometrical arrangement, is the central atom or ion. In $[\ce{NiCl2(H2O)4}]$ it is $\ce{Ni^2+}$, in $[\ce{CoCl(NH3)5}]^{2+}$ it is $\ce{Co^3+}$, and in $[\ce{Fe(CN)6}]^{3-}$ it is $\ce{Fe^3+}$. Because the centre accepts electron pairs from the groups around it, it is also called a Lewis acid.

## Ligands and how they bind

The ions or molecules attached to the centre are ligands. They range from a simple ion such as $\ce{Cl-}$, through small molecules such as $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{NH3}$, to larger ones such as $\ce{H2NCH2CH2NH2}$ and $\ce{N(CH2CH2NH2)3}$, and even macromolecules such as proteins. Each ligand attaches through a donor atom that supplies an electron pair, so the useful question is how many donor atoms it uses at once.

- **Unidentate:** one donor atom. $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{NH3}$ are examples.
- **Didentate:** two donor atoms. Ethane-1,2-diamine (en) and the oxalate ion $\ce{C2O4^2-}$ are examples.
- **Polydentate:** several donor atoms in one ligand, as in $\ce{N(CH2CH2NH2)3}$. The ethylenediaminetetraacetate ion, $\ce{EDTA^4-}$, is hexadentate: it binds through two nitrogen and four oxygen atoms.

The number of donor atoms a single ligand uses is its denticity. When a didentate or polydentate ligand grips one metal ion with two or more donor atoms together, it acts as a chelate ligand, and the product is a chelate complex. Chelate complexes tend to be more stable than similar complexes built from unidentate ligands.

One more type needs its own name. An ambidentate ligand has two different donor atoms but uses only one of them in any given complex. Nitrite, $\ce{NO2-}$, can bind through nitrogen (nitrito-N) or through oxygen (nitrito-O), and thiocyanate, $\ce{SCN-}$, can bind through sulphur (thiocyanato-S) or through nitrogen (thiocyanato-N). The ligand is the same, yet the two linkages make different complexes.

## Coordination number

The coordination number (CN) of a metal ion is the number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to it. It is a count of donor atoms, not of ligands. In $[\ce{PtCl6}]^{2-}$ the CN of platinum is 6 and in $[\ce{Ni(NH3)4}]^{2+}$ the CN of nickel is 4. In $[\ce{Fe(C2O4)3}]^{3-}$ and $[\ce{Co(en)3}]^{3+}$ there are only three ligands, but each is didentate, so the CN of both iron and cobalt is $3 \times 2 = 6$.

$$
\text{CN} = \sum \left(\text{number of ligands} \times \text{donor atoms bound per ligand}\right)
$$

NCERT adds an important restriction. Only sigma bonds from the ligand to the centre are counted. Any pi bonds that also form between ligand and metal do not add to the coordination number.

## Coordination sphere and counter ions

The central atom or ion with its ligands is enclosed in square brackets. This is the coordination sphere. Groups written outside the bracket are counter ions, and they are the ionisable part. In $\ce{K4[Fe(CN)6]}$ the coordination sphere is $[\ce{Fe(CN)6}]^{4-}$ and the counter ion is $\ce{K+}$. In water the compound gives four $\ce{K+}$ ions and one intact $[\ce{Fe(CN)6}]^{4-}$ ion.

## Coordination polyhedron

The spatial arrangement of the ligand atoms attached to the centre is the coordination polyhedron. The three common ones are octahedral, tetrahedral and square planar. $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$ is octahedral, $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$ is tetrahedral and $[\ce{PtCl4}]^{2-}$ is square planar. NCERT Fig. 5.1 draws these three alongside two five-coordinate shapes, trigonal bipyramidal and square pyramidal, using M for the central atom or ion and L for a unidentate ligand.

## Oxidation number of the central atom

The oxidation number of the centre is the charge it would carry if every ligand were removed, each taking away the electron pair it shares with the metal. It is written as a Roman numeral in brackets after the name. In $[\ce{Cu(CN)4}]^{3-}$, four cyanide ligands carry $-4$ in total, so for the entity charge of $-3$ the copper must carry $+1$, and the metal is written Cu(I).

$$
\text{oxidation number of metal} = \text{charge on entity} - \text{sum of ligand charges}
$$

Neutral ligands such as $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ contribute zero. That is why $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$ has cobalt in the +3 state without any further arithmetic.

## Homoleptic and heteroleptic complexes

A complex in which the metal is bound to only one kind of donor group is homoleptic, for example $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$. If more than one kind of donor group is present, the complex is heteroleptic, as in $[\ce{Co(NH3)4Cl2}]^{+}$.

<KeyIdeas>
- A coordination entity is a central metal atom or ion bonded to a fixed number of ligands, and the square brackets mark the coordination sphere; ionisable counter ions such as the $\ce{K+}$ in $\ce{K4[Fe(CN)6]}$ sit outside.
- Ligands are unidentate, didentate or polydentate by the number of donor atoms they use, and an ambidentate ligand such as $\ce{NO2-}$ or $\ce{SCN-}$ has two possible donors but uses one. Chelate ligands tend to give more stable complexes.
- The coordination number counts donor atoms bonded by sigma bonds, so $[\ce{Co(en)3}]^{3+}$ and $[\ce{Fe(C2O4)3}]^{3-}$ both have CN 6.
- Common coordination polyhedra are octahedral, tetrahedral and square planar, as in $[\ce{Co(NH3)6}]^{3+}$, $[\ce{Ni(CO)4}]$ and $[\ce{PtCl4}]^{2-}$.
- The oxidation number is the entity charge minus the sum of ligand charges, written in Roman numerals; a complex with one kind of donor group is homoleptic and with more than one is heteroleptic.
</KeyIdeas>
