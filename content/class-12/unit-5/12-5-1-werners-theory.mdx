<Hook>
Four cobalt(III) chloride compounds, yellow, purple, green and violet, each contain cobalt, chlorine and ammonia. Add silver nitrate to their solutions and they give three, two, one and one mole of white silver chloride. Why does the same chloride not precipitate the same way every time?
</Hook>

## A puzzle from the lab bench

Late in the nineteenth century, chemists had a stubborn problem. Cobalt(III) chloride, $\ce{CoCl3}$, combines with ammonia to give several brightly coloured solids. Ammonia and the chloride are both fully "saturated" in the old sense of valence, so why should they stick together at all, and why in such different proportions?

Alfred Werner, a Swiss chemist (1866 to 1919), attacked this with simple experiments: precipitations, conductivity readings and careful comparison of properties. In 1898 he put forward a theory of coordination compounds. In 1913 he won the Nobel Prize for his work on how atoms are linked together and on coordination theory. This Section follows the evidence the way Werner did.

## Two kinds of valence

Werner began by saying that a metal ion has two different kinds of bonding capacity.

- The **primary valence** is the ordinary ionic charge of the metal. In binary chlorides it is easy to read off: $\ce{CrCl3}$ has a primary valence of 3, and $\ce{CoCl2}$ and $\ce{PdCl2}$ each have 2.
- The **secondary valence** is the number of groups attached directly to the metal ion. It is a count of attachments, not a charge.

A group is either a negative ion or a neutral molecule. A chloride ion can therefore do two jobs at once: it can satisfy a primary valence, or it can occupy a secondary position, or it can sit in both roles at the same time.

## What silver nitrate reveals

Add excess cold silver nitrate to a solution of each compound and weigh the silver chloride. Only chloride that is free in solution, as an independent ion, is caught as $\ce{AgCl}$. Chloride that is bound directly to cobalt stays put.

| Colour | Empirical formula | Moles of AgCl per mole |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow | $\ce{CoCl3.6NH3}$ | 3 |
| Purple | $\ce{CoCl3.5NH3}$ | 2 |
| Green | $\ce{CoCl3.4NH3}$ | 1 |
| Violet | $\ce{CoCl3.4NH3}$ | 1 |

Each compound holds three chlorides in total, but fewer and fewer of them precipitate as ammonia is lost. The missing chlorides have taken the places that ammonia left.

## The six-group rule

Compare the numbers in each row. In the yellow compound there are six ammonia molecules and no bound chloride. In the purple one there are five ammonia molecules and one bound chloride. In both green and violet there are four ammonia molecules and two bound chlorides. Every time, ammonia plus bound chloride equals six. Werner read this as the secondary valence of cobalt(III): six groups, no more and no fewer, whatever those groups happen to be.

Conductivity points the same way. A solution's conductivity matches a count of ions per formula unit, written as a ratio of entity to counter ions. The yellow compound behaves as a 1:3 electrolyte, the purple as 1:2, and the green and violet as 1:1. The counter ions equal the AgCl moles, so the two experiments agree.

## Werner's postulates in plain terms

Werner's theory, put forward in 1898, can be compressed into four statements.

1. A metal in a coordination compound shows two kinds of linkage, primary and secondary.
2. Primary valences are normally ionisable, and negative ions satisfy them.
3. Secondary valences are not ionisable. Neutral molecules or negative ions satisfy them. The secondary valence equals the coordination number and is fixed for a given metal.
4. The groups held by secondary linkages have a definite spatial arrangement that goes with the coordination number.

In modern language, such an arrangement is a **coordination polyhedron**. The part inside the square bracket is the **coordination entity**, or complex, and the ions outside the bracket are the **counter ions**. In $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]Cl3}$ the entity is $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$ and the counter ions are three $\ce{Cl-}$.

## Shapes Werner predicted

Werner also argued that three shapes are the most common for transition-metal complexes: octahedral, tetrahedral and square planar. The cobalt entities above, $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$, $\ce{[CoCl(NH3)5]^2+}$ and $\ce{[CoCl2(NH3)4]^+}$, are octahedral. $\ce{[Ni(CO)4]}$ is tetrahedral and $\ce{[PtCl4]^2-}$ is square planar. The octahedral shape is what makes the green and violet compounds different: with four ammonia and two chlorides on one octahedron, the two chlorides can sit opposite each other or side by side.

<KeyIdeas>
- Werner proposed in 1898 that a metal has a primary valence, normally ionisable and met by negative ions, and a secondary valence, non-ionisable and met by neutral molecules or negative ions.
- Silver nitrate precipitates only free chloride: $\ce{CoCl3.6NH3}$, $\ce{CoCl3.5NH3}$ and $\ce{CoCl3.4NH3}$ (green and violet) give 3, 2, 1 and 1 mol of AgCl, so bound chloride is not precipitated.
- The secondary valence equals the coordination number and is fixed for a metal, six for cobalt(III) in all these compounds.
- The bracketed part is the coordination entity and the ions outside are counter ions; the groups form a coordination polyhedron, commonly octahedral, tetrahedral or square planar.
- Green and violet $\ce{[CoCl2(NH3)4]Cl}$ share one formula but differ in properties, so they are isomers.
</KeyIdeas>
