<Hook>
Nickel metal and carbon monoxide gas, mixed at mild conditions, give a volatile liquid that boils off and can be decomposed again to pure nickel. Nickel here is in oxidation state zero, with no positive charge to attract a ligand, and CO is a neutral molecule with no charge to be attracted. Why do the two hold on so tightly?
</Hook>

## Carbonyls: metals with only CO attached

A metal carbonyl is a complex in which carbon monoxide is the ligand, written CO and named carbonyl. Most transition metals form **homoleptic carbonyls**, meaning CO is the only kind of ligand present. The metal sits in oxidation state 0, and every CO ligand attaches to the metal through its carbon atom. Their shapes are simple and well defined, and they follow the geometries you already know from the earlier sections.

- $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$, tetracarbonylnickel(0): tetrahedral.
- $\ce{Fe(CO)5}$, pentacarbonyliron(0): trigonal bipyramidal.
- $\ce{Cr(CO)6}$, hexacarbonylchromium(0): octahedral.

Two carbonyls contain two metal atoms. In $\ce{[Mn2(CO)10]}$, decacarbonyldimanganese(0), two square pyramidal $\ce{Mn(CO)5}$ units are joined directly by a Mn-Mn bond. In $\ce{[Co2(CO)8]}$, octacarbonyldicobalt(0), there is a Co-Co bond and two of the CO groups bridge across the two cobalt atoms.

## The two-way bond between metal and CO

The metal-carbon bond in a carbonyl has both sigma and pi character, and the two parts work in opposite directions.

1. **Sigma bond, ligand to metal.** The carbon atom of CO has a lone pair. It donates this pair into a vacant orbital of the metal and forms the M-C sigma bond.
2. **Pi bond, metal to ligand.** The metal has filled d orbitals. It donates a pair of electrons from one of them into the empty antibonding $\pi^*$ orbital of carbon monoxide. This forms the M-C pi bond.

The second step is called **back bonding**, because charge flows back from the metal to the ligand. It does not run independently of the first. Sigma donation loads the metal with extra electron density, which makes it a better donor in the pi bond. Pi back donation in turn drains that excess, so the metal can accept more sigma donation. Each bond encourages the other, which is the **synergic effect**, and the net result is a stronger bond between CO and the metal. This explains why a neutral ligand can bind a metal in oxidation state zero so firmly.

## Putting it together

When you meet a carbonyl, ask three questions. What is the metal's oxidation state (zero)? How many CO groups are attached, and is the shape tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral or built from two such units? Are any CO groups bridging between two metals? Then recall the bond: sigma from carbon to metal, pi from metal back to carbon, and the two together strengthen each other.

<KeyIdeas>
- Homoleptic carbonyls contain only CO as ligand, with the metal in oxidation state 0. $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$ is tetrahedral, $\ce{Fe(CO)5}$ trigonal bipyramidal and $\ce{Cr(CO)6}$ octahedral.
- $\ce{[Mn2(CO)10]}$ is two square pyramidal $\ce{Mn(CO)5}$ units joined by a Mn-Mn bond. $\ce{[Co2(CO)8]}$ has a Co-Co bond bridged by two CO groups.
- The M-C sigma bond forms when the carbon lone pair is donated into a vacant metal orbital.
- The M-C pi bond forms when a filled metal d orbital donates electrons into the vacant antibonding $\pi^*$ orbital of CO.
- The two donations reinforce each other (synergic effect), strengthening the bond between CO and the metal.
</KeyIdeas>
