Both are chemistry, and both are measured by the same question: how fast does the change happen? To answer it you need a number for "how fast", and that number has to be defined carefully.

## Three questions about a reaction

Chemists ask three things about any reaction. Thermodynamics tells you whether it is feasible, which is the case when its free energy change is negative at constant temperature and pressure. Chemical equilibrium tells you how far it proceeds. Kinetics tells you how quickly it gets there. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen is thermodynamically eager to become water, yet it can sit on a shelf for a very long time without any visible change. Kinetics explains that wait, and it shows how concentration, temperature, pressure and a catalyst can each speed up or slow down the pace.

## Rate as change per unit time

How fast a reaction runs is read from how far the concentration of one species shifts during each unit of time. You can follow either a reactant, whose concentration falls, or a product, whose concentration rises. Take a reaction in which one reactant $\ce{R}$ turns into a product $\ce{P}$ in a vessel of constant volume. Between two instants $t_1$ and $t_2$ the reactant's concentration changes by $\Delta[\ce{R}] = [\ce{R}]_2 - [\ce{R}]_1$, which is a negative number. The minus sign in front of the definition turns it positive:

$$
\text{Rate} = -\frac{\Delta[\ce{R}]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{\Delta[\ce{P}]}{\Delta t}
$$

Both expressions give the same positive number, because every reactant lost is a product gained in this simple case. The rate is never negative. When the species are gases held at a fixed temperature, their concentrations scale with their partial pressures, so you may track a pressure instead, and the rate then carries the unit $\text{atm s}^{-1}$. For concentrations in molar units the rate is in $\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$.

## Average rate and the tangent

The change over a finite interval gives the **average rate**. Graphically, it is the slope of the chord that joins two points on the concentration–time curve. The hydrolysis of butyl chloride, $\ce{C4H9Cl + H2O -> C4H9OH + HCl}$, shows the idea well in NCERT's Table 3.1 data. Over the first 50 seconds the average rate is $1.90 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$. Over the 700 to 800 second interval it has fallen to $0.40 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$. The rate drops because the concentrations drop.

An average taken over one interval cannot tell you the rate at one moment, since it is the same for the whole interval. The **instantaneous rate** comes from letting the interval shrink to zero. Graphically it is the slope of the tangent at that instant, the line that just touches the curve there:

$$
r_{\text{inst}} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\left(-\frac{\Delta[\ce{R}]}{\Delta t}\right) = -\frac{d[\ce{R}]}{dt}
$$

The minus sign in the last form is the same reversal as before, so the instantaneous rate is positive when the tangent slopes downward.

## One coefficient, one species

If every coefficient in the equation is one, a reactant vanishes at exactly the pace a product appears. Larger coefficients break that tie, because a species taking part in bigger numbers changes more briskly than the reaction as a whole. Dividing each species' rate by its own coefficient puts all of them on a shared footing. For $a\,\ce{A} + b\,\ce{B} \rightarrow c\,\ce{C} + d\,\ce{D}$ the rate of the reaction is

$$
\text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{d[\ce{A}]}{dt} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{d[\ce{B}]}{dt} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{d[\ce{C}]}{dt} = \frac{1}{d}\frac{d[\ce{D}]}{dt}
$$

For $\ce{2NO2 -> 2NO + O2}$ the nitrogen dioxide falls twice as fast as the oxygen appears, because $-\tfrac{1}{2}\,d[\ce{NO2}]/dt = d[\ce{O2}]/dt$. The coefficients fix the relation between the rates, not the rate of the reaction itself.

<KeyIdeas>
- The **rate of a reaction** is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. Written as $-\Delta[\ce{R}]/\Delta t$ for a reactant, it is always positive.
- The **average rate** is the slope of the chord over a finite interval. The **instantaneous rate** is the slope of the tangent at one instant, found as the interval shrinks to zero.
- The units of rate are concentration per unit time, $\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, or $\text{atm s}^{-1}$ for gases followed through partial pressure.
- For $a\,\ce{A} + b\,\ce{B} \rightarrow c\,\ce{C} + d\,\ce{D}$, each species rate is divided by its coefficient, so every species reports the same rate of reaction.
- Kinetics asks how fast a reaction runs, which feasibility from thermodynamics and equilibrium from chemical equilibrium cannot answer.
</KeyIdeas>
