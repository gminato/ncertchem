## A catalyst speeds a reaction and is left unchanged

Picture a helper that quickens a reaction yet finishes the job in the same state it started in. Chemists call that helper a catalyst. Manganese dioxide is a standard illustration. Heated with potassium chlorate, it hurries the decomposition along, and the manganese dioxide can be recovered afterwards unchanged:

$$
\ce{2KClO3 ->[MnO2] 2KCl + 3O2}
$$

Materials that slow a reaction down belong to a separate class. They are called **inhibitors**, and they work in the opposite direction to a catalyst, so the two names are never interchanged.

## How a catalyst works: an alternative route

The most widely taught account is **intermediate complex theory**, and it begins with the catalyst's first move. The catalyst captures one reactant and holds it inside a short-lived combined species. That species is then handed a second reactant, which gives the product and releases the catalyst to start again. Written out, with a catalyst $\ce{M}$, the change is

$$
\ce{A + M -> AM}, \qquad \ce{AM + B -> P + M}
$$

Add the two steps and $\ce{M}$ cancels, leaving the net change $\ce{A + B -> P}$. The catalyst is consumed in neither step, which is why it is never used up.

Why does this speed things up? The catalyst gives the reaction a different pathway, one with a lower activation energy. The energy barrier is lower, so more molecules can cross it in a given time. The Arrhenius equation makes this precise: the smaller the activation energy, the faster the reaction, at the same temperature. Because a catalyst works through a cycle like this, a small amount can convert a large amount of reactant.

  <Step>
    **The uncatalysed route.** Start with the energy profile on its own. The reactants sit on the left at a level we call zero. To reach the products, the molecules must climb over a single hump. The height of that hump above the reactants is the activation energy, $E_\text{a} = 60\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. The products sit 20 kJ mol⁻¹ lower, so the reaction is exothermic, with $\Delta_\text{r}H = -20\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. Only this one curve is drawn.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A second route with lower humps.** The catalysed path now appears in a second colour. It starts at the same reactant level and ends at the same product level. It rises first to a lower hump, 30 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants. It then dips into a shallow valley, where the intermediate complex sits, and climbs to a second hump 50 kJ mol⁻¹ above the reactants. That second hump is the barrier that limits the rate. The uncatalysed hump stays faintly visible for comparison.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **More molecules over the lower barrier.** This view shows the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve at 298 K, with the fraction of molecules on the vertical axis. Two markers sit at 60 and 50 kJ mol⁻¹. By the Arrhenius factor $e^{-E_\text{a}/RT}$, the fraction able to cross 60 kJ mol⁻¹ is $3.04 \times 10^{-11}$; for 50 kJ mol⁻¹ it is $1.72 \times 10^{-9}$, about 57 times as many. The curve is the same for both routes; only the threshold marker moves left.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Both directions speed up equally.** Reverse the arrow and the backward reaction has its own barrier, given by $E_{\text{a}}^{\text{backward}} = E_\text{a}^{\text{forward}} - \Delta_\text{r}H$. Uncatalysed it is 80 kJ mol⁻¹, and catalysed it is 70 kJ mol⁻¹. Both barriers drop by the same 10 kJ mol⁻¹. The forward and backward rates therefore rise by the same factor, $e^{10\,000/RT} = 56.6$ at 298 K. The end levels and $\Delta_\text{r}H$ do not move.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **What a catalyst cannot do.** Now take a reaction whose Gibbs energy change is positive, so it is non-spontaneous. This profile is drawn on a Gibbs-energy scale, and the products stand above the reactants. The catalysed path is drawn again, with its lower hump, but the products stay where they were. A catalyst can make the climb quicker, but it cannot turn a positive $\Delta_\text{r}G$ negative, because it does not alter the Gibbs energy of the reaction.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Equilibrium: reached sooner, not moved

A catalyst does not change the equilibrium constant. It speeds up the forward and backward reactions by the same factor, so the ratio that defines $K$ stays fixed. The mixture simply reaches its equilibrium composition earlier. A catalyst also leaves the Gibbs energy of the reaction, $\Delta_\text{r}G$, untouched. That follows from $\Delta_\text{r}G^\circ = -RT\ln K$: since the catalyst does not change $K$, $\Delta_\text{r}G$ cannot change either. A catalyst therefore speeds up only spontaneous reactions. It can help a reaction that is already moving downhill get there faster, but it cannot make an uphill reaction happen.

<KeyIdeas>
- A catalyst raises the rate by offering an alternative route with a lower activation energy. It is chemically unchanged at the end, and a substance that slows a reaction is an inhibitor.
- In intermediate complex theory the catalyst forms temporary bonds with the reactants, giving an intermediate complex that breaks down to return the catalyst and give the products.
- With the same $A$, a fall of 10 kJ mol⁻¹ in $E_\text{a}$ at 298 K raises the rate by $e^{10\,000/RT} = 56.6$.
- A catalyst leaves $\Delta_\text{r}H$, $\Delta_\text{r}G$ and $K$ unchanged. It speeds the forward and backward reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner.
- A catalyst speeds up only spontaneous reactions. It cannot make a non-spontaneous reaction proceed.
</KeyIdeas>
