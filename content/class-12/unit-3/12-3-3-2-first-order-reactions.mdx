<Hook>
A doctor once explained that a medicine's level in the blood falls by the same fraction every few hours, not by the same amount. Why would the body clear a fixed share of what remains, rather than a fixed number of molecules? The answer is the signature of a first order reaction: the rate depends on how much is left, so every stretch of time strips off the same proportion of it.
</Hook>

## Rate that follows what is left

Some reactions slow down in a very orderly way. The simplest pattern is one in which a single reactant is the only thing that sets the speed, with its concentration raised to the first power. Take $\ce{R -> P}$. Its rate law is

$$
\text{Rate} = -\frac{d[\ce{R}]}{dt} = k[\ce{R}]
$$

The rate constant $k$ has the unit $\text{s}^{-1}$ for a first order reaction, and it does not depend on the starting concentration. Halve $[\ce{R}]$ and the rate halves with it, so the reaction slows as it runs. The constant $k$ is the fraction of what remains that disappears in each second.

Many familiar changes follow this law. NCERT's examples include the hydrogenation of ethene and the decomposition of $\ce{N2O5}$ and $\ce{N2O}$, along with natural and artificial radioactive decay, where each unstable nucleus decays at a fixed fraction per unit time.

## Integrating the rate law

Separate the variables and integrate from the start to a later time. The result is

$$
\ln[\ce{R}] = -kt + \ln[\ce{R}]_0
$$

where $[\ce{R}]_0$ is the concentration at $t = 0$. Taking antilogarithms gives the concentration form

$$
[\ce{R}] = [\ce{R}]_0\, e^{-kt}
$$

and rearranging gives the rate constant from a single pair of readings:

$$
k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{[\ce{R}]_0}{[\ce{R}]}
$$

If you have two readings instead, taken at times $t_1$ and $t_2$, the starting concentration drops out altogether:

$$
k = \frac{1}{t_2 - t_1}\ln\frac{[\ce{R}]_1}{[\ce{R}]_2}
$$

The exponential is a curve, but its logarithm is a straight line. Plot $\ln[\ce{R}]$ against $t$ and you get a line of slope $-k$ and intercept $\ln[\ce{R}]_0$. Plot $\log([\ce{R}]_0/[\ce{R}])$ against $t$ and you get a line through the origin with slope $k/2.303$. A straight line on either plot is the test for first order behaviour.

## A gas and its total pressure

Gases make a convenient test bed, because a pressure gauge on a sealed vessel is easier to read than a sample taken out for analysis. Take $\ce{A(g) -> B(g) + C(g)}$ in a vessel of fixed volume, with $p_i$ the pressure of A at the start. Let $y$ stand for the pressure of A that has decomposed by time $t$. Each decomposed A leaves one B and one C behind, so the three partial pressures are

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_A &= p_i - y \\
p_B &= y \\
p_C &= y
\end{aligned}
$$

and the total pressure is their sum, $p_t = p_i + y$. Solving that for $y$ and putting it back into $p_A = p_i - y$ leaves only the measured total:

$$
p_A = 2p_i - p_t
$$

Feeding this $p_A$ into the first order integrated law gives the rate constant from the total pressure alone:

$$
k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{p_i}{2p_i - p_t}
$$

So a gauge reading at one time is enough to find $k$, provided you know the starting pressure.

<KeyIdeas>
- A first order reaction has rate $= k[\ce{R}]$, so the rate depends on the concentration that is left and falls as the reaction runs.
- The integrated law $\ln[\ce{R}] = -kt + \ln[\ce{R}]_0$ gives $[\ce{R}] = [\ce{R}]_0 e^{-kt}$: equal time steps remove equal fractions, not equal amounts.
- $\ln[\ce{R}]$ against $t$ is a straight line of slope $-k$; $\log([\ce{R}]_0/[\ce{R}])$ against $t$ is a line through the origin of slope $k/2.303$.
- The rate constant of a first order reaction is in $\text{s}^{-1}$ and is found from $k = \dfrac{2.303}{t}\log\dfrac{[\ce{R}]_0}{[\ce{R}]}$.
- For a gas $\ce{A(g) -> B(g) + C(g)}$ at constant volume, $p_A = 2p_i - p_t$, so the total pressure alone gives $k = \dfrac{2.303}{t}\log\dfrac{p_i}{2p_i - p_t}$.
</KeyIdeas>
