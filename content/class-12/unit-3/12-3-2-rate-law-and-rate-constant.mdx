## Rate depends on the mixture

Section 3.1 followed one reaction as its reactants ran down. Now compare different starting mixtures of the same reaction. The rate depends on the concentrations of the reactants (pressures, for gases), and also on temperature and on any catalyst present. This Section keeps temperature and catalyst fixed and asks how the rate depends on concentration alone.

The relation between rate and concentration is called the **rate law**, also known as the rate equation or rate expression. For a general reaction $a\,\ce{A} + b\,\ce{B} \rightarrow c\,\ce{C} + d\,\ce{D}$, the rate law reads

$$
\text{Rate} = k\,[\ce{A}]^{x}[\ce{B}]^{y}
$$

Here $k$ is the **rate constant**, and the exponents $x$ and $y$ describe how strongly each reactant matters. Do not assume that $x$ and $y$ equal the coefficients $a$ and $b$. They may match, or they may not, and only experiment can tell you which.

## Finding the exponents from initial rates

Chemists often start each experiment afresh and measure the **initial rate**, the rate in the first moments before the reactants have changed much. Changing one concentration at a time shows how each reactant matters. For nitric oxide and oxygen, $\ce{2NO(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO2(g)}$, Table 3.2 lists the initial rate of NO₂ formation for four mixtures:

| Experiment | $[\ce{NO}]$ / mol L⁻¹ | $[\ce{O2}]$ / mol L⁻¹ | Initial rate / mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.30 | 0.30 | 0.096 |
| 2 | 0.60 | 0.30 | 0.384 |
| 3 | 0.30 | 0.60 | 0.192 |
| 4 | 0.60 | 0.60 | 0.768 |

Compare experiments 1 and 2. Doubling $[\ce{NO}]$ while $[\ce{O2}]$ stays at 0.30 mol L⁻¹ multiplies the rate by four. The exponent follows from the ratio of the two rates and the ratio of the two concentrations:

$$
x = \frac{\log(r_2/r_1)}{\log(c_2/c_1)} = \frac{\log 4}{\log 2} = 2
$$

Now compare experiments 1 and 3. Doubling $[\ce{O2}]$ only doubles the rate, so its exponent is 1. Experiment 4 doubles both, and the rate rises by eight, which is exactly $2^2 \times 2$. The factors multiply, so the law is

$$
\text{Rate} = k\,[\ce{NO}]^{2}[\ce{O2}]
$$

Here the rate is the rate of formation of NO₂, the quantity Table 3.2 measures, so $k$ belongs to that rate. Substituting experiment 1 gives the rate constant:

$$
k = \frac{0.096\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(0.30\ \text{mol L}^{-1})^{2}\,(0.30\ \text{mol L}^{-1})} = 3.56\ \text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{L}^{2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
$$

Every one of the four experiments gives this same value, which is what makes the law trustworthy.

## The exponents need not match the equation

The coefficients in $2\ce{NO} + \ce{O2} \rightarrow 2\ce{NO2}$ are 2 and 1, and the exponents agree. That is a feature of this reaction, not a rule. Two other examples show the gap. In $\ce{CHCl3 + Cl2 -> CCl4 + HCl}$, the rate law is $\text{Rate} = k[\ce{CHCl3}][\ce{Cl2}]^{1/2}$, so chlorine enters to the half power. In the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate, $\text{Rate} = k[\ce{CH3COOC2H5}][\ce{H2O}]^{0}$: water carries exponent zero, so $[\ce{H2O}]^{0} = 1$ and water drops out of the law. The balanced equation cannot predict a rate law. It has to be measured.

## The rate constant and its units

The rate constant $k$ is the rate when every concentration term in the rate law equals $1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. Its units depend on the overall order $n$, the sum of all the exponents. The units follow from the rate law:

$$
\text{units of } k = (\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1-n}\,\text{s}^{-1}
$$

So a zero-order rate constant is in $\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, a first-order one is in $\text{s}^{-1}$, and a second-order one is in $\text{mol}^{-1}\,\text{L}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. The chlorine example above has overall order $1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac32$, so its $k$ carries the fractional units $\text{mol}^{-1/2}\,\text{L}^{1/2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. The nitric oxide law has overall order $2 + 1 = 3$, which gives $\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{L}^{2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

## Predicting a rate from the law

Once the exponents and $k$ are known, the law predicts the rate for any mixture. Take $[\ce{NO}] = 0.90\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ and $[\ce{O2}] = 0.30\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$:

$$
\text{Rate} = 3.56 \times (0.90)^{2} \times 0.30 \approx 0.864\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
$$

Here $k$ is carried unrounded, which is why the product is 0.864 and not 0.865.

<KeyIdeas>
- The **rate law** $\text{Rate} = k[\ce{A}]^{x}[\ce{B}]^{y}$ relates the rate to the concentrations of the reactants. Its exponents must be found by experiment.
- Comparing **initial rates** from runs that change one concentration at a time gives each exponent: doubling a concentration that multiplies the rate by $2^{x}$ identifies the order $x$.
- The exponents need not equal the coefficients of the balanced equation. For $\ce{NO}$ and $\ce{O2}$ they agree, but $\ce{CHCl3 + Cl2}$ has an exponent of $\tfrac12$ on chlorine.
- The **rate constant** $k$ is the rate when every concentration term equals $1\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$. Its units, $(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1-n}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, depend on the overall order $n$.
- Once $k$ and the exponents are known, the rate law predicts the rate for any starting mixture.
</KeyIdeas>
