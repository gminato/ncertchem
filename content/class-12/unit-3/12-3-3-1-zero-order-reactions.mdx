Some chemical reactions behave the same way: the rate holds steady while the reactant runs down. Why would a reaction ignore how much of its reactant is left?
</Hook>

## Why integrate the rate law?

The rate at any instant is the slope of the tangent to a concentration–time curve. Drawing tangents is fiddly, and a small error in one slope can make the order of the reaction hard to pin down. Measuring concentrations at a few clock times is far easier. An **integrated rate equation** links those measured concentrations directly to the rate constant. Each order has its own integrated form, and here we work through the simplest case, the zero-order reaction.

## The zero-order rate law

Take a reaction $\ce{R -> P}$ whose rate does not depend on the concentration of $\ce{R}$ at all. Its rate is

$$
\text{Rate} = -\dfrac{d[\ce{R}]}{dt} = k[\ce{R}]^{0} = k
$$

Because $[\ce{R}]^{0}$ equals 1 whatever the concentration, the rate is simply $k$ and stays the same for the whole run. Because the rate is measured in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, the rate constant of a zero-order reaction carries the same units.

To turn this into a relation between concentration and time, separate the variables, $d[\ce{R}] = -k\,dt$, and integrate. Starting from $[\ce{R}]_0$ at $t = 0$ gives

$$
[\ce{R}] = -kt + [\ce{R}]_0
$$

Solving the same equation for the rate constant gives a direct recipe from two measured concentrations:

$$
k = \dfrac{[\ce{R}]_0 - [\ce{R}]}{t}
$$

## Reading the straight line

Compare $[\ce{R}] = -kt + [\ce{R}]_0$ with the equation of a straight line, $y = mx + c$. A plot of $[\ce{R}]$ against $t$ is therefore a straight line with slope $-k$ and intercept $[\ce{R}]_0$. The concentration falls by the same amount every second.

Take $[\ce{R}]_0 = 0.80\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ and $k = 0.025\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$. The reactant falls by 0.025 mol L⁻¹ each second, so after 10 s the concentration is 0.55 mol L⁻¹. The line is ideal: it reaches zero at $t = [\ce{R}]_0/k$, which here is 32 s.

## Zero order on surfaces

Zero order shows up only in special settings. Some enzyme-catalysed reactions behave this way, and so do a number of reactions run on a solid catalyst surface. Take ammonia breaking down over hot platinum: when the gas is compressed, the metal's sites fill up completely, and adding yet more ammonia gives no extra speed. Hydrogen iodide decomposing on gold follows the same pattern.

  </Step>
  <Step>
    **The gas thins, the rate does not:** As the reaction goes on, ammonia is used up and fewer molecules hover over the surface. The sites stay covered, because there is still more than enough gas to fill them. The rate arrow keeps the same length, and each second removes the same amount of reactant from the vessel.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **The straight-line plot:** A panel shows time on the horizontal axis and the concentration of ammonia on the vertical axis. Each measured point lies on one straight line that starts at $[\ce{R}]_0 = 0.80\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ and falls steadily. A slope triangle on the line marks the fall per second, which is $-k$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Two points give k:** Pick two times on the line, $t = 0$ and $t = 12\ \text{s}$. At 12 s the concentration has fallen to 0.50 mol L⁻¹. The fall of 0.30 mol L⁻¹ over 12 s gives $k = 0.025\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$. Any two points on the line give the same value, because the line never bends.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **When the sites empty:** Near the end, the gas is scarce and some sites are left bare. Fewer molecules now reach the sites, so the rate starts to depend on the concentration again. The real curve lies above the ideal line: it flattens away from the line and approaches zero more slowly. The ideal line would reach zero at 32 s, but the true curve leaves it well before that.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## When the straight line bends

The straight line is an idealisation, and it holds only while the surface stays saturated. A zero-order rate is therefore a rate that is fixed by something other than the reactant's concentration, usually the catalyst surface. Remember that the constant rate has nothing to do with the reactant running low. Only when the gas thins so much that not every site stays covered does the concentration matter again.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Integrated rate equations** connect measured concentrations and times directly to the rate constant, which is easier than measuring tangent slopes.
- **Zero order** means Rate = k. The rate is constant and independent of the reactant's concentration, and k has units of mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- **The straight line** is $[\ce{R}] = -kt + [\ce{R}]_0$: slope −k, intercept $[\ce{R}]_0$, and $k = ([\ce{R}]_0 - [\ce{R}])/t$.
- **Surface saturation** and some enzyme-catalysed reactions give zero order, because the catalyst's sites, not the reactant, set the pace.
- **The line has limits.** It holds only while the surface stays saturated. Near the end of the reaction, the real curve flattens and approaches zero more slowly than the line.
</KeyIdeas>
