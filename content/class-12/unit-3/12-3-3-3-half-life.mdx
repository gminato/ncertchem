Charcoal from an old hearth can be dated by measuring how much of its carbon-14 has decayed. A large lump and a tiny splinter both give a reliable date, yet the time for half of the carbon-14 to decay is the same for each. Why should the amount of carbon make no difference to the clock?

## What a half-life measures

Pick any reactant and ask how long it takes to lose half of its starting concentration. That time is the **half-life**, written $t_{1/2}$. It is one number, but its value depends on the order of the reaction, so the two simplest orders need separate treatment.

## Zero-order half-life

For a zero-order reaction the integrated rate law gives $k = \dfrac{[\ce{R}]_0 - [\ce{R}]}{t}$. Put $t = t_{1/2}$ and $[\ce{R}] = \tfrac{1}{2}[\ce{R}]_0$ into it, and the algebra gives

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{[\ce{R}]_0}{2k}
$$

Here the starting amount sits on top of the fraction. Doubling $[\ce{R}]_0$ doubles the wait, while doubling $k$ halves it. Load the vessel with twice the reactant and the reaction needs twice as long to shed half of it.

## First-order half-life

For a first-order reaction, $k = \dfrac{2.303}{t}\log\dfrac{[\ce{R}]_0}{[\ce{R}]}$. At the half-life the ratio $[\ce{R}]_0/[\ce{R}]$ is exactly 2, so

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log 2 = \frac{0.693}{k}
$$

Since $\log 2 = 0.301$, the factor $2.303 \times 0.301$ comes to 0.693. The result contains no $[\ce{R}]_0$. Each half-life halves whatever is left, so the time needed does not depend on how much you started with. That independence is the signature of a first-order reaction. The same relation reads the other way: $k = 0.693/t_{1/2}$. With $k = 0.0500\ \text{s}^{-1}$, the half-life is $0.693/0.0500 = 13.9\ \text{s}$, the same whether the sample began at 1.00 or at 2.00 mol L⁻¹.

This is why radioactive dating works. Radioactive decay is a first-order process, so the time for half of the carbon-14 in a sample to decay does not depend on how much carbon the sample holds.

## Order can shift with conditions

Order is not a fixed label stamped on a reaction. It comes from measurement, and the conditions a reaction runs under can change which concentrations show up in its rate law. A common case is a reaction whose rate law looks first order even though both reactants are involved. Take the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate:

$$
\ce{CH3COOC2H5 + H2O -> CH3COOH + C2H5OH}
$$

The rate law here does contain both reactants, yet the measurements often hide that. When one partner is supplied in a huge surplus, its concentration hardly moves as the reaction proceeds, so for practical purposes it drops out of the rate law. Water plays that part in ethyl acetate hydrolysis: almost every molecule of it is untouched, and only the ester's concentration changes in any meaningful way. The measured rate therefore tracks the ester alone, and the reaction is classed as **pseudo-first order**.

Inversion of cane sugar follows the same pattern. Water is the surplus partner, so the rate law collapses to $\text{Rate} = k[\ce{C12H22O11}]$. Cane sugar splits into glucose and fructose, which are both $\ce{C6H12O6}$.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Half-life** is the wait for a reactant to be cut in two, measured from whatever amount it starts with.
- **Zero order:** $t_{1/2} = [\ce{R}]_0/2k$. It is proportional to the starting concentration, so it changes when the start changes.
- **First order:** $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$. It does not depend on $[\ce{R}]_0$, and k can be found from the half-life.
- **Table 3.4 in short:** zero order gives a straight line for $[\ce{R}]$ against $t$ with k in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹; first order gives a straight line for $\ln[\ce{R}]$ against $t$ with k in s⁻¹.
- **Pseudo-first order:** a reaction with one reactant in large excess, such as hydrolysis in water, behaves as first order.
</KeyIdeas>
