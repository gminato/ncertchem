<Hook>
A chlor-alkali plant pumps brine through a row of electrolysis cells and ships out caustic soda, chlorine and hydrogen, the three basic chemicals that a huge share of industry depends on. Sodium metal, the first product you might expect from electrolysing salt, never leaves the plant. Why does the same tank of salt solution yield one set of products in the factory and a different set in a laboratory cell with molten salt?
</Hook>

## Same cell, different products

In the last section you saw that electrolysis forces a non-spontaneous reaction by pushing electrons through the cell from an external source. Charge and mass are linked by Faraday's laws, but those laws tell you how much of a product forms, not which product it is. The identity of the product is a separate question.

For a given cell, the product depends on three things. The first is the **state of the electrolyte**: whether the ions are free to move in a melt or are shared with water molecules in solution. The second is the **electrode material**. An inert electrode such as platinum or gold does not take part in the chemistry; it only supplies or removes electrons. A reactive electrode, such as a copper rod, is oxidised or reduced itself. The third is the **list of species present**, each with its own tendency to gain or lose electrons.

## Competition decides the winner

When several species could react at an electrode, the one that reacts is the one that needs the least driving force. In a practical sense, you read it from the standard electrode potentials, $E^\circ$, that you met earlier in this Unit and in the table of reduction potentials.

At the **cathode**, reduction takes place, so the species with the most positive reduction potential is reduced first. At the **anode**, oxidation takes place, so the species that is easiest to oxidise is the one with the lowest reduction potential, and it is oxidised first. Read the table in that direction and the rule is simple, even when the list of possible reactions looks long.

Standard potentials describe ideal conditions at unit concentration, so they are a guide and not a final answer. When the concentrations differ, the Nernst potential, which corrects $E^\circ$ for the actual concentrations, takes its place. Some electrode processes are also slow. A reaction can be thermodynamically favourable and still need a little extra voltage before it happens quickly. That extra voltage is the **overpotential**. The overpotential for oxygen evolution is large enough to change which product appears at an anode, as the aqueous sodium chloride case shows.

## Molten sodium chloride: no competition

Melt pure sodium chloride and the only mobile ions are $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$. Water is absent, so nothing else can be reduced or oxidised. At the cathode, sodium ions gain electrons:

$$
\ce{Na+ + e- -> Na(s)}
$$

At the anode, chloride ions lose electrons and form chlorine gas:

$$
\ce{Cl- -> 1/2 Cl2(g) + e-}
$$

The two products are sodium metal and chlorine. With no water and no other ion to share the work, each electrode has just one reaction available, so the two ions alone decide what forms.

## Aqueous sodium chloride: the water arrives

Dissolve the salt in water and the picture changes. Water molecules, and the $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions they produce, now sit beside the sodium and chloride ions. At the cathode, $\ce{Na+}$ has a very negative standard potential, about $-2.71$ V. Hydrogen ions from water, whose couple $\ce{H+}/\ce{H2}$ sits at $0.00$ V, are reduced far more readily, so hydrogen gas is released and sodium never deposits. Because the $\ce{H+}$ comes from water, the cathode reaction is best written with water as the reactant:

$$
\ce{H2O(l) + e- -> 1/2 H2(g) + OH-(aq)}
$$

The hydroxide ions stay in solution, so the liquid near the cathode becomes alkaline. At the anode, there are two candidates: chloride, with $E^\circ = 1.36$ V, and water, with $E^\circ = 1.23$ V. The lower value suggests that water should be oxidised, releasing oxygen. Yet oxygen has a high overpotential, so chloride is oxidised instead and chlorine bubbles form. Combining the two electrode reactions with the spectator sodium ions gives:

$$
\ce{2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g) + Cl2(g)}
$$

This is the reaction behind the chlor-alkali industry that opened this Section. Sodium hydroxide remains in the solution, while hydrogen and chlorine are collected as gases.

## Sulphuric acid: dilute or concentrated

Electrolysing sulphuric acid also depends on the concentration of the acid. At the cathode, hydrogen ions are always reduced to hydrogen gas. The anode is where the choice happens. In dilute acid, water is oxidised to oxygen:

$$
\ce{2H2O(l) -> O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e-}
$$

The standard potential for this reaction is $1.23$ V. In concentrated acid, sulphate ions are oxidised instead to peroxodisulfate:

$$
\ce{2SO4^2-(aq) -> S2O8^2-(aq) + 2e-}
$$

NCERT gives $1.96$ V for this persulfate reaction. Taken on standard potentials alone, water at $1.23$ V would be oxidised first, because it has the lower value. NCERT explains that at a higher concentration of sulphuric acid the persulfate route is preferred, so $\ce{S2O8^2-}$ forms and water is not oxidised. The electrolyte's composition has changed the winner, and the anode product changes with it.

## Metals obtained by electrolysis

The same competition explains why some metals are extracted by electrolysis of their molten compounds, and not from aqueous solutions. Aluminium, for example, has $E^\circ = -1.66$ V for $\ce{Al^3+ + 3e- -> Al}$. In an aqueous solution, hydrogen ions from water, at $0.00$ V, are reduced far more readily than $\ce{Al^3+}$, so hydrogen forms at the cathode and no aluminium deposits. In the molten state, water is absent and aluminium can be reduced.

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- The product of electrolysis depends on the **state of the electrolyte**, the **electrode material** (inert or reactive) and which species are present to compete.
- At the **cathode**, the species with the most positive reduction potential is reduced first. At the **anode**, the species with the lowest reduction potential is oxidised first.
- **Molten NaCl** gives sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode, because only Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are present.
- In **aqueous NaCl**, the H⁺ from water is reduced to H₂ at the cathode, leaving OH⁻ in solution. At the anode chlorine forms rather than oxygen, because oxygen has a high overpotential. The net products are NaOH, H₂ and Cl₂.
- In **dilute H₂SO₄** the anode releases O₂ from water; in **concentrated H₂SO₄** the anode forms peroxodisulfate (S₂O₈²⁻) instead.
</KeyIdeas>
