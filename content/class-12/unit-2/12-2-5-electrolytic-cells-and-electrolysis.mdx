## An electrolytic cell runs on borrowed energy

In a Daniell cell, a spontaneous reaction makes electricity. An **electrolytic cell** does the reverse. An external DC source, rather than the chemistry itself, supplies the voltage that makes a reaction happen. Reactions that would never run on their own, such as pulling metal out of a solution, become possible this way.

Electrolysis matters far beyond the lab, because industry relies on it to plate, refine and extract metals. The apparatus is simple: two electrodes dip into an electrolyte, which can be a melt or a solution, and a DC source drives electrons through the external wire. The same rule as in a galvanic cell still holds, with a twist. The anode is the electrode joined to the positive terminal of the source, and it is still the site of oxidation. The cathode is joined to the negative terminal, and it is still the site of reduction.

## Copper in copper sulphate: an anode that dissolves

The simplest electrolytic cell has two copper strips dipping into aqueous $\ce{CuSO4}$. Switch on a DC source and two things happen at once. At the cathode, $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ ions gain two electrons and deposit as copper metal. At the anode, copper atoms lose two electrons and enter the solution as $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$. The anode thins while the cathode thickens, and the blue colour of the solution stays roughly constant because copper leaves one electrode as fast as it arrives at the other.

This is not just a classroom picture. Refiners run the same reaction on purpose: a slab of crude copper serves as the anode, and as current draws copper off it, a thin sheet of pure copper builds up on the cathode. Metals that do not dissolve drop to the bottom as sludge.

Electrons leave the anode, pass through the source, and reach the cathode, while a conventional current flows the opposite way. Nothing happens until the source drives the current.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Ions carry the current:** Inside the solution, $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ ions drift toward the cathode and $\ce{SO4^{2-}}$ ions drift toward the anode. At the cathode, $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ gains two electrons and plates out as reddish copper metal. At the anode, copper loses two electrons and dissolves as $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$. The anode visibly thins while the cathode thickens.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Double the time, double the metal (first law):** At 1.00 A, the cathode gains 0.329 g of copper in 1000 s. Let the same current run for 2000 s and the cathode gains 0.659 g. Twice the charge deposits twice the mass. The amount of product is proportional to the charge passed, Q = It, whatever the shape of the cell.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Same charge, two metals (second law):** Two cells are joined in series so one current passes through both. The first holds $\ce{Ag+}$ and the second holds $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$. A charge of 3600 C deposits 4.02 g of silver and only 1.19 g of copper. Each silver ion needs one electron and each copper ion needs two, so the masses scale with molar mass divided by electrons needed: 107.87 for silver against 31.78 for copper.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Counting electrons with Q = It:** Multiply the current by the time in seconds to get Q in coulombs. Divide Q by the faraday, $F = 96\,487\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$, to get moles of electrons. 3600 C is 0.0373 mol of electrons. That is enough for 0.0373 mol of $\ce{Ag+}$, but only 0.0187 mol of $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$, because each copper ion needs two electrons.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Faraday's two laws, in numbers

Michael Faraday worked this out in the 1830s, before constant-current sources existed. He used a coulometer, a standard cell that showed how much metal a charge had deposited. Today we set the current directly and time it, so the two laws become a short calculation.

**First law:** charge is the lever. Pass twice the charge and the electrode gains twice the product; halve it and the deposit halves.

**Second law:** for one fixed charge, compare metals. Each metal's deposit follows its molar mass divided by the electrons its ion takes, the equivalent mass, and that quotient sets the ratio between the masses.

The **faraday** is the charge carried by one mole of electrons: $F = 96\,487\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$, usually rounded to $96\,500\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$ for rough work. Put these together and any electrolysis problem follows the same three steps: find Q from $Q = It$, convert Q into moles of electrons, then use the stoichiometry of the electrode reaction to get moles of product and grams.

Take a 2.00 A current running for 1800 s. Q is 3600 C, which corresponds to 0.0373 mol of electrons. At the silver electrode that means 0.0373 mol of silver, which is 4.02 g. At the copper electrode it means 0.0187 mol of copper, which is 1.19 g.

Aluminium is a special case that catches out plating workshops. Water is reduced to hydrogen before an $\ce{Al^{3+}}$ ion can be reduced, so no metallic aluminium deposits from an aqueous bath. Aluminium is made instead by electrolysing molten aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite. Magnesium ions take two electrons and aluminium ions take three, the counts that enter the second law.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Electrolytic cells** use an external DC source to drive a reaction that is not spontaneous. The anode is joined to the positive terminal and is still the site of oxidation; the cathode is joined to the negative terminal and is still the site of reduction.
- **Copper electrolysis** dissolves copper at the anode and deposits it at the cathode with the same stoichiometry, which is the basis of refining impure copper.
- **Faraday's first law:** the mass deposited is proportional to the charge passed, Q = It.
- **Faraday's second law:** for the same charge, masses are proportional to molar mass divided by the number of electrons per ion.
- **The faraday** is $F = 96\,487\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$, so moles of electrons = Q / F, and every electrolysis mass follows from that count.
</KeyIdeas>
