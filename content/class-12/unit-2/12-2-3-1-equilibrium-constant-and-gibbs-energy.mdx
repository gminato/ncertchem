<Hook>
A teacher drops a strip of clean copper into a beaker of silver nitrate solution. Within minutes, feathery crystals of silver spread over the metal and the solution turns blue. Chemists say the reaction has gone to completion. But every reaction can run in both directions, so how complete is complete? Could a trace of copper still be dissolving, or would the last traces take forever to disappear? A voltage reading can answer that, once you know what the number on the voltmeter means.
</Hook>

## When a cell runs down

In the Daniell cell, zinc dissolves into the zinc solution as $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ ions, and copper ions leave the copper solution as copper metal. Charge keeps flowing through the external wire for as long as the two half-reactions can keep going. Each step makes the zinc side richer in $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ and the copper side poorer in $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$, so the ratio of the two concentrations creeps upward. The voltmeter reading falls with it.

Eventually the reading reaches zero. At that point the concentrations stop changing, because the cell is at equilibrium and no net reaction is left to drive a current. Written as a chemical equation, the cell reaction is

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) -> Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

and the reaction quotient $Q$ for it is $[\ce{Zn^{2+}}]/[\ce{Cu^{2+}}]$. The cell potential depends on $Q$ and falls as $Q$ grows. When the cell reaches equilibrium, $Q$ has become $K_c$, the equilibrium constant, and the cell potential is zero.

## Linking E° to K

Set the cell potential to zero in the concentration relationship and you get a direct bridge between a measurable voltage and an equilibrium constant. The general result is

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = \frac{2.303\,RT}{nF}\,\log K_c
$$

At $298\ \text{K}$ the factor $\frac{2.303\,RT}{F}$ simplifies to $0.059\ \text{V}$, which gives

$$
E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = \frac{0.059\ \text{V}}{n}\,\log K_c
$$

For the Daniell cell, $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 1.10\ \text{V}$ and $n = 2$. Solving for the logarithm gives $\log K_c \approx 37.3$, so $K_c \approx 2 \times 10^{37}$. That number is so large that, at equilibrium, the ratio $[\ce{Cu^{2+}}]/[\ce{Zn^{2+}}]$ is about $5 \times 10^{-38}$. The copper ions are, for every practical purpose, gone. This is how you can answer the puzzle in the hook: the reaction is complete because its equilibrium lies so far to the right that the leftover amount of reactant is far below anything you could detect.

Chemists rarely measure such constants directly, because the concentrations involved are impossibly small to track. The cell voltage gives you $K_c$ indirectly, which is why this relation is so useful.

## Electrical work and Gibbs energy

A galvanic cell turns chemical change into electricity. Every mole of reaction pushes $n$ moles of electrons around the external circuit, and each mole of electrons carries $F = 96487\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$, so the total charge moved is $nF$. Multiply that charge by the voltage that drives it and you have the electrical energy the cell delivers. A cell run reversibly delivers exactly the drop in Gibbs energy of the reaction, which gives

$$
\Delta_r G = -nFE_{\text{cell}}
$$

At standard conditions, where every reacting species is at unit concentration, $E_{\text{cell}}$ becomes $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ and

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}
$$

For the iron and nickel reaction above, with $n = 2$ and $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.19\ \text{V}$, the standard Gibbs energy change is

$$
\Delta_r G^\circ = -2 \times 96487\ \text{C mol}^{-1} \times 0.19\ \text{V}
$$

$$
= -36\,665\ \text{J mol}^{-1} = -36.67\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

The negative sign says the reaction is spontaneous as written under standard conditions. Combining this with $\Delta_r G^\circ = -RT\ln K_c$ ties the same reaction to its equilibrium constant, so $E^\circ$, $\Delta_r G^\circ$ and $K_c$ are three views of one reaction.

## Intensive or extensive?

Think of the cell potential as a property like temperature: split the beaker in two and the voltage stays put. The Gibbs energy change is different, because it scales with the number of electrons the balanced equation transfers. Double every coefficient in the iron and nickel reaction and $n$ rises from 2 to 4. The voltage still reads $0.19\ \text{V}$, but $\Delta_r G^\circ$ becomes $-73.33\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. So before you compare two Gibbs energies, check that both equations are balanced the same way.

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- **Equilibrium in a cell** is reached when the cell potential falls to zero and the reaction quotient $Q$ has become the equilibrium constant $K_c$.
- **E° and K are linked** by $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = \frac{2.303\,RT}{nF}\log K_c$, which at $298\ \text{K}$ is $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = \frac{0.059\ \text{V}}{n}\log K_c$. A larger standard potential means a larger equilibrium constant.
- **The Daniell reaction** has $K_c \approx 2 \times 10^{37}$ at $298\ \text{K}$, so the copper ions are almost entirely consumed at equilibrium.
- **Electrical work and Gibbs energy** are connected by $\Delta_r G = -nFE_{\text{cell}}$, and at standard conditions $\Delta_r G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$, which for the iron and nickel reaction is $-36.67\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
- **Intensive and extensive:** $E_{\text{cell}}$ does not change when a reaction is doubled, while $\Delta_r G$ and $n$ do, so compare Gibbs energies only for equations written the same way.
</KeyIdeas>
