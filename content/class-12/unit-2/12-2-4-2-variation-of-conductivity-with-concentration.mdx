<Hook>
Pour a little vinegar into a beaker of water and its conductivity falls: fewer ions now sit in each cubic centimetre, so less current gets through. Yet if you ask how hard each mole of acetic acid works, the answer goes the other way, and the more water you add, the harder every mole works. How can the same solution both conduct worse and work better?
</Hook>

## Two ways of measuring conduction

The **conductivity**, $\kappa$, is the conductance of a cube of solution exactly $1\ \text{cm}$ on each side, held between two electrodes facing each other across it. Its unit is $\text{S cm}^{-1}$. Because it refers to a fixed volume, $\kappa$ reflects how many ions that volume holds, and it always falls when you dilute a solution, whether the electrolyte is strong or weak.

The **molar conductivity**, $\Lambda_m$, takes a different view. It is the conductance of the volume $V$ that contains exactly one mole of electrolyte, with the electrodes kept one centimetre apart. Since the electrode separation is fixed, $\Lambda_m = \kappa V$. Dilution shrinks $\kappa$, but it stretches $V$, and the stretch wins. That is why $\Lambda_m$ rises as the concentration falls.

## Strong electrolytes: a straight line against √c

For a strong electrolyte, $\Lambda_m$ rises only gently on dilution, and it follows

$$
\Lambda_m = \Lambda^\circ_m - A\,c^{1/2}
$$

Here $\Lambda^\circ_m$ is the **limiting molar conductivity**, the value at infinite dilution, and $A$ is a constant for a given solvent and temperature. Plot $\Lambda_m$ against $c^{1/2}$ and you get a straight line: the intercept is $\Lambda^\circ_m$ and the slope is $-A$. The constant $A$ depends on the charge types of the ions. Electrolytes such as $\ce{NaCl}$ (1-1), $\ce{CaCl2}$ (2-1) and $\ce{MgSO4}$ (2-2) each share one value of $A$ within their own type.

Four dilutions of potassium chloride at 298 K show the trend. A least-squares line through their $c^{1/2}$ values gives $\Lambda^\circ_m \approx 149.8\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $A \approx 87.4\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}\,(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{-1/2}$. Extrapolating the line to $c = 0$ is the only sensible way to reach the limiting value for a strong electrolyte, because a strong electrolyte is essentially fully dissociated at every concentration in the measured range.

## Weak electrolytes: a steep curve with no intercept

A weak electrolyte such as acetic acid is only partly dissociated at ordinary concentrations. When you dilute it, more molecules dissociate, so the number of ions in the mole-holding volume rises. This extra dissociation makes $\Lambda_m$ climb steeply, particularly at low concentrations, which is the opposite of the gentle rise seen for KCl.

That steepness is the problem. A weak electrolyte's $\Lambda_m$ curve does not extend to a straight line with a convenient intercept, and at the very dilute concentrations where the dissociation is complete, the conductance is too small to measure accurately. The limiting value $\Lambda^\circ_m$ therefore cannot be found by extrapolation. You need a different route.

## Kohlrausch's law of independent migration

Friedrich Kohlrausch noticed that the limiting values of related salts differ by almost the same amount. For example, $\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{KCl}) - \Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{NaCl})$ and $\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{KBr}) - \Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{NaBr})$ both come to about $23\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. From these regularities he proposed that each ion migrates independently of its partner, so a salt's limiting value is the sum of its ions' contributions:

$$
\Lambda^\circ_m = \nu_+\,\lambda^\circ_+ + \nu_-\,\lambda^\circ_-
$$

Here $\nu_+$ and $\nu_-$ count the cations and anions per formula unit, and $\lambda^\circ_+$ and $\lambda^\circ_-$ are their limiting ionic conductivities. Table 2.4 lists these for common ions. The difference between $\lambda^\circ(\ce{K+})$ and $\lambda^\circ(\ce{Na+})$ is $23.4$, and the difference between $\lambda^\circ(\ce{Br-})$ and $\lambda^\circ(\ce{Cl-})$ is $1.8$, both in $\text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.

The same rule predicts salts that were never measured directly. Using the table, $\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{MgSO4}) = 106.0 + 160.0 = 266.0\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$.

## Using Λ°m to find the strength of a weak acid

Once the limiting molar conductivity of a weak electrolyte is known from Kohlrausch's law, its degree of dissociation at any concentration is simply the ratio of the two values:

$$
\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda^\circ_m}
$$

For acetic acid, $\Lambda^\circ_m$ comes from two strong acids and salts that you can combine: $\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{CH3COOH}) = \lambda^\circ(\ce{H+}) + \lambda^\circ(\ce{CH3COO-}) = 390.5\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. Once $\alpha$ is known, the dissociation constant follows from the Ostwald dilution law $K_a = c\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)$. Measure $\Lambda_m$ at one concentration and you have both $\alpha$ and $K_a$.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Conductivity $\kappa$** (S cm⁻¹) counts the ions in a 1 cm cube, so it always falls on dilution, for weak and strong electrolytes alike.
- **Molar conductivity** $\Lambda_m = \kappa V$ rises on dilution because the volume $V$ holding one mole grows faster than $\kappa$ falls.
- **Strong electrolytes** give $\Lambda_m = \Lambda^\circ_m - A\,c^{1/2}$: a straight line whose intercept is the limiting value, and $A$ depends only on the charge types.
- **Weak electrolytes** have no usable intercept, so $\Lambda^\circ_m$ comes from Kohlrausch's law: $\Lambda^\circ_m = \nu_+\lambda^\circ_+ + \nu_-\lambda^\circ_-$ (for example, $\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{CaCl2}) = 271.6\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$).
- **Degree of dissociation** $\alpha = \Lambda_m / \Lambda^\circ_m$ and $K_a = c\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)$ turn one measured $\Lambda_m$ into the strength of a weak acid.
</KeyIdeas>
