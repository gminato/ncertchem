<Hook>
Pour a little vinegar into a beaker of water and its conductivity falls: fewer ions now sit in each cubic centimetre, so less current gets through. Yet if you ask how hard each mole of acetic acid works, the answer goes the other way, and the more water you add, the harder every mole works. How can the same solution both conduct worse and work better?
</Hook>

## Two ways of measuring conduction

The **conductivity**, $\kappa$, is the conductance of a cube of solution exactly $1\ \text{cm}$ on each side, held between two electrodes facing each other across it. Its unit is $\text{S cm}^{-1}$. Because it refers to a fixed volume, $\kappa$ reflects how many ions that volume holds, and it always falls when you dilute a solution, whether the electrolyte is strong or weak.

The **molar conductivity**, $\Lambda_m$, asks a different question: how well does one mole of electrolyte conduct? Take the volume $V$, in $\text{cm}^3$, that holds exactly one mole. If the concentration $c$ is in $\text{mol L}^{-1}$, then $V = 1000/c$. With the electrodes kept one centimetre apart, $\Lambda_m = \kappa V$:

$$
\Lambda_m = \kappa V
$$

With $\kappa$ in $\text{S cm}^{-1}$ and $V$ in $\text{cm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}$, the product comes out in $\text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. Dilution shrinks $\kappa$, but it stretches $V$, and the stretch wins. That is why $\Lambda_m$ rises as the concentration falls.

## Strong electrolytes: a straight line against √c

For a strong electrolyte, $\Lambda_m$ rises only slightly as the solution is diluted, and the rise is captured by a straight line in $c^{1/2}$:

$$
\Lambda_m = \Lambda^\circ_m - A\,c^{1/2}
$$

Here $\Lambda^\circ_m$ is the **limiting molar conductivity**, the value at infinite dilution, and $A$ is a constant for a given solvent and temperature. Plot $\Lambda_m$ against $c^{1/2}$ and the intercept is $\Lambda^\circ_m$, while the slope is $-A$. The value of $A$ depends on the charge types of the ions: a 1-1 salt such as $\ce{KCl}$ has its own $A$, and a 2-1 salt such as $\ce{MgCl2}$ has a different one.

Table 2.3 gives $\ce{KCl}$ at two dilute concentrations, $0.10$ and $0.010\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, with $\Lambda_m = 129.0$ and $141.0\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ at 298 K. Two points fix a straight line, so the line through them gives

$$
\Lambda^\circ_m \approx 146.5\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \qquad A \approx 55.5\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}\,(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{-1/2}
$$

Because the line rests on only two points, treat 146.5 as an estimate of the limiting value.

## Weak electrolytes: a steep curve with no intercept

Acetic acid is only partly dissociated at ordinary concentrations. Dilute it and a larger fraction of its molecules split into ions, so the mole-holding volume contains more ions than before. That extra dissociation pushes $\Lambda_m$ up steeply, most of all at low concentration, in contrast with the modest rise for KCl.

That steepness rules out extrapolation. The curve bends too sharply to give a usable intercept, and the most dilute readings are the hardest to measure. A different route is needed to find $\Lambda^\circ_m$.

## Kohlrausch's law of independent migration

Compare salts that share an anion. The potassium salt and the sodium salt differ in limiting value by the same amount, whatever that anion is. For the cations alone:

$$
\lambda^\circ(\ce{K+}) - \lambda^\circ(\ce{Na+}) = 73.5 - 50.1
$$

$$
= 23.4\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}
$$

This pattern points to independence: each ion carries its share of the current regardless of its partner, so a salt's limiting value is the sum of its ions' contributions:

$$
\Lambda^\circ_m = \nu_+\,\lambda^\circ_+ + \nu_-\,\lambda^\circ_-
$$

Here $\nu_+$ and $\nu_-$ count the cations and anions per formula unit, and $\lambda^\circ_+$ and $\lambda^\circ_-$ are their limiting ionic conductivities. Table 2.4 lists these for common ions. The same rule predicts salts that have never been measured directly, from their ions alone.

## Using Λ°m to find the strength of a weak acid

Once the limiting molar conductivity of a weak electrolyte is known, its degree of dissociation at any concentration is simply the ratio of the two values:

$$
\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda^\circ_m}
$$

For acetic acid, $\Lambda^\circ_m$ is the sum of its two ions in Table 2.4:

$$
\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{CH3COOH}) = \lambda^\circ(\ce{H+}) + \lambda^\circ(\ce{CH3COO-})
$$

$$
= 349.6 + 40.9 = 390.5\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}
$$

Once $\alpha$ is known, the dissociation constant follows from the Ostwald dilution law, $K_a = c\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)$. Measure $\Lambda_m$ at one concentration and you have both $\alpha$ and $K_a$.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Conductivity $\kappa$** (S cm⁻¹) counts the ions in a 1 cm cube, so it always falls on dilution, for weak and strong electrolytes alike.
- **Molar conductivity** $\Lambda_m = \kappa V$, with $V = 1000/c$ cm³ mol⁻¹, rises on dilution because the volume $V$ holding one mole grows faster than $\kappa$ falls.
- **Strong electrolytes** give $\Lambda_m = \Lambda^\circ_m - A\,c^{1/2}$: a straight line whose intercept is the limiting value, and $A$ depends only on the charge types.
- **Weak electrolytes** have no usable intercept, so $\Lambda^\circ_m$ comes from Kohlrausch's law: $\Lambda^\circ_m = \nu_+\lambda^\circ_+ + \nu_-\lambda^\circ_-$ (for example, $\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{MgCl2}) = 258.6\ \text{S cm}^2\ \text{mol}^{-1}$).
- **Degree of dissociation** $\alpha = \Lambda_m / \Lambda^\circ_m$ and $K_a = c\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)$ turn one measured $\Lambda_m$ into the strength of a weak acid.
</KeyIdeas>
