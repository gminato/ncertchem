An auto-rickshaw mechanic lifts the caps of an old lead–acid battery and dips a hydrometer into the acid to read its strength. Nobody does that to the sealed cell in a TV remote, yet both are batteries and both push electrons around a circuit. Why does one invite a fluid test while the other is thrown away when it dies? The answer lies in what the chemical reaction does to the electrolyte, and in whether that reaction can be run backwards.
</Hook>

## What makes a battery practical

Every battery is a galvanic cell in disguise: a spontaneous redox reaction that turns chemical energy into electrical energy. A laboratory cell is fine for a demonstration, but a battery you carry in a torch or bolt into a car has to meet three tests. It should be light and compact, so it fits where it is needed. Its voltage should hold steady while it works, so the device it powers behaves predictably. And it should last long enough to be worth its cost.

Batteries fall into two families. In a **primary battery** the reaction can happen only once: when the reactants are used up, the battery is dead and cannot be reused. In a **secondary battery** the reaction can be reversed by passing current through the cell in the opposite direction, so the same cell can be discharged and recharged many times.

## Primary cells: used once

The **dry cell**, also called the Leclanché cell after its discoverer, is the one in your torch and wall clock. Its zinc container doubles as the anode, so zinc atoms leave the metal as $\ce{Zn^2+}$ and release two electrons each. The cathode is a graphite rod sitting in a mixture of powdered manganese dioxide and carbon. Between the two lies a moist paste of ammonium chloride and zinc chloride. The electrode reactions are complicated in detail, but a useful summary is that manganese is reduced from the +4 to the +3 state, and the cell gives a potential of nearly 1.5 V.

The **mercury cell** is built for steady, low-current work, such as hearing aids and watches. Its anode is zinc dissolved in mercury, an amalgam, and its cathode is a paste of mercury(II) oxide and carbon. Around the electrodes sits an alkaline paste made from potassium hydroxide, with zinc oxide mixed in. Its overall reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Zn(Hg) + HgO(s)} \\
&\quad\ce{-> ZnO(s) + Hg(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

and the cell potential is about 1.35 V. The reason it stays put is the subject of the first Check below.

## Secondary cells: recharged again and again

The most important secondary cell is the **lead storage battery** that starts a car engine and runs the inverter in many homes. Its anode is lead, Pb, and its cathode is a grid of lead packed with lead dioxide, PbO₂. The electrolyte is 38% sulphuric acid. When the battery supplies current, both plates end up coated with lead sulphate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Anode:} \\
&\quad\ce{Pb(s) + SO4^2-(aq)} \\
&\qquad\ce{-> PbSO4(s) + 2e-} \\[0.6em]
&\text{Cathode:} \\
&\quad\ce{PbO2(s) + SO4^2-(aq)} \\
&\qquad\ce{+ 4H+(aq) + 2e-} \\
&\qquad\ce{-> PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the two gives the overall discharge reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Pb(s) + PbO2(s) + 2H2SO4(aq)} \\
&\quad\ce{-> 2PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the acid is consumed and water is produced, so the electrolyte gets weaker as the cell discharges. That is exactly what the hydrometer in the Hook measures.

Charging reverses the process. Current pushed back through the cell turns the PbSO₄ on the anode back into lead and the PbSO₄ on the cathode back into PbO₂, regenerating the acid. The **nickel–cadmium cell** is a second rechargeable choice. Its working life is longer than that of a lead storage cell, but building it costs more. Its positive plate, separator and negative plate are rolled into a compact spiral and wetted with an alkali solution. Its overall discharge reaction is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{Cd(s) + 2Ni(OH)3(s)} \\
&\quad\ce{-> CdO(s) + 2Ni(OH)2(s)} \\
&\qquad\ce{+ H2O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

<Step>
Start with the dry cell. Its zinc container is the anode, where $\ce{Zn -> Zn^2+ + 2e-}$ releases electrons into the external wire. The graphite rod at the centre is the cathode, sitting in powdered manganese dioxide and carbon. A moist paste of ammonium chloride and zinc chloride fills the gap, and the electrons travel from zinc to carbon through the circuit.
</Step>
<Step>
Next, the mercury cell of a hearing aid. Zinc dissolved in mercury, the amalgam, is the anode, and the cathode is mercury(II) oxide with carbon in a paste of potassium hydroxide and zinc oxide. The overall change turns solid $\ce{HgO}$ into liquid mercury and zinc into zinc oxide. Here a small bulb stands in for the device, and the potential stays at about 1.35 V for most of the cell's life.
</Step>
<Step>
Now the lead storage battery is discharging. The lead anode forms $\ce{PbSO4}$, and the $\ce{PbO2}$ cathode also turns into $\ce{PbSO4}$. Both plates grow a coating of lead sulphate while the sulphuric acid between them is used up and water appears. An acid-strength bar drops steadily. Electrons leave the lead plate, the anode, and travel through the external wire to the PbO₂ plate, while conventional current leaves the cell through the PbO₂ (+) terminal.
</Step>
<Step>
Connect a charger and push current the other way. The reaction runs in reverse: lead sulphate on the lead plate is turned back into lead, and lead sulphate on the other plate into $\ce{PbO2}$. The plate that was the anode is now the site of reduction and so acts as the cathode during charging. The acid bar rises again as sulphuric acid is regenerated.
</Step>
<Step>
Finally, the nickel–cadmium cell. Its positive plate, separator and negative plate are rolled in a jelly-roll stack and soaked in moist alkali. During discharge, cadmium and nickel(III) hydroxide give cadmium oxide and nickel(II) hydroxide, with water formed. The drawing shows this overall equation only, because the detailed electrode reactions are beyond what this Section needs.
</Step>

<KeyIdeas>
- **Primary batteries** such as the dry cell (about 1.5 V) and the mercury cell (about 1.35 V) are not designed to be recharged once their reactants are used up.
- The **mercury cell's potential stays steady** because its overall reaction involves no dissolved ion whose concentration changes during its life.
- In the **lead storage battery** both plates turn into PbSO₄ on discharge while the 38% H₂SO₄ electrolyte is consumed and water is formed; charging reverses the reactions.
- The **nickel–cadmium cell** is a rechargeable alternative with a longer life and a higher manufacturing cost, with overall discharge reaction Cd + 2Ni(OH)₃ → CdO + 2Ni(OH)₂ + H₂O.
- A **secondary battery** can be recharged because its discharge reaction can be reversed by passing current in the opposite direction.
</KeyIdeas>
