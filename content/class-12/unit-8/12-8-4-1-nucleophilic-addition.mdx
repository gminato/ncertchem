## Where the attack lands

Section 8.1 showed that the carbonyl carbon is δ+ and the oxygen is δ−. That polarity makes the carbon the place where an electron-rich species can dock. A species that brings a lone pair to an electron-poor carbon is called a nucleophile.

The nucleophile does not come in along the bonds that already exist. It approaches from a direction close to perpendicular to the plane that holds the carbonyl carbon's three σ bonds. We will follow the simplest case, a cyanide ion adding to ethanal, and watch the electrons move.

The charge is gone and the product is a neutral molecule: 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, CH₃CH(OH)CN, with OH and CN on the same carbon.

## Why aldehydes react faster than ketones

Ethanal and propan-2-one both have a carbonyl carbon that a cyanide ion can reach, but ethanal takes it up more readily. Two reasons work in the same direction. Sterically, a ketone has two fairly large groups around its carbonyl carbon, which makes it harder for the nucleophile to get in; an aldehyde has only one. Electronically, each alkyl group pushes a little electron density into the carbonyl carbon, and two such groups reduce its electrophilicity more than one does.

## Cyanide needs a helper

Pure hydrogen cyanide adds to a carbonyl compound very slowly. A base makes things far quicker, because it converts some HCN into the cyanide ion, CN⁻, which is a stronger nucleophile and adds readily. The product is a cyanohydrin, a molecule with an OH and a CN on the same carbon. Cyanohydrins are useful intermediates in synthesis, so this addition is worth knowing well.

$$\ce{CH3CHO + HCN ->[\text{base}] CH3CH(OH)CN}$$

Here the product is 2-hydroxypropanenitrile.

## A reversible grip on aldehydes

The bisulphite route is a balancing act. NaHSO₃ can join an aldehyde or a ketone, yet which side of the equilibrium wins depends on the structure of the carbonyl partner. For most aldehydes the balance sits on the product side; for most ketones it sits on the starting side, a steric effect. The adduct is soluble in water, and treating it with dilute acid or alkali brings back the original carbonyl compound. That reversibility is why chemists use the reaction to separate and purify aldehydes, which answers the Hook. Grignard reagents also add to carbonyl groups, but that addition is covered in Unit 7.

<KeyIdeas>
- A nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon from a direction close to perpendicular to the plane of the C=O, and that carbon changes from sp² to sp³.
- The tetrahedral alkoxide takes a proton from the medium, so the net result is that the nucleophile and an H add across the C=O.
- Aldehydes react faster than ketones: a ketone has two fairly large groups around its carbonyl carbon, and two alkyl groups lower its electrophilicity more than one does.
- HCN adds very slowly on its own; a base gives the stronger nucleophile CN⁻, and the product is a cyanohydrin.
- Sodium hydrogensulphite addition lies right for most aldehydes and left for most ketones, and dilute acid or alkali returns the carbonyl compound, which is how aldehydes are purified.
