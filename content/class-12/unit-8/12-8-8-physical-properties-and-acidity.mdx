<Hook>
Drop baking soda into vinegar and it fizzes at once. Drop it into a phenol solution and nothing happens, even though both are called acids. What makes one acid strong enough to push carbon dioxide out of a carbonate?
</Hook>

## Pairs of molecules and a high boiling point

A short carboxylic acid is a colourless liquid with an unpleasant smell. Up to nine carbon atoms the acids are still liquids at room temperature, and the longer ones are waxy solids that barely smell, because they hardly evaporate.

Their boiling points are surprisingly high. An acid boils above aldehydes and ketones of similar mass, and above alcohols of similar mass too. The reason is the hydrogen bond. Each acid molecule has an O–H group that grabs onto the C=O oxygen of a neighbour, so molecules pair up into dimers. Those pairs survive even in the vapour, and in solvents that do not donate protons they still stay together.

## Mixing with water and dissolving

Acids with up to four carbon atoms mix with water in every proportion, since each one forms hydrogen bonds with water. As the hydrocarbon part grows, the molecule becomes less willing to mix, and solubility falls. Benzoic acid, which carries a benzene ring, is nearly insoluble in cold water. All these acids dissolve well in less polar solvents such as benzene, ether, alcohol and chloroform.

## Three reactions that show the acid

A carboxylic acid can give up its O–H proton in three ways that a student can see. With an electropositive metal such as sodium, it releases hydrogen gas and forms a salt.

$$
\ce{2CH3COOH + 2Na -> 2CH3COONa + H2}
$$

With an alkali, it neutralises to a salt and water.

$$
\ce{CH3COOH + NaOH -> CH3COONa + H2O}
$$

With a carbonate or a hydrogencarbonate, it releases carbon dioxide. Phenols, which are also acidic, do not do this, so the fizz is the test for a carboxyl group in an unknown compound.

$$
\ce{CH3COOH + NaHCO3 -> CH3COONa + H2O + CO2}
$$

## How strong is an acid?

When an acid meets water, the proton passes to the water and forms the hydronium ion, while the acid's anion is left behind. The reaction is an equilibrium, so it has an equilibrium constant, and the acid dissociation constant $K_a$ is written from it.

$$
\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO- + H3O+}
$$

$$
K_a = \frac{[\ce{CH3COO-}][\ce{H3O+}]}{[\ce{CH3COOH}]}
$$

Working with $K_a$ values is awkward, because they run over many powers of ten. Chemists therefore quote $pK_a = -\log K_a$. A smaller $pK_a$ means a stronger acid, which is a better proton donor. Chemists sort acids into four bands by this number. The strong band lies below 1, the next band reaches up to 5, and weak acids occupy the range that runs to 15. Anything above that is extremely weak.

## Why a carboxylic acid beats a phenol

A carboxylic acid is weaker than a mineral acid such as hydrochloric acid, yet it is a stronger acid than an alcohol or a simple phenol. Phenol, with $pK_a$ near 10, is a weak acid. Ethanol, whose $pK_a$ is about 16, is extremely weak.

The difference is in the conjugate base. Acetate can be drawn in two resonance forms that are exactly equivalent, and in both of them the negative charge sits on an oxygen atom, which is the more electronegative element. In phenoxide the charge sits on the oxygen in one form and on ring carbons in the others, and those forms are not equivalent. Acetate spreads its charge over two oxygens, whereas phenoxide spreads it over one oxygen and several less electronegative carbons. The more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid.

Attaching a phenyl ring directly to the carboxyl carbon makes the acid stronger, even though resonance would normally be expected to push the effect the other way. The reason is that the ring carbon is sp² hybridised, and an sp² carbon is more electronegative than an sp³ carbon, so the carboxyl group sits on a more electron-pulling framework. That is why benzoic acid sits above acetic acid in the strip.

Ring substituents act in the same direction on benzoic acid. An electron-withdrawing nitro group on the ring makes the acid stronger, and an electron-donating methoxy group makes it weaker. The effect is visible on a scale that uses $pK_a$ values alone.

<KeyIdeas>
- Carboxylic acids form hydrogen-bonded dimers, even in the vapour, so they boil higher than aldehydes, ketones and alcohols of similar mass.
- Acids with up to four carbons mix with water; solubility falls as the hydrocarbon part grows, and benzoic acid is nearly insoluble in cold water.
- Carboxylic acids release hydrogen with electropositive metals, form salts with alkalis, and release CO₂ with carbonates and hydrogencarbonates, which phenols do not do.
- A smaller $pK_a$ means a stronger acid, and the four bands run from strong (below 1) through moderately strong and weak to extremely weak (above 15).
- The carboxylate ion shares its charge equally between two oxygen atoms, which makes carboxylic acids stronger than phenols; electron-withdrawing groups strengthen the acid and electron-donating groups weaken it.
</KeyIdeas>
