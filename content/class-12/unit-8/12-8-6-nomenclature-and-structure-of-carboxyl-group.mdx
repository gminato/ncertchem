<Hook>
Red ants and vinegar have almost nothing in common, yet the acids in both were named after the Latin word for their source: formica for the ant, acetum for the vinegar. A name records where a molecule was found, not what it does. So what single structural feature makes these molecules acids?
</Hook>

Every carboxylic acid carries the same functional group, –COOH. It is a C=O and an –OH stacked on one carbon, and its name, carboxyl, is built from those two parts. The Scrolly takes the group apart, then shows how the names and the family of related compounds follow from it.

<Scrolly animation="carboxyl-group">
  <Step>
  Acetic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, is drawn with its carboxyl group in full. The carboxyl carbon has three bonds to other atoms: to the double-bonded oxygen, to the –OH and to the methyl group. All three lie in one plane, and the angles between them are about 120°.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The drawing now shows the two resonance structures of the group. In the second, a lone pair on the –OH oxygen is pushed into the C=O bond, so that oxygen carries a positive charge and the carbonyl oxygen a negative one. This extra donation makes the carboxyl carbon less electrophilic than the carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde or a ketone.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  Three acids show where the common names came from. Formic acid, $\ce{HCOOH}$, was first obtained from red ants, and its name comes from the Latin formica. Acetic acid comes from vinegar (Latin acetum). Butyric acid, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}$, comes from rancid butter (Latin butyrum). Each common name ends in –ic acid.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The IUPAC name follows the chain. Take the alkane with the same carbons, drop its final –e and add –oic acid, so pentane becomes pentanoic acid. The carboxyl carbon is always carbon 1. Here the methyl branch sits on carbon 3, which gives 3-methylpentanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2COOH}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  Two carboxyl groups use the same rule with a multiplier. Heptanedioic acid, $\ce{HOOC(CH2)5COOH}$, has a COOH at each end of a seven-carbon chain. A benzene ring can carry the group too: benzoic acid, $\ce{C6H5COOH}$, has one, and benzene-1,2-dicarboxylic acid, commonly called phthalic acid, has two on adjacent ring carbons numbered 1 and 2.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The carboxyl group is the parent of a family. Swap its –OH for a halogen and you get an acyl halide, $\ce{CH3COCl}$. Join two acyl groups through one oxygen and you get an acid anhydride, $\ce{(CH3CO)2O}$. Replace the –OH with –OR and you get an ester, drawn here with R as ethyl, and with –NH₂ you get an amide. All four carry the same acyl group, CH₃CO–.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- The –COOH group is a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on one carbon. Its three bonds to that carbon lie in one plane, about 120° apart.
- Resonance from the –OH oxygen makes the carboxyl carbon less electrophilic than the carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde or a ketone.
- Common names come from natural sources and end in –ic acid: formic (red ants), acetic (vinegar), butyric (rancid butter).
- In IUPAC names the –e of the alkane becomes –oic acid, the carboxyl carbon is carbon 1, and two groups give a –dioic acid.
- Acyl halides, acid anhydrides, esters and amides all carry the acyl group CH₃CO– from the carboxyl group.
</KeyIdeas>
