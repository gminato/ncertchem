In a teaching lab, a few drops of a reagent turn an unknown liquid into a brightly coloured solid, and the chemist learns that the liquid carries a carbonyl group. What did the reagent do to the molecule to change it so visibly?
</Hook>

## Two kinds of partner for the carbonyl carbon

The carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde or a ketone is a good target for a nucleophile, a species that offers a lone pair to an electron-poor atom. Two families of partner are worth knowing here. Alcohols add across the C=O bond to give acetals and ketals. Ammonia and its relatives add as well, but they go on to lose water, and the product carries a C=N bond.

## Alcohols need an acid

An alcohol will not attack a carbonyl carbon on its own in any useful amount. Dry hydrogen chloride changes that. The acid puts a proton on the carbonyl oxygen, and the carbon next to it becomes more electrophilic, so an alcohol can now attack it. The acid is not used up in the product; it simply makes the reaction possible.

The number of alcohol molecules that join depends on the carbonyl compound. An aldehyde reacts with one equivalent of an alcohol to give a hemiacetal, a carbon carrying both an OH and an OR group. That hemiacetal then reacts with a second alcohol to give an acetal, in which the carbon carries two OR groups. Ketones react with ethylene glycol instead, a diol whose two OH groups close onto the same carbon to form a ring. That ring is an ethylene glycol ketal.

Acetals and ketals are not difficult to undo. Aqueous mineral acid hydrolyses them back to the aldehyde or ketone they came from.

## Ammonia and its relatives add and lose water

The same opening move happens when a $\ce{H2N–Z}$ reagent, ammonia included, meets an aldehyde or ketone: the nitrogen reaches for the carbonyl carbon. The second half of the story differs, because the intermediate then gives up water and the product has a C=N–Z group in place of the C=O. Acid catalyses the reaction, and the reaction can run in either direction. What tips the balance toward the C=N product is how quickly the intermediate loses water.

Table 8.2 lists the Z groups that are most often used. Each row names the reagent, the Z group it brings and the product. Derivatives made with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine tend to be coloured solids in the yellow-to-red range, which makes them handy for pinning down which aldehyde or ketone a sample was.

  Butan-2-one, $\ce{CH3CH2C(CH3)=O}$, is a ketone, so its carbonyl carbon has two carbon groups and no hydrogen. Ethylene glycol, $\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}$, arrives with an OH at each end. The two OH groups close onto the carbonyl carbon in turn, and a five-membered ring forms from two oxygens and three carbons, with the two ring oxygens highlighted. The product is a cyclic ethylene glycol ketal, $\ce{C6H12O2}$, and water is released.
  </Step>

<KeyIdeas>
- Dry HCl protonates the carbonyl oxygen and so makes the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic, which is what lets an alcohol add.
- An aldehyde with one alcohol gives a hemiacetal, and with a second alcohol it gives an acetal, such as $\ce{CH3CH2CH(OCH3)2}$. A ketone with ethylene glycol gives a cyclic ethylene glycol ketal.
- Aqueous mineral acid hydrolyses acetals and ketals back to the aldehyde or ketone.
- Ammonia and its derivatives $\ce{H2N-Z}$ add to the carbonyl group, lose water and give a C=N–Z product. Table 8.2 lists the common Z groups, and the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazones are yellow, orange or red solids.
</KeyIdeas>
