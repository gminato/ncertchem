<Hook>
Benzaldehyde is the compound behind the bitter-almond note of badam essence, a flavouring used in many sweets. Yet the methyl group that benzaldehyde can come from is exactly the group a strong oxidising agent pushes straight past the aldehyde, into benzoic acid. How do chemists make the oxidation stop halfway?
</Hook>

The carbon skeleton of an aromatic aldehyde is already there in methylbenzene, which chemists also call toluene. Its CH₃ group sits on the ring, and that group can be oxidised. The difficulty is control. A strong oxidising agent keeps going, and the side chain finishes as a carboxylic acid.

Each method below handles the methyl group through an intermediate. Hydrolysing that intermediate then releases the aldehyde, so the oxidation never reaches the acid stage.

<Scrolly animation="aromatic-carbonyl-routes">
  <Step>
    **The overshoot.** Begin with methylbenzene, a ring carrying one CH₃ group. A strong oxidising agent attacks that group, and the carbon climbs the oxidation ladder. Benzaldehyde appears only as a passing rung. The reaction carries on until the side chain is a COOH group, and the product is benzoic acid.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Chromyl chloride.** Dissolve $\ce{CrO2Cl2}$, chromyl chloride, in carbon disulphide and add it to methylbenzene. It oxidises the CH₃ group only as far as a chromium complex, an intermediate that is hard to oxidise further. Hydrolysis of that complex with H₃O⁺ releases benzaldehyde. This is the Etard reaction.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Two acetates on one carbon.** Chromic oxide, $\ce{CrO3}$, in acetic anhydride at 273–283 K turns methylbenzene into benzylidene diacetate, $\ce{C6H5CH(OCOCH3)2}$. Both acetate groups hang on the carbon that used to be the methyl. Aqueous acid then hydrolyses the diacetate, and benzaldehyde is left behind.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Chlorine on the side chain.** Light drives chlorine into the methyl group. CH₃ becomes CHCl₂, which makes benzal chloride, $\ce{C6H5CHCl2}$. Water at 373 K hydrolyses benzal chloride to benzaldehyde and two molecules of HCl. This side-chain route is the one used for commercial manufacture of benzaldehyde.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Starting from the ring.** Now the start is benzene, with no side chain at all. Feed in carbon monoxide and hydrogen chloride, and a CHO group lands on the ring once anhydrous aluminium chloride or cuprous chloride is present. The product is benzaldehyde. This is the Gatterman–Koch reaction.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Two routes to one ketone.** Acetophenone, 1-phenylethan-1-one, can be reached from a compound that already carries the benzene ring and one carbon. Benzoyl chloride meets dimethylcadmium, $\ce{(CH3)2Cd}$, and gives acetophenone. Benzonitrile meets $\ce{CH3MgBr}$, and hydrolysis afterwards gives the same ketone.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A third route, from benzene itself.** Benzene meets ethanoyl chloride under anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$, and the acyl group joins the ring. The product is acetophenone again, with hydrogen chloride as the other product.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

Chemists make the dialkylcadmium reagent by treating cadmium chloride with a Grignard reagent. The cadmium reagent then reacts with an acyl chloride, and the ketone forms with cadmium chloride as the other product:

$$
\ce{2C6H5COCl + (CH3)2Cd -> 2C6H5COCH3 + CdCl2}
$$

The Friedel–Crafts acylation builds the ketone directly on the ring. Under anhydrous aluminium chloride, an acid chloride can hand its acyl group to benzene:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + CH3COCl ->[\text{anhydrous AlCl3}] C6H5COCH3 + HCl}
$$

The nitrile route starts from a different functional group. Treat a nitrile with a Grignard reagent and then hydrolyse, and a ketone results:

$$
\ce{C6H5CN ->[1. CH3MgBr][2. H3O+] C6H5COCH3}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- A strong oxidising agent carries the side-chain CH₃ of methylbenzene past benzaldehyde to benzoic acid, so the aldehyde needs a special route.
- The Etard reaction (CrO₂Cl₂ in CS₂, then H₃O⁺) and the chromic oxide route (CrO₃ in acetic anhydride at 273–283 K, then aqueous acid) both give benzaldehyde through an intermediate that is hydrolysed.
- Side-chain chlorination gives benzal chloride, and hydrolysis at 373 K gives benzaldehyde. It is the commercial method.
- The Gatterman–Koch reaction puts a CHO group on benzene, with CO and HCl in the presence of anhydrous AlCl₃ or CuCl.
- Ketones come from acyl chlorides with dialkylcadmium, from nitriles with a Grignard reagent followed by hydrolysis, and from benzene with an acid chloride in the Friedel–Crafts acylation.
</KeyIdeas>
