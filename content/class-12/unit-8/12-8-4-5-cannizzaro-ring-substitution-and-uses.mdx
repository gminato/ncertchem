<Hook>

Methanal, the molecule in formalin, has no carbon beside its C=O that carries a hydrogen. Pour it into concentrated alkali and one molecule ends up as an alcohol while another ends up as an acid salt. How can one aldehyde do both jobs?

</Hook>

This Section covers two further reactions of the carbonyl group. The first is the Cannizzaro reaction, where an aldehyde with no α-hydrogen reacts with itself. The second is substitution on the ring of an aromatic aldehyde or ketone, where the C=O decides where a new group lands. The Section ends with the uses of these compounds.

## Aldehydes with no α-hydrogen

An α-hydrogen is a hydrogen on the carbon directly next to the C=O. Methanal has none, since no carbon sits beside its C=O at all. Benzaldehyde's C=O is bonded to a ring carbon that carries no hydrogen. On heating with concentrated alkali, such an aldehyde does not stay put: one molecule is reduced to an alcohol while another is oxidised to the salt of a carboxylic acid.

$$
\ce{2HCHO + NaOH ->[\text{heat, conc.}] HCOO^-Na^+ + CH3OH}
$$

The same pattern holds for benzaldehyde, which gives phenylmethanol and a carboxylate salt of benzoic acid.

$$
\ce{2C6H5CHO + NaOH ->[\text{heat, conc.}] C6H5COO^-Na^+ + C6H5CH2OH}
$$

## Reactivity of the ring

Benzaldehyde's aromatic ring can still undergo electrophilic substitution, which is the reaction type you met for benzene. The carbonyl group, however, changes the outcome. It deactivates the ring and directs incoming groups to the meta position. Nitration of benzaldehyde therefore gives 3-nitrobenzaldehyde, not the ortho or para isomer.

The same statement covers aromatic ketones, so one rule serves both families.

## Uses of aldehydes and ketones

Industry uses these compounds in three ways. Some serve as solvents, some as the raw material for other products, and some as reagents in the next step of a synthesis. Methanal is the most familiar example in daily life, sold as a 40% solution called formalin. It keeps biological specimens intact, and it is also the starting point for bakelite, a phenol–formaldehyde resin, and for urea–formaldehyde glues.

Ethanal is mainly a building block. Chemists turn it into acetic acid and several esters, and it also feeds into polymers and some drugs. Benzaldehyde finds work in perfumes and in dyes, and the small ketones acetone and butan-2-one are common solvents in industry.

Several others are known for their odours and flavours rather than for bulk use: butyraldehyde, vanillin, acetophenone and camphor are examples.

<KeyIdeas>

- An aldehyde with no α-hydrogen undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction on heating with concentrated alkali: one molecule is reduced to an alcohol, and another is oxidised to the salt of a carboxylic acid.
- The carbonyl group deactivates an aromatic ring and directs electrophilic substitution to the meta position.
- Formalin, a 40% solution of methanal, preserves biological specimens and supplies bakelite and urea–formaldehyde glues.
- Ethanal, benzaldehyde, acetone and butan-2-one are important industrial starting materials and solvents.
