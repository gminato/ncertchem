<Hook>
Two identical small molecules of ethanal can join into one bigger molecule just by standing in a little dilute alkali. Which hydrogen makes this possible, and why that one?
</Hook>

## The hydrogen next to the carbonyl

In ethanal, CH₃CHO, the carbon next to the carbonyl group is the CH₃ carbon. Its hydrogens are called α-hydrogens, and they behave differently from the hydrogens on the same carbon in an ordinary alkane. A proton can be removed from an α-carbon far more readily than from a typical C–H bond.

Two features of the carbonyl group explain this. The group pulls electron density towards its oxygen, which makes the α-C–H bond more easily broken. And once the proton is gone, the negative charge left on the α-carbon can be spread over the carbonyl group by resonance, so the conjugate base is stabilised. Aldehydes and ketones that carry at least one α-hydrogen can therefore take part in a family of reactions that the others cannot.

## Aldol: one molecule adds to another

Dilute alkali is enough to start the first reaction. An aldehyde or ketone with at least one α-hydrogen gives a β-hydroxy aldehyde, called an aldol, or a β-hydroxy ketone, called a ketol. The name aldol comes from the two groups in the product, an aldehyde and an alcohol.

$$
\ce{2 CH3CHO ->[dil. OH-] CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO}
$$

The product carries the hydroxyl group on the carbon two steps away from the carbonyl carbon, the β-carbon, which is why it is called β-hydroxy. The walk-through below follows one ethanal molecule through this change.

## Losing water: the aldol condensation

The aldol is not always the end of the story. On heating, aldols and ketols readily lose a molecule of water. The C=C double bond that forms conjugates with the carbonyl group, and the product is an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound. The whole sequence, addition followed by loss of water, is called aldol condensation.

$$
\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CHO ->[\Delta] CH3CH=CHCHO + H2O}
$$

Ketones take part too. Their ketols lose water in the same way, and chemists still use the name aldol condensation for these reactions, because they behave very like the aldehyde cases.

## Cross aldol: two different partners

When two different aldehydes or ketones are mixed under the same conditions, the reaction is called a cross aldol condensation. If both partners have α-hydrogens, each one can lose an α-hydrogen to form a carbanion and can also be attacked as the electrophile. The result is a mixture of four products, not one.

Acetone is a useful ketone partner. Two molecules of propanone under dilute alkali give a ketol, 4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one:

$$
\ce{2 CH3COCH3 ->[dil. OH-] (CH3)2C(OH)CH2COCH3}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- An α-hydrogen is more acidic than a typical C–H bond, because the carbonyl group withdraws electron density and the conjugate base is stabilised by resonance.
- Under dilute alkali, an aldehyde or ketone with at least one α-hydrogen adds to a second molecule, giving a β-hydroxy aldehyde (aldol) or a β-hydroxy ketone (ketol).
- Heating an aldol or a ketol removes water and gives an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound: the aldol condensation.
- In a cross aldol with two partners that both have α-hydrogens, four products form. If one partner has no α-hydrogen, it cannot form a carbanion, so the products are the other partner's self-aldol and one cross product.
- Acetone under dilute alkali gives a ketol, 4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one, which shows that ketones can take part in the same reactions.
</KeyIdeas>
