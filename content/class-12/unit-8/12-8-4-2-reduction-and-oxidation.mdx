## Reducing the carbonyl group

The C=O bond of an aldehyde or a ketone can take up hydrogen, and the product depends on the reagent. Sodium borohydride ($\ce{NaBH4}$), lithium aluminium hydride ($\ce{LiAlH4}$) and catalytic hydrogenation all stop at the alcohol. An aldehyde becomes a primary alcohol and a ketone becomes a secondary alcohol.

A different pair of conditions goes further and removes the oxygen. Two named routes strip the oxygen out and leave a $\ce{CH2}$ group in its place. Clemmensen's version uses zinc amalgam in full-strength hydrochloric acid. In the Wolff–Kishner route, hydrazine is added first, and then the mixture is heated with a strong alkali in a solvent that boils at a high temperature, such as ethylene glycol. Both routes give a hydrocarbon. The walk-through below follows one aldehyde down both routes.

## Oxidising an aldehyde, and a ketone that resists

Aldehydes are oxidised to carboxylic acids by the common oxidants, such as nitric acid, potassium permanganate and potassium dichromate. Even the mild reagents, Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution, oxidise them. Ketones need strong oxidants and high temperatures, and when they do break down, the C–C bonds next to the carbonyl group are cut, leaving a mixture of acids with fewer carbon atoms than the ketone.

## The tests in the tube

The three tests use the same logic: a mild reagent changes an aldehyde and leaves a ketone alone. In Tollens' test, the silver ions are reduced to silver while the aldehyde is oxidised, as the simplified equation shows:

$$
\ce{RCHO + 2Ag+ + 3OH- -> RCOO- + 2Ag(s) + 2H2O}
$$

Tollens' test gives a silver mirror on the glass wall. Fehling's test gives a reddish-brown precipitate when the solution is heated. Fehling's test does not work for every aldehyde, though: aromatic aldehydes such as benzaldehyde do not respond to it.

The iodoform test is a different kind of test. It picks out the $\ce{CH3CO}$ group in a methyl ketone, and also the $\ce{CH3CH(OH)}$ group, because that group is oxidised to a $\ce{CH3CO}$ group. Propan-2-ol, which carries this group, gives a yellow iodoform precipitate. The haloform reaction does not affect a C=C double bond if one is present:

$$
\ce{CH3COCH2CH3 + 3NaOI -> CH3CH2COONa + CHI3(s) + 2NaOH}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- Sodium borohydride, lithium aluminium hydride and catalytic hydrogenation reduce an aldehyde to a primary alcohol and a ketone to a secondary alcohol.
- The Clemmensen reduction (zinc amalgam with concentrated $\ce{HCl}$) and the Wolff–Kishner reduction (hydrazine, then $\ce{NaOH}$ or $\ce{KOH}$ with heat in a high-boiling solvent) reduce the C=O group to $\ce{CH2}$.
- Aldehydes are oxidised to carboxylic acids by strong oxidants and also by the mild Tollens' and Fehling's reagents, while ketones need vigorous conditions and then break into acids with fewer carbons.
- Tollens' test gives a silver mirror, and Fehling's test gives a reddish-brown precipitate. Fehling's solution leaves aromatic aldehydes unchanged.
- Methyl ketones, and compounds such as propan-2-ol that give them on oxidation, are oxidised by sodium hypohalite to a carboxylic acid with one carbon fewer and to the haloform, $\ce{CHX3}$, which for iodine is the yellow iodoform.
</KeyIdeas>
