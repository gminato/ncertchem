<Hook>
Dry ice is only solid carbon dioxide, the gas we breathe out. Drop it into a flask of Grignard reagent and a new carbon–carbon bond forms, and the carbon of that gas becomes part of an acid. How can a gas join an alkyl group to make a carboxylic acid?
</Hook>

## Six starting points, one group

Every carboxylic acid carries the –COOH group, and chemists have several ways to build that group. Some routes oxidise a carbon that already carries oxygen. Some hydrolyse a bond to nitrogen or chlorine with water. One adds a whole new carbon. The walk-through below follows six of them, each ending on a different acid.

<Scrolly animation="acid-routes">
  <Step>
  The –COOH group sits at the centre of the scene, one carbon with a double-bonded oxygen and an –OH. The first route arrives from hexan-1-ol, a primary alcohol whose $\ce{CH2OH}$ group sits at one end of the chain. Alkaline $\ce{KMnO4}$ oxidises that carbon, and $\ce{H3O+}$ then acidifies the mixture to give hexanoic acid, $\ce{C6H12O2}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The second route starts from hexylbenzene, a benzene ring carrying a six-carbon chain. Hot alkaline $\ce{KMnO4}$ attacks the side chain. The whole chain is replaced by a single COOH on the ring, and after $\ce{H3O+}$ the product is benzoic acid, $\ce{C6H5COOH}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The third route starts from 3-phenylpropanenitrile, which carries a $\ce{C#N}$ group on a three-carbon chain. Water with $\ce{H+}$ or $\ce{OH-}$ turns the $\ce{C#N}$ group into $\ce{CONH2}$, giving 3-phenylpropanamide. With heat and the same catalyst, that amide is hydrolysed on to 3-phenylpropanoic acid, and $\ce{NH3}$ leaves.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The fourth route starts from 1-bromobutane. In dry ether it becomes the Grignard reagent, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2MgBr}$. That reagent takes up $\ce{CO2}$ from dry ice, and the new carbon is the one from the gas. $\ce{H3O+}$ then gives pentanoic acid, $\ce{CH3(CH2)3COOH}$, with five carbons: one more than the four in 1-bromobutane.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The fifth route needs only water. Ethanoyl chloride, $\ce{CH3COCl}$, reacts with water to give ethanoic acid and $\ce{HCl}$. Ethanoic anhydride, $\ce{(CH3CO)2O}$, gives two molecules of ethanoic acid. With aqueous base the acyl chloride is hydrolysed more readily, and the drawing shows the carboxylate ion $\ce{CH3COO-}$ before acidification.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The last route begins with ethyl butanoate, an ester with a $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CO}$ part and an $\ce{OCH2CH3}$ part. Acid and water split it into butanoic acid and ethanol. In base the same ester gives the carboxylate ion $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COO-}$ and ethanol, and $\ce{H3O+}$ turns that ion back into butanoic acid.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Oxidation of alcohols, aldehydes and side chains

A primary alcohol carries the –$\ce{CH2OH}$ group, and common oxidants can carry it all the way to the acid. Permanganate does the job whether the medium is neutral, acidic or basic. Dichromate and chromium trioxide need an acidic medium, and the chromium trioxide mixture in aqueous sulfuric acid is the Jones reagent. Aldehydes can also be taken to the acid, with gentler oxidants that Section 8.4 explains.

Vigorous oxidation of an alkyl benzene, with chromic acid or with acidic or alkaline potassium permanganate, takes the whole side chain to a carboxyl group, however long the chain is. The reagents attack side chains whose first carbon is primary or secondary. A tertiary alkyl group is not affected, so tert-butylbenzene survives the same treatment that converts sec-butylbenzene. Suitably substituted alkenes are oxidised to carboxylic acids by these same reagents.

## Nitriles, amides and Grignard reagents

Nitriles are hydrolysed through the amide: $\ce{H+}$ or $\ce{OH-}$ as catalyst converts $\ce{RCN}$ to $\ce{RCONH2}$ and then to $\ce{RCOOH}$. Gentle conditions leave the reaction at the amide, which is the intermediate in this route.

Grignard reagents add to carbon dioxide in dry ether to form a salt of a carboxylic acid. Mineral acid then releases the free acid. This route and the nitrile route both turn an alkyl halide into an acid with one carbon atom more, because Grignard reagents and nitriles are themselves made from alkyl halides, as Unit 6 showed.

## Acyl chlorides, anhydrides and esters

Acid chlorides hydrolyse with water to give the carboxylic acid. Aqueous base hydrolyses them more readily, giving carboxylate ions that acid converts into the carboxylic acid. Anhydrides hydrolyse with water to give the corresponding acid or acids.

Acidic hydrolysis of an ester gives the carboxylic acid directly. Basic hydrolysis gives a carboxylate, and acidifying that carboxylate gives the acid.

$$
\ce{RCN + 2H2O ->[\text{H+ or OH-, heat}] RCOOH + NH3}
$$

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2MgBr + CO2 ->[\text{dry ether}] CH3CH2CH2CH2COOMgBr}
$$

$$
\ce{CH3COCl + H2O -> CH3COOH + HCl}
$$

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3 + H2O ->[\text{H+}] CH3CH2CH2COOH + CH3CH2OH}
$$

<KeyIdeas>
- Primary alcohols and aldehydes give carboxylic acids on oxidation: $\ce{KMnO4}$ in neutral, acidic or alkaline solution, or $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ and $\ce{CrO3}$ in acid (the Jones reagent).
- Vigorous oxidation of an alkyl benzene turns the whole side chain into one COOH, whatever its length; a tertiary alkyl group is not affected.
- Nitriles hydrolyse through the amide, and mild conditions stop at the amide stage: $\ce{RCN + 2H2O -> RCOOH + NH3}$ under acid or base, with heat to reach the acid.
- A Grignard reagent takes up $\ce{CO2}$, and $\ce{H3O+}$ then gives the acid, one carbon longer than the alkyl halide the Grignard reagent came from.
- Acyl chlorides and anhydrides hydrolyse with water; an ester gives the acid directly in acid, or a carboxylate in base that acidifies to the acid.
</KeyIdeas>
