<Hook>
Formalin, the preservative in many biology-lab jars, is methanal dissolved in water. Methanal is made on a large scale by passing methanol vapour over a hot metal. How can a metal surface strip hydrogen out of an alcohol, and why does the vapour not simply burn?
</Hook>

## Five starting points, one carbonyl group

A carbonyl group has its carbon and oxygen joined by a double bond. The preparations start from a different functional group each time and leave a C=O behind. Five starting points cover the routes in this Section: alcohols, alkenes, alkynes, acyl chlorides and nitriles.

Alcohols are the easiest. A primary alcohol is oxidised to an aldehyde and a secondary alcohol to a ketone, using the oxidants you met in Unit 7:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CH2CH2CHO + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH3 + [O] -> CH3CH2COCH2CH3 + H2O}
$$

The first product is butanal and the second is pentan-3-one. Volatile alcohols can also be dehydrogenated, which means their vapour is passed over a heavy-metal catalyst, silver or copper, and hydrogen gas comes away. This suits volatile alcohols and is used on an industrial scale. Methanol gives methanal this way:

$$
\ce{CH3OH ->[\text{Ag or Cu}] CH2O + H2}
$$

Alkenes and alkynes give carbonyl compounds when their multiple bonds are broken or hydrated. Ozonolysis cuts an alkene at its double bond. Ozone is followed by zinc dust and water, and the two halves of the molecule each end in a carbonyl group. Which halves you get depends on how the alkene is substituted:

$$
\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH3 ->[1. O3][2. Zn, H2O] (CH3)2C=O + CH3CHO}
$$

Water adds across an alkyne in the presence of sulfuric acid and mercury(II) sulfate. Ethyne is the one exception, giving ethanal. Every other alkyne gives a ketone:

$$
\ce{CH3C#CCH3 + H2O ->[\text{H2SO4, HgSO4}] CH3COCH2CH3}
$$

## Routes from acyl chlorides and nitriles

Acyl chlorides and nitriles need a reducing step rather than an oxidant. An acyl chloride is reduced to an aldehyde when hydrogen is passed over palladium on barium sulphate. This is called the Rosenmund reduction:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2COCl + H2 ->[\text{Pd–BaSO4}] CH3CH2CH2CHO + HCl}
$$

A nitrile goes to an aldehyde through an imine. Stannous chloride in hydrochloric acid reduces the C≡N group to CH=NH, and hydrolysis of the imine then gives the aldehyde. This is the Stephen reaction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\ce{CH3(CH2)3CN + 2 [H] ->[\text{SnCl2, HCl}] CH3(CH2)3CH=NH} \\
&\ce{CH3(CH2)3CH=NH + H2O ->[\text{H3O+}] CH3(CH2)3CHO + NH3}
\end{aligned}
$$

Diisobutylaluminium hydride, written DIBAL-H, reduces a nitrile only as far as the imine. Hydrolysis then gives the aldehyde. DIBAL-H also reduces esters to aldehydes, so ethyl butanoate, for example, can be converted into butanal this way.

<KeyIdeas>
- Oxidising a primary alcohol gives an aldehyde and a secondary alcohol gives a ketone; dehydrogenation over Ag or Cu does the same with H₂ as the by-product.
- Ozonolysis followed by zinc and water cuts an alkene at its double bond, and the two pieces each end in a carbonyl group.
- Hydration of an alkyne with H₂SO₄ and HgSO₄ gives a ketone, except for ethyne, which gives ethanal.
- The Rosenmund reduction turns an acyl chloride into an aldehyde with H₂ over Pd–BaSO₄; the Stephen reaction and DIBAL-H both go through an imine on the way from a nitrile to an aldehyde.
</KeyIdeas>
