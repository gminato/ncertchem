<Hook>
Vanilla ice cream, the warm note of cinnamon and the sharp smell of nail-polish remover have little in common to the nose. Yet each one owes its character to a molecule carrying the same carbon–oxygen double bond. How can one double bond sit at the heart of such different smells?
</Hook>

## One double bond, many families

Chemists call the carbon–oxygen double bond, $\ce{>C=O}$, the carbonyl group. It is one of the most important groups in organic chemistry. Plants and animals make compounds that carry it, and it gives fruit, spice and vanilla their flavour and fragrance. Solvents, paints, resins, plastics and medicines are also built from these compounds.

If the carbonyl carbon is bonded to one hydrogen and one carbon group, the compound is an **aldehyde**. If it is bonded to two carbon groups, the compound is a **ketone**. Acetone, the simplest ketone, has two methyl groups on the carbonyl carbon. Carboxylic acids and their derivatives also carry a carbonyl group, but they belong to a later part of this Unit.

  </Step>
  <Step>
  The bonding electrons are pulled towards oxygen, so the bond is polarised. The carbon carries a partial positive charge, δ⁺, and the oxygen a partial negative charge, δ⁻. Two resonance forms show this: a neutral form with a C=O double bond, and a dipolar form with C⁺ and O⁻. The real molecule is a blend of the two.
  </Step>
  <Step>

## Common names

The common name of an aldehyde comes from the common name of the carboxylic acid with the same carbon skeleton. Take the acid's name and swap its ending: butyric acid gives butyraldehyde. A chlorine on the carbon two steps from the CHO group then gives β-chlorobutyraldehyde. Greek letters mark where a substituent sits along the chain. The α-carbon is the one directly bonded to the CHO group, the β-carbon is the next, and so on.

Ketones are named by naming the two groups on the carbonyl carbon and adding "ketone". Their substituents are located with α, α′, β, β′ and so on, starting from the carbons next to the carbonyl group. Some ketones keep historical names. The simplest, $\ce{CH3COCH3}$, is called acetone. Alkyl phenyl ketones take the name of the acyl group as a prefix to "phenone", so $\ce{C6H5COCH3}$ is acetophenone.

## IUPAC names

In IUPAC names, an open-chain aldehyde takes the alkane name with the ending -e replaced by -al, and a ketone takes -one. An aldehyde's chain is numbered from the CHO carbon, which is always C1. A ketone's chain is numbered from the end nearer the carbonyl group, so the C=O gets the lowest number possible. Substituents are listed in alphabetical order, each with its number.

Take $\ce{CH3COCH2CH(Br)CH3}$. Numbered from the end nearer the C=O, its carbonyl carbon is C2 and the bromine sits on C4. Its name is 4-bromopentan-2-one. In a cyclic ketone the carbonyl carbon is C1. A ring with a CHO group takes the suffix -carbaldehyde, and its ring numbering starts from the carbon that carries the CHO group.

## The carbonyl group in more detail

The carbonyl carbon is sp²-hybridised. It forms three σ bonds, and its fourth valence electron makes a π bond with oxygen. Everything around the carbonyl carbon is flat: its three neighbours sit in the same plane, and the angles between the bonds come out near 120°, which is what a trigonal arrangement predicts.

Oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, so the C=O bond is polarised. The carbonyl carbon is therefore an electrophilic centre, a Lewis acid, and the carbonyl oxygen is a nucleophilic centre, a Lewis base. Measured dipole moments of these compounds are large, and they exceed those of ethers, which shows the bond is strongly polar. The resonance forms in the Scrolly explain this high polarity.

<KeyIdeas>
- The carbonyl group, >C=O, is planar at its carbon, with bond angles near 120° and a π bond from overlapping p orbitals.
- The C=O bond is polarised: carbon is electrophilic, oxygen is nucleophilic, and resonance explains the high polarity.
- Aldehydes carry a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon. Ketones carry two carbon groups.
- Aldehyde chains are numbered from the CHO carbon. Ketone chains are numbered from the end nearer the C=O.
- Common names use Greek letters, acid-based names and the -phenone pattern. IUPAC names use -al, -one and -carbaldehyde.
</KeyIdeas>
