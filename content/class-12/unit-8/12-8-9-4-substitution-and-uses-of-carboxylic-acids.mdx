<Hook>
On one shelf sit a jar of pickle, its label naming vinegar, and a squash bottle listing sodium benzoate as a preservative. Both come from the same family of acids that also makes the nylon in a rope. Yet the carboxyl group behaves in two different ways. It lets a halogen in on the carbon beside it, but on a benzene ring it steers new groups to one particular position and refuses a Friedel–Crafts reaction. How can one group do both?
</Hook>

## Halogen on the carbon next to the acid

A carboxylic acid carries its carbonyl and hydroxyl groups on one carbon. The carbon next to that carbon is called the α-carbon, and any hydrogen attached to it is an α-hydrogen. Acids that have an α-hydrogen can be halogenated at that position. Add a halogen, chlorine or bromine, along with a pinch of red phosphorus, and the hydrogen next to the carboxyl carbon gives way to a halogen atom. The reaction is named after the three chemists who worked it out, Hell, Volhard and Zelinsky.

As an equation:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH + Br2 ->[red P] CH3CH2CHBrCOOH + HBr}
$$

The α-position is the only place that changes. Every other carbon, and the COOH group, stays exactly where it was.

## Ring substitution with a deactivating group

Aromatic carboxylic acids can undergo electrophilic substitution on the ring, and the carboxyl group controls where the new group lands. It is deactivating, so the ring reacts more slowly than benzene does, and it is meta-directing, so the new group goes to the meta position. Benzoic acid nitrated under the mixture above gives 3-nitrobenzoic acid:

$$
\ce{C6H5COOH + HNO3 ->[H2SO4] C6H4(NO2)COOH + H2O}
$$

The same group also blocks the Friedel–Crafts reaction, for two reasons. The carboxyl group deactivates the ring, and the catalyst, aluminium chloride, is a Lewis acid that binds to the carboxyl group. Together these stop the Friedel–Crafts reaction on the ring.

## Where the acids are used

Each acid in the family has its own job. In industry, methanoic acid serves the rubber and textile trades, dye houses, tanneries and electroplating baths. Ethanoic acid is a common solvent and the acid that gives vinegar its sharpness in food. Hexanedioic acid is one of the two building blocks of nylon-6,6. Esters of benzoic acid add scent to perfumes, and sodium benzoate keeps food from spoiling. Higher fatty acids, long carbon chains with a COOH at one end, are turned into soaps and detergents.

<KeyIdeas>
- Acids with an α-hydrogen are halogenated at the α-carbon by Cl₂ or Br₂ with a little red phosphorus, the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction.
- An acid with no hydrogen on its α-carbon has nothing to swap, so the halogenation does not take place.
- On a benzene ring, COOH slows substitution down and sends the incoming group to the meta spot.
- Aluminium chloride binds to the carboxyl group, so aromatic carboxylic acids do not undergo Friedel–Crafts reactions.
- Uses follow the chain: methanoic acid, ethanoic acid, hexanedioic acid for nylon-6,6, benzoate esters and sodium benzoate, and higher fatty acids for soaps and detergents.
</KeyIdeas>
