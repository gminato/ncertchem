<Hook>
Apples, bananas and pear drops owe their smell to esters, and every ester is built from an acid and an alcohol joined with the loss of water. Yet a carboxylic acid and an alcohol can stand side by side on a bench for a long time without joining. What does a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid add to that mixture?
</Hook>

Chemists change a carboxylic acid mainly in two places. The first is the C–OH bond, where the –OH is replaced by another group. The second is the –COOH group as a whole, which can be reduced to an alcohol or can lose carbon dioxide. The Scrolly takes the C–OH reactions first, then the reactions of the –COOH group.

<Scrolly animation="acid-derivatives">
  <Step>
  Two molecules of ethanoic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$, lose one water molecule between them when heated with a mineral acid or with the drying agent $\ce{P2O5}$. The product is ethanoic anhydride, $\ce{(CH3CO)2O}$. Its two acyl groups are joined through a single oxygen, and the reaction breaks at the C–OH bond of the acid.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  Heat ethanoic acid with methanol and a few drops of concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$, and methyl ethanoate forms with water as the second product. The acid is a catalyst: it starts the reaction and is recovered at the end. Phenols can be joined to an acid in the same way.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  Follow the arrows of the mechanism. The carbonyl oxygen of the acid takes a proton, so the carbonyl carbon becomes more positive. The oxygen of methanol adds to that carbon, leaving a tetrahedral carbon with two –OH groups. A proton then moves so that one –OH becomes –OH₂⁺, which is easier to lose than a plain –OH. It leaves as water, and the protonated ester gives up a proton to become methyl ethanoate.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  Replace the –OH of butanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}$, with chlorine using $\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{PCl3}$ or $\ce{SOCl2}$, and you get butanoyl chloride, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COCl}$. Thionyl chloride is the reagent chemists prefer, because its other two products, $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{HCl}$, are gases that leave the flask. The by-products of $\ce{PCl5}$ and $\ce{PCl3}$ include a phosphorus compound that stays in the mixture.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  Stir propanoic acid with ammonia and a salt forms first, ammonium propanoate, $\ce{CH3CH2COONH4}$. The two stages are separate: the salt appears as soon as the reagents meet, but the amide appears only after the salt is heated at a high temperature. On that heating, water is driven off, leaving propanamide, $\ce{CH3CH2CONH2}$. The nitrogen of the amide comes from the ammonia.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  The –COOH carbon can also be reduced. Lithium aluminium hydride, or diborane, turns pentanoic acid into pentan-1-ol, $\ce{CH3(CH2)3CH2OH}$. Sodium borohydride leaves the acid untouched. Heat the sodium salt of an acid with soda-lime, and the COONa group is removed as sodium carbonate, leaving a hydrocarbon with one carbon fewer.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

The same joining works with phenols in place of alcohols, so an acid can also be linked to the oxygen of a phenol. Build the bench below from any acid you like. <KeyIdeas>
- The C–OH bond of an acid is the site of anhydride, ester and acyl chloride formation: the –OH is replaced, and the acyl group CH₃CO– carries over.
- Esterification of an acid with an alcohol uses a mineral acid as a catalyst. Protonation of the carbonyl oxygen makes the carbonyl carbon ready for attack, and water leaves from the tetrahedral intermediate.
- Among PCl₅, PCl₃ and SOCl₂, thionyl chloride is preferred because its two other products are gases that escape the mixture.
- Heating an acid with ammonia forms an ammonium salt, which gives an amide on stronger heating.
- Carboxylic acids reduce to primary alcohols with LiAlH₄ or B₂H₆, but not with NaBH₄. Soda-lime decarboxylation gives a hydrocarbon with one carbon fewer.
</KeyIdeas>
