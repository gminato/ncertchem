<Hook>
Imagine preparing two crisp bottles of aqueous urea in an air-conditioned $15\ ^\circ\text{C}$ laboratory: one labelled $1.00\text{ M}$ (molar) and the other labelled $1.00\text{ m}$ (molal). On a sweltering $42\ ^\circ\text{C}$ summer afternoon, neither bottle has lost a single drop of water or a single molecule of urea — yet the $1.00\text{ M}$ solution now reads slightly weaker than $1.00\text{ M}$, while the $1.00\text{ m}$ solution is still $1.00\text{ m}$ to the last decimal place. Why does a simple change in room temperature shift one concentration label while leaving the other untouched?
</Hook>

Describing a mixture casually as **dilute** (very little solute) or **concentrated** (abundant solute) gives a rough impression, but it breaks down the moment a pharmacist compounds an intravenous drip or an analytical chemist runs a titration. To reproduce a solution reliably anywhere in the world, chemists express **concentration** quantitatively as an exact ratio comparing the amount of solute to the amount of solution or solvent.

In Class 12 chemistry (NCERT Unit 1, Section 1.2), seven quantitative concentration scales cover everything from trace dissolved gases in the ocean to industrial antifreeze blends and precision thermodynamics.

When a solute is present only in trace amounts — like roughly $6\text{ mg}$ of dissolved $\ce{O2}$ in a litre of seawater — scaling by $10^6$ gives clean **parts per million (ppm)** numbers.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Mole fraction counts particles directly.** Dividing the moles of one component by the total moles of every component produces a pure, dimensionless number $x_i$ between $0$ and $1$. Because every molecule in the beaker belongs to one component or another, the mole fractions of all components always sum to unity: $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_i = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step>

  </Step>
  <Step>

The mass and mole counts inside never change, so **molality**, **mole fraction**, and **mass percentage** remain locked — while **molarity** drops because the same moles now occupy a larger volume.
  </Step>

## The Seven Concentration Scales

### 1. Mass Percentage ($w/w$)

Mass percentage reports how many grams of a component sit inside $100\text{ g}$ of the total solution:

$$
\text{Mass \% of a component} = \frac{\text{Mass of the component in the solution}}{\text{Total mass of the solution}} \times 100
$$

For instance, a $12\%\ (w/w)$ aqueous sucrose solution contains $12\text{ g}$ of sucrose dissolved in $88\text{ g}$ of water, giving $100\text{ g}$ of solution in total. Industrial chemical stocks — such as commercial sodium hypochlorite ($\ce{NaOCl}$) bleach — routinely specify composition by mass percentage.

### 2. Volume Percentage ($V/V$)

When both solute and solvent are liquids, volume percentage is convenient:

$$
\text{Volume \% of a component} = \frac{\text{Volume of the component}}{\text{Total volume of solution}} \times 100
$$

A $35\%\ (V/V)$ radiator antifreeze mixture contains $35\text{ mL}$ of ethylene glycol ($\ce{C2H6O2}$) blended with water to reach a final solution volume of $100\text{ mL}$, depressing water's freezing point well below $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

### 3. Mass-by-Volume Percentage ($w/V$)

Pharmacists and clinicians frequently specify the mass of solute (in grams) contained in $100\text{ mL}$ of final solution:

$$
\text{Mass by volume \%} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute (g)}}{\text{Volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100
$$

Standard physiological saline ($0.9\%\ w/V$ $\ce{NaCl}$) holds $0.9\text{ g}$ of sodium chloride in every $100\text{ mL}$ of sterile aqueous solution.

### 4. Parts per Million ($\text{ppm}$)

When concentrations are extremely small — dissolved gases in natural waters, fluoride ions in drinking water, or airborne pollutants — percentages become clumsy decimals filled with leading zeros. Multiplying the part-to-whole ratio by one million produces compact **parts per million**:

$$
\text{ppm} = \frac{\text{Number of parts of the component}}{\text{Total number of parts of all components}} \times 10^6
$$

Like percentages, $\text{ppm}$ can be expressed on a mass-to-mass, volume-to-volume, or mass-to-volume basis. In seawater, roughly $6 \times 10^{-3}\text{ g}$ of dissolved $\ce{O2}$ per $1030\text{ g}$ (one litre) works out to about $5.8\text{ ppm}$.

### 5. Mole Fraction ($x$)

Mole fraction compares the mole count of a component $i$ to the sum of moles of every component in the solution:

$$
x_i = \frac{n_i}{n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_k} = \frac{n_i}{\sum n_i}
$$

For a binary mixture of solvent $A$ and solute $B$:

$$
x_A = \frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}, \qquad x_B = \frac{n_B}{n_A + n_B}, \qquad x_A + x_B = 1
$$

Because moles cancel in numerator and denominator, mole fraction has **no units**. It is the natural concentration variable for vapour-pressure laws (Raoult's law in later Sections) and gas mixtures.

### 6. Molarity ($M$)

**Molarity** is the number of moles of solute dissolved per litre (or cubic decimetre, $\text{dm}^3$) of **solution**:

$$
M = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}} = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}\ (\text{L})}
$$

Its units are $\text{mol L}^{-1}$ (equivalently $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$, written concisely as $\text{M}$). Molarity shines in volumetric titrations where solutions are dispensed quickly by pipette and burette.

### 7. Molality ($m$)

**Molality** is the number of moles of solute dissolved per kilogram of **pure solvent**:

$$
m = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}} = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{W_{\text{solvent}}\ (\text{kg})}
$$

Its units are $\text{mol kg}^{-1}$ (written concisely as $\text{m}$). Notice the two crucial switches from molarity: **mass** replaces volume, and **solvent alone** replaces total solution.

## Why Temperature Separates the Scales

Every concentration unit falls cleanly into one of two families depending on whether **volume** appears in its definition:

- **Temperature-independent scales:** mass percentage ($w/w$), mass-based $\text{ppm}$, mole fraction ($x$), and molality ($m$). Because mass and particle count do not change when a sealed container is heated or cooled, these values stay exact across any temperature range.
- **Temperature-dependent scales:** volume percentage ($V/V$), mass-by-volume percentage ($w/V$), and molarity ($M$). Heating expands the liquid ($V$ increases while $n_{\text{solute}}$ stays fixed), so molarity decreases slightly as temperature rises.

That solves our opening Hook: whenever experiments span changing temperatures — such as boiling-point elevation or freezing-point depression in later Sections of this Unit — chemists reach for **molality** and **mole fraction** instead of molarity.

<KeyIdeas>
- Quantitative concentration scales replace ambiguous words like "dilute" and "concentrated" with exact ratios of solute to solution or solvent.
- Percentage scales ($w/w$, $V/V$, $w/V$) express parts per hundred, whereas parts per million ($\text{ppm}$) scales trace solutes by $10^6$.
- Mole fraction ($x_i = n_i / \sum n_i$) is dimensionless and always satisfies $x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_i = 1$ across all components.
- Molarity ($M$) measures moles of solute per litre of **solution**, while molality ($m$) measures moles of solute per kilogram of **solvent**.
- Mass percentage ($w/w$), mass-based $\text{ppm}$, mole fraction, and molality are **independent of temperature**; molarity depends on temperature because solution volume changes with heat.
</KeyIdeas>
