<Hook>
When fermented sugarcane wash enters a distillery's fractional distillation column, repeated boiling and condensation steadily enrich ethanol from 12% to 60%, then 85%, and finally about 95% by volume. Then the column hits an immovable wall: adding ten more trays or running it twice as long will not remove the last 5% of water. Why does a separation method built on boiling-point differences suddenly stall before reaching pure ethanol?
</Hook>

Liquid–liquid solutions are sorted into **ideal** and **non-ideal** solutions by one decisive test: whether their vapour pressure follows Raoult's law across the entire composition range from $x_2 = 0$ to $x_2 = 1$. The answer comes straight from the tug-of-war between intermolecular attractions in the liquid.

### Ideal Solutions

An **ideal solution** obeys Raoult's law at every composition:

$$
p_1 = x_1 p_1^0, \qquad p_2 = x_2 p_2^0, \qquad p_{\text{total}} = x_1 p_1^0 + x_2 p_2^0
$$

Because every partial pressure grows linearly with mole fraction, the vapour-pressure graph consists of straight lines. Ideal mixing also satisfies two thermodynamic signatures:

$$
\Delta_{\text{mix}}H = 0, \qquad \Delta_{\text{mix}}V = 0
$$

Mixing the two pure liquids neither absorbs nor releases heat, and pouring $50\text{ mL}$ of liquid A into $50\text{ mL}$ of liquid B yields exactly $100\text{ mL}$ of solution.

At the molecular scale, pure liquid A contains only A–A attractions and pure liquid B contains only B–B attractions. Once mixed, A–B neighbours appear alongside them. Ideal behaviour emerges whenever **A–B attractions are nearly identical in strength to A–A and B–B attractions**. A molecule leaving the surface feels the same downward pull whether its neighbours are identical or different, so its escaping tendency matches the pure liquid.

Strictly ideal mixtures are rare, yet structurally similar pairs come remarkably close:
- **n-hexane + n-heptane** (both non-polar alkanes held by comparable dispersion forces)
- **bromoethane + chloroethane** (similar polar haloalkanes)
- **benzene + toluene** (closely matched aromatic rings)

### Non-ideal Solutions

Whenever A–B attractions differ noticeably from A–A and B–B attractions, the solution departs from Raoult's law and becomes a **non-ideal solution**. Two opposite regimes arise:

- **Positive deviation from Raoult's law ($p_{\text{total}} > p_{\text{total,ideal}}$):**
  Here **A–B attractions are weaker** than A–A and B–B attractions. Because unlike neighbours hold each other less tightly, molecules of both species escape into the vapour more readily than Raoult's law predicts, bowing all vapour-pressure curves **upward**. Breaking stronger like–like attractions absorbs energy and loosens packing, giving $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H > 0$ (endothermic) and $\Delta_{\text{mix}}V > 0$ (slight expansion).
  - *Ethanol + acetone:* Pure ethanol forms an extensive network of $\ce{O-H}\cdots\ce{O}$ hydrogen bonds. When acetone is added, its molecules slip between ethanol chains and disrupt some of those hydrogen bonds, weakening the average attraction.
  - *Carbon disulphide + acetone:* Non-polar $\ce{CS2}$ dilutes acetone's dipole–dipole attractions, producing weaker A–B forces.

- **Negative deviation from Raoult's law ($p_{\text{total}} < p_{\text{total,ideal}}$):**
  Here **A–B attractions are stronger** than A–A and B–B attractions. Unlike neighbours cling more tightly in the liquid, suppressing evaporation and bowing the vapour-pressure curves **downward**. Forming stronger A–B contacts releases energy and pulls molecules slightly closer together, giving $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H < 0$ (exothermic) and $\Delta_{\text{mix}}V < 0$ (slight contraction).
  - *Chloroform + acetone:* Three electronegative chlorine atoms polarise the $\ce{C-H}$ bond in chloroform, allowing it to form a fresh hydrogen bond with acetone's carbonyl oxygen: $\ce{Cl3C-H}\cdots\ce{O=C(CH3)2}$. Neither pure liquid had this bond.
  - *Phenol + aniline:* The phenolic $\ce{O-H}$ proton forms an unusually strong hydrogen bond with the lone pair on aniline's nitrogen atom, outcompeting the self-association in either pure component.

### Azeotropes: Constant-Boiling Mixtures

Moderate deviations bend the vapour-pressure curve while keeping $p_{\text{total}}$ monotonic between $p_1^0$ and $p_2^0$. **Large** deviations, however, push $p_{\text{total}}$ past a true peak or valley. At that extremum, the vapour rising off the boiling liquid has the **exact same composition as the liquid itself** ($y_1 = x_1$, $y_2 = x_2$). Such a mixture is an **azeotrope**: it boils at a single constant temperature like a pure substance and cannot be separated further by fractional distillation.

1. **Minimum-boiling azeotropes** arise from **large positive deviations**. Because the vapour pressure peaks above both pure liquids, the mixture reaches atmospheric pressure at a boiling point *lower* than either pure component. Fermented **ethanol + water** hits this peak at roughly **95% ethanol by volume**—explaining why distillery columns stop at 95%.
2. **Maximum-boiling azeotropes** arise from **large negative deviations**. Because the vapour pressure dips below both pure liquids, extra heating is needed to reach atmospheric pressure, giving a boiling point *higher* than either pure component. **Nitric acid + water** forms a classic maximum-boiling azeotrope at approximately **68% $\ce{HNO3}$ and 32% water by mass**, boiling steadily at $393.5\text{ K}$.

<KeyIdeas>
- **Ideal solutions** obey Raoult's law across all compositions with $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H = 0$ and $\Delta_{\text{mix}}V = 0$ because A–B attractions nearly match A–A and B–B attractions (e.g., n-hexane + n-heptane, benzene + toluene).
- **Positive deviation** occurs when A–B attractions are weaker than A–A and B–B, increasing escaping tendency so vapour pressure bows above Raoult's line with $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H > 0$ and $\Delta_{\text{mix}}V > 0$ (e.g., ethanol + acetone, $\ce{CS2}$ + acetone).
- **Negative deviation** occurs when A–B attractions are stronger than A–A and B–B, suppressing escaping tendency so vapour pressure bows below Raoult's line with $\Delta_{\text{mix}}H < 0$ and $\Delta_{\text{mix}}V < 0$ (e.g., chloroform + acetone, phenol + aniline).
- **Azeotropes** are constant-boiling binary mixtures whose vapour and liquid phases have identical composition ($y_i = x_i$), making separation by fractional distillation impossible.
- **Large positive deviations** form **minimum-boiling azeotropes** (e.g., ~95% ethanol + 5% water by volume), while **large negative deviations** form **maximum-boiling azeotropes** (e.g., ~68% $\ce{HNO3}$ + 32% water by mass, boiling at $393.5\text{ K}$).
</KeyIdeas>
