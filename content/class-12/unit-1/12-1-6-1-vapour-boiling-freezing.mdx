## What makes a property colligative?

Whenever a **non-volatile solute** is dissolved in a volatile solvent, fewer solvent molecules occupy the liquid surface, so the solvent's equilibrium vapour pressure falls. Four macroscopic properties of dilute solutions stem directly from this drop:

1. Relative lowering of vapour pressure of the solvent
2. Elevation of boiling point of the solvent
3. Depression of freezing point of the solvent
4. Osmotic pressure of the solution (explored in the next Section)

All four share a remarkable trait: in ideal dilute solutions, their magnitude depends **only on the number of solute particles** present relative to the solvent — never on the chemical identity, shape or colour of those particles. Such particle-counting properties are called **colligative properties** (from the Latin *co-* "together" and *ligare* "to bind", reflecting how they are tied together by a single physical cause).

## 1. Relative lowering of vapour pressure

For a binary solution of a volatile solvent (component $1$) and a non-volatile solute (component $2$), Raoult's law gives the vapour pressure of the solution purely from the solvent:

$$
p_1 = x_1 p_1^0
$$

The drop in vapour pressure is therefore $\Delta p_1 = p_1^0 - p_1 = p_1^0(1 - x_1)$. Since $x_1 + x_2 = 1$, dividing both sides by the pure-solvent vapour pressure $p_1^0$ yields the **relative lowering of vapour pressure**:

$$
\frac{\Delta p_1}{p_1^0} = \frac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} = x_2 = \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2}
$$

Notice that the relative drop equals the solute's mole fraction $x_2$ directly. If multiple non-volatile solutes are dissolved together, the relative lowering equals the sum of all solute mole fractions.

In a **dilute solution**, $n_2 \ll n_1$, so neglecting $n_2$ in the denominator simplifies the expression to:

$$
\frac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} \approx \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{w_2 \, M_1}{M_2 \, w_1}
$$

Here $w_1$ and $w_2$ are the masses (in $\text{g}$) and $M_1$ and $M_2$ are the molar masses (in $\text{g mol}^{-1}$) of solvent and solute. Rearranging this equation lets a chemist measure an unknown solute's molar mass $M_2$ straight from a vapour-pressure reading:

$$
M_2 = \frac{w_2 \, M_1}{w_1} \cdot \frac{p_1^0}{p_1^0 - p_1}
$$

## 2. One curve shift, two thermal consequences

Once you see that a non-volatile solute shifts the solvent's **entire** vapour-pressure curve downward across all temperatures, both boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression follow immediately from a single graph. Walk through the five Steps below to watch both temperature shifts emerge together.

<Scrolly animation="colligative-phase-shift">
  <Step>
    **Start with the pure solvent.** Its liquid vapour-pressure curve rises steeply with temperature. The pure liquid freezes at $T_f^0$, where its vapour pressure matches that of the pure solid solvent, and boils at $T_b^0$, where its vapour pressure reaches atmospheric pressure ($1.013\ \text{bar}$).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Dissolve a non-volatile solute.** Solvent molecules escape less readily at every temperature, so the entire liquid vapour-pressure curve drops downward by a fractional amount $\Delta p_1 / p_1^0 = x_2$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Boiling point shifts upward.** At the old boiling point $T_b^0$, the solution's vapour pressure now sits below $1.013\ \text{bar}$. You must heat the solution further to reach $1.013\ \text{bar}$ at a higher temperature $T_b = T_b^0 + \Delta T_b$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Freezing point shifts downward.** Because the solid solvent's vapour-pressure curve is steeper than the liquid curve (sublimation demands more enthalpy than vaporisation), the lowered solution curve intersects the solid curve further to the left at a lower freezing temperature $T_f = T_f^0 - \Delta T_f$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **The liquid window widens on both ends.** One dissolved solute expands the liquid span $[T_f, T_b]$ in both directions at once — explaining radiator antifreeze, road de-icing salts and why measuring $\Delta T_b$ or $\Delta T_f$ reveals an unknown solute's molar mass $M_2$.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## 3. Elevation of boiling point ($\Delta T_b$)

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding pressure ($1.013\ \text{bar}$ at normal atmospheric pressure). Because a non-volatile solute lowers vapour pressure, the solution requires a higher temperature $T_b$ to boil than the pure solvent ($T_b^0$). The **elevation of boiling point**, $\Delta T_b = T_b - T_b^0$, is directly proportional to the solution's **molality** $m$ (moles of solute per kilogram of solvent):

$$
\Delta T_b = K_b \, m
$$

The proportionality constant $K_b$ is the **ebullioscopic constant** (or molal boiling-point elevation constant), with units $\text{K kg mol}^{-1}$. It depends solely on the solvent, not on the solute. Writing molality in terms of masses $w_2$ (solute, $\text{g}$) and $w_1$ (solvent, $\text{g}$), $m = (1000 \, w_2) / (M_2 \, w_1)$, gives a direct formula for molar mass determination:

$$
\Delta T_b = \frac{1000 \, K_b \, w_2}{M_2 \, w_1} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad M_2 = \frac{1000 \, K_b \, w_2}{\Delta T_b \, w_1}
$$

## 4. Depression of freezing point ($\Delta T_f$)

Freezing occurs at the temperature where the liquid phase and solid phase stand in dynamic equilibrium — meaning both phases share the **same vapour pressure**. When a non-volatile solute lowers the liquid curve (assuming pure solid solvent crystallises out), equilibrium with the solid is restored only at a lower temperature $T_f$. The **depression of freezing point**, $\Delta T_f = T_f^0 - T_f$, also scales linearly with molality in dilute solutions:

$$
\Delta T_f = K_f \, m
$$

Here $K_f$ is the **cryoscopic constant** (molal freezing-point depression constant), in $\text{K kg mol}^{-1}$. Substituting $m = (1000 \, w_2) / (M_2 \, w_1)$ yields the cryoscopic molar-mass equation:

$$
\Delta T_f = \frac{1000 \, K_f \, w_2}{M_2 \, w_1} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad M_2 = \frac{1000 \, K_f \, w_2}{\Delta T_f \, w_1}
$$

## 5. Why $K_b$ and $K_f$ belong strictly to the solvent

Thermodynamics links both constants directly to the pure solvent's molar mass $M_1$ (in $\text{g mol}^{-1}$), normal transition temperatures ($T_f^0$, $T_b^0$ in $\text{K}$) and molar transition enthalpies ($\Delta_{\text{fus}} H$, $\Delta_{\text{vap}} H$ in $\text{J mol}^{-1}$), with $R = 8.314\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}$:

$$
K_f = \frac{R \, M_1 \, (T_f^0)^2}{1000 \, \Delta_{\text{fus}} H} \qquad\text{and}\qquad K_b = \frac{R \, M_1 \, (T_b^0)^2}{1000 \, \Delta_{\text{vap}} H}
$$

Because melting a crystal requires much less energy than vaporising a liquid ($\Delta_{\text{fus}} H \ll \Delta_{\text{vap}} H$), $K_f$ is consistently larger than $K_b$ for the same solvent — which is why freezing-point depression is typically more sensitive than boiling-point elevation for measuring molar masses.

| Solvent | Normal b.p. $T_b^0$ (K) | $K_b$ ($\text{K kg mol}^{-1}$) | Normal f.p. $T_f^0$ (K) | $K_f$ ($\text{K kg mol}^{-1}$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Water ($\ce{H2O}$) | 373.15 | 0.52 | 273.0 | 1.86 |
| Ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$) | 351.5 | 1.20 | 155.7 | 1.99 |
| Benzene ($\ce{C6H6}$) | 353.3 | 2.53 | 278.6 | 5.12 |
| Cyclohexane ($\ce{C6H12}$) | 353.74 | 2.79 | 279.55 | 20.00 |
| Chloroform ($\ce{CHCl3}$) | 334.4 | 3.63 | 209.6 | 4.79 |
| Carbon tetrachloride ($\ce{CCl4}$) | 350.0 | 5.03 | 250.5 | 31.8 |
| Acetic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$) | 391.1 | 2.93 | 290.0 | 3.90 |

<KeyIdeas>
- **Colligative properties** count dissolved solute particles rather than identifying them: in dilute non-electrolyte solutions, equal molalities of different solutes in the same solvent produce identical $\Delta p_1 / p_1^0$, $\Delta T_b$ and $\Delta T_f$.
- **Relative vapour-pressure lowering** equals the solute mole fraction: $(p_1^0 - p_1)/p_1^0 = x_2$, simplifying in dilute solutions to $(w_2 M_1)/(M_2 w_1)$.
- **One vapour-pressure drop widens the liquid range on both ends**: the lowered liquid curve reaches $1.013\ \text{bar}$ at a higher boiling point ($\Delta T_b = K_b m$) and meets the steeper solid curve at a lower freezing point ($\Delta T_f = K_f m$).
- **Solvent constants $K_b$ and $K_f$** depend only on the pure solvent's molar mass, transition temperature and transition enthalpy ($K_f = R M_1 (T_f^0)^2 / 1000 \Delta_{\text{fus}} H$ and $K_b = R M_1 (T_b^0)^2 / 1000 \Delta_{\text{vap}} H$), explaining why $K_f$ is generally larger than $K_b$.
- **Molar mass determination**: measuring any of the three shifts in a known mass of solvent yields $M_2$ directly for non-volatile, non-electrolyte solutes.
</KeyIdeas>
