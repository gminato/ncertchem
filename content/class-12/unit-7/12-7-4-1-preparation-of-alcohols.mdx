<Hook>
Start from propene, a simple three-carbon alkene, and ask for a three-carbon alcohol. One sequence of reagents places the hydroxyl group squarely on the middle carbon to give propan-2-ol. Another places it on the end carbon to give propan-1-ol in high yield. Both reactions add the elements of water across the same double bond, so why do the atoms end up on opposite carbons?
</Hook>

## Hydration of alkenes: two paths to opposite carbons

Alkenes are rich in $\pi$ electrons, so water alone does not react with a double bond rapidly enough to be useful. Chemists bridge that gap in two complementary ways: **acid-catalysed hydration**, which proceeds through a carbocation intermediate, and **hydroboration-oxidation**, which attaches boron first and replaces it with oxygen later. Because their mechanisms differ, their regioselectivities point in opposite directions.

### Acid-catalysed hydration (Markovnikov route)

In the presence of a dilute mineral acid such as $\ce{H2SO4}$ (supplying $\ce{H+}$), water adds reversibly across a carbon-carbon double bond:

$$
\ce{>C=C< + H2O <=>[H+] >CH-C(OH)<}
$$

When the alkene is unsymmetrical, addition obeys **Markovnikov's rule**: hydrogen attaches to the $sp^2$ carbon that already bears more hydrogen atoms, and the $-\ce{OH}$ group ends up on the more substituted carbon. Thus propene yields propan-2-ol:

$$
\ce{CH3CH=CH2 + H2O <=>[H+] CH3-CH(OH)-CH3}
$$

This regioselectivity arises directly from the three-step mechanism. First, the acid protonates water to generate the hydronium ion ($\ce{H2O + H+ -> H3O+}$). Next, the $\pi$ bond attacks $\ce{H3O+}$ (Step 1), forming the more stable carbocation: for propene, protonation at $\ce{C-1}$ yields a secondary carbocation rather than a much less stable primary one. In Step 2, a water molecule acts as a nucleophile and attacks the positively charged carbon to form a protonated alcohol (an oxonium ion). In Step 3, a second water molecule removes a proton from that oxonium ion, releasing the neutral alcohol and regenerating the $\ce{H3O+}$ catalyst.

### Hydroboration-oxidation (anti-Markovnikov route)

In 1959, H. C. Brown reported an indirect hydration sequence using diborane, $\ce{(BH3)2}$ (work for which Brown shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with G. Wittig). Diborane adds across three equivalents of an alkene to produce a **trialkylborane**:

$$
\ce{3 CH3-CH=CH2 + 1/2 (BH3)2 -> (CH3-CH2-CH2)3B}
$$

During each $\ce{B-H}$ addition across the double bond, the boron atom attaches to the $sp^2$ carbon carrying the greater number of hydrogen atoms, while hydrogen adds to the more substituted carbon. The resulting trialkylborane is then oxidised without isolation using hydrogen peroxide ($\ce{H2O2}$) in aqueous sodium hydroxide ($\ce{NaOH}$):

$$
\ce{(CH3-CH2-CH2)3B + 3 H2O2 ->[OH- , H2O] 3 CH3-CH2-CH2-OH + B(OH)3}
$$

Oxidation replaces every $\ce{C-B}$ bond with a $\ce{C-OH}$ bond and leaves boric acid, $\ce{B(OH)3}$, in solution. The overall transformation looks like adding water opposite to Markovnikov's rule (**anti-Markovnikov** hydration), giving primary alcohols such as propan-1-ol in excellent yields.

<Scrolly animation="alkene-hydration-mechanisms">
  <Step>
    **Acid hydration, Step 1 (protonation):** Propene meets $\ce{H3O+}$ in acidic water. The $\pi$ bond reaches out to a proton on $\ce{H3O+}$, forming a $\ce{C-H}$ bond at the terminal $\ce{CH2}$ carbon and placing the positive charge on the internal carbon as a secondary carbocation.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Acid hydration, Step 2 (nucleophilic attack):** A lone pair on oxygen in a neutral $\ce{H2O}$ molecule attacks the planar carbocation carbon. A new $\ce{C-O}$ bond forms, producing a positively charged oxonium intermediate.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Acid hydration, Step 3 (deprotonation):** Another $\ce{H2O}$ molecule abstracts one proton from the oxonium oxygen. The $\ce{O-H}$ bonding pair stays on oxygen, yielding propan-2-ol (Markovnikov product) and regenerating $\ce{H3O+}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Hydroboration (trialkylborane formation):** Switching to diborane, $\ce{(BH3)2}$, reverses the placement of oxygen. Each $\ce{B-H}$ unit adds across a propene double bond with boron attaching to the less substituted terminal $sp^2$ carbon, building up propylborane, dipropylborane and finally tripropylborane, $\ce{(CH3CH2CH2)3B}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Oxidation (boron to hydroxyl):** Aqueous $\ce{H2O2}$ in $\ce{NaOH}$ cleaves all three $\ce{C-B}$ bonds and installs $-\ce{OH}$ at the terminal carbons. The products are three molecules of propan-1-ol (anti-Markovnikov outcome) and boric acid, $\ce{B(OH)3}$.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Reduction of carbonyl compounds and carboxylic acids

When a molecule already carries a carbon-oxygen double bond ($\ce{C=O}$), reducing it adds hydrogen across the carbonyl group without changing the carbon skeleton. Different carbonyl functional groups, however, require reducing agents of different strengths.

### Aldehydes and ketones

Aldehydes ($\ce{RCHO}$) and ketones ($\ce{RCOR'}$) are converted to **primary** and **secondary** alcohols, respectively, by two main methods:

1. **Catalytic hydrogenation:** Adding $\ce{H2}$ gas in the presence of a finely divided metal catalyst such as platinum ($\ce{Pt}$), palladium ($\ce{Pd}$), or nickel ($\ce{Ni}$):

$$
\ce{RCHO + H2 ->[Pd] RCH2OH}
$$

2. **Complex metal hydrides:** Treating the carbonyl compound with **sodium borohydride** ($\ce{NaBH4}$) or **lithium aluminium hydride** ($\ce{LiAlH4}$). For example, reducing a ketone gives a secondary alcohol:

$$
\ce{RCOR' ->[NaBH4] R-CH(OH)-R'}
$$

### Carboxylic acids and esters

Carboxylic acids ($\ce{RCOOH}$) are much harder to reduce than aldehydes or ketones. Sodium borohydride is too mild for them, so laboratory preparations use **$\ce{LiAlH4}$**, a much stronger hydride donor, followed by careful hydrolysis with water:

$$
\ce{RCOOH ->[(i) LiAlH4][(ii) H2O] RCH2OH}
$$

Notice the numbered sequence on the arrow: `(ii) H2O` is added only after the reaction with `(i) LiAlH4` has finished, because $\ce{LiAlH4}$ reacts violently with water.

Although $\ce{LiAlH4}$ gives primary alcohols in excellent yields, it is expensive and reserved for specialised laboratory syntheses. Commercially, carboxylic acids are first converted to **esters** by heating with an alcohol in acid ($\ce{H+}$), and the ester is then reduced with $\ce{H2}$ over a hydrogenation catalyst:

$$
\ce{RCOOH ->[R'OH][H+] RCOOR' ->[H2][Catalyst] RCH2OH + R'OH}
$$

The alcohol $\ce{R'OH}$ used to form the ester is recovered at the end, making the industrial sequence economical.

## Building carbon-carbon bonds with Grignard reagents

Hydrogenation and hydride reduction keep the carbon count fixed. To lengthen or branch the carbon chain at the same time as forming an alcohol, chemists turn to **Grignard reagents** ($\ce{RMgX}$, prepared from haloalkanes and magnesium in dry ether). Because magnesium is far more electropositive than carbon, the $\ce{C-Mg}$ bond is polarised as $\ce{^{\delta-}R-Mg^{\delta+}-X}$, turning the alkyl group into a carbon nucleophile.

The reaction proceeds in two clean steps:

1. **Nucleophilic addition:** The $\ce{^{\delta-}R}$ group attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon while the carbonyl $\pi$ electrons shift onto oxygen, forming a magnesium alkoxide **adduct**:

$$
\ce{>C=O + R-MgX -> [>C(R)-O- Mg+ -X]}
$$

2. **Hydrolysis:** Adding water breaks the oxygen-magnesium association, yielding the alcohol and $\ce{Mg(OH)X}$:

$$
\ce{[>C(R)-O- Mg+ -X] + H2O -> >C(R)-OH + Mg(OH)X}
$$

Choosing the carbonyl partner gives direct control over the degree of the resulting alcohol:

- **Methanal ($\ce{HCHO}$)** has two hydrogens on the carbonyl carbon, so adding one $\ce{R}$ group produces a **primary ($1^\circ$) alcohol** ($\ce{RCH2OH}$).
- **Any other aldehyde ($\ce{RCHO}$)** already carries one alkyl group, so adding $\ce{R'MgX}$ yields a **secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohol** ($\ce{RCH(OH)R'}$).
- **A ketone ($\ce{RCOR}$)** already carries two carbon groups, so adding $\ce{R'MgX}$ produces a **tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohol** ($\ce{R2C(OH)R'}$).

<Scrolly animation="carbonyl-reduction-and-grignard">
  <Step>
    **Aldehydes and ketones with hydrides or $\ce{H2}$:** Adding $\ce{H2}$ over $\ce{Pt}$, $\ce{Pd}$ or $\ce{Ni}$, or using $\ce{NaBH4}$ or $\ce{LiAlH4}$, adds $\ce{H}$ across the $\ce{C=O}$ bond. An aldehyde yields a primary alcohol ($\ce{RCH2OH}$), and a ketone yields a secondary alcohol ($\ce{RCH(OH)R'}$) with the same carbon skeleton.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Carboxylic acids (lab vs commercial):** In the lab, $\ce{RCOOH}$ requires strong $\ce{LiAlH4}$ in step (i) followed by $\ce{H2O}$ in step (ii) to reach $\ce{RCH2OH}$. Commercially, acid-catalysed esterification ($\ce{R'OH / H+}$) converts $\ce{RCOOH}$ into $\ce{RCOOR'}$, which then undergoes catalytic hydrogenation ($\ce{H2}$ / catalyst) to give $\ce{RCH2OH}$ and regenerate $\ce{R'OH}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Grignard addition, Step (i) (adduct formation):** A curved arrow runs from the polarised $\ce{^{\delta-}R-Mg^{\delta+}X}$ bond to the carbonyl carbon, pushing $\ce{C=O}$ $\pi$ electrons onto oxygen to build a new $\ce{C-C}$ bond inside the magnesium alkoxide adduct.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Grignard hydrolysis, Step (ii) ($1^\circ$, $2^\circ$ or $3^\circ$ alcohol):** Adding $\ce{H2O}$ protonates the alkoxide and releases $\ce{Mg(OH)X}$. Methanal gives a $1^\circ$ alcohol, higher aldehydes give $2^\circ$ alcohols, and ketones give $3^\circ$ alcohols.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- Acid-catalysed hydration of an unsymmetrical alkene ($\ce{H2O / H+}$) proceeds through a carbocation intermediate in three steps (protonation, nucleophilic attack of $\ce{H2O}$, deprotonation) and follows Markovnikov's rule.
- Hydroboration-oxidation ($\ce{(BH3)2}$ followed by $\ce{H2O2 / OH-}$) adds boron to the less substituted $sp^2$ carbon and oxidises the trialkylborane to an anti-Markovnikov alcohol plus $\ce{B(OH)3}$.
- Aldehydes reduce to $1^\circ$ alcohols and ketones reduce to $2^\circ$ alcohols with $\ce{H2}$ ($\ce{Pt}$, $\ce{Pd}$, or $\ce{Ni}$), $\ce{NaBH4}$, or $\ce{LiAlH4}$.
- Carboxylic acids require $\ce{LiAlH4}$ followed by $\ce{H2O}$ in the laboratory, whereas commercially they are converted to esters ($\ce{R'OH / H+}$) and hydrogenated ($\ce{H2}$ / catalyst) to give $1^\circ$ alcohols.
- Grignard reagents ($\ce{RMgX}$) add nucleophilically to carbonyl compounds to form an alkoxide adduct that hydrolyses with $\ce{H2O}$: methanal yields $1^\circ$ alcohols, higher aldehydes yield $2^\circ$ alcohols, and ketones yield $3^\circ$ alcohols.
</KeyIdeas>
