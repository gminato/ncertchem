<Hook>
A clinic antiseptic on a shelf, a warm floral note in a perfume, and the crisp pages of your notebook all depend on oxygen atoms tucked into carbon frameworks. In wood-polishing spirit (ethanol) and plant cellulose (the polymer behind cotton and paper), oxygen sits inside hydroxyl groups on aliphatic chains or rings. Move that hydroxyl group directly onto an aromatic ring and you enter the phenolic antiseptics; replace its hydrogen with a second carbon group and you step into fragrant ethers. How do chemists sort these three families before predicting their reactions?
</Hook>

## Three families built from one substitution idea

Replacing one or more hydrogen atoms of a hydrocarbon with an oxygen-bearing group creates compounds with completely new properties. Across everyday materials and industrial chemistry, alcohols serve as precursors to detergents, phenols supply antiseptics, and ethers provide fragrances and solvents.

Look closely at where the oxygen sits relative to the carbon framework:

- **Alcohols** carry one or more hydroxyl ($\ce{-OH}$) groups bonded directly to carbon atom(s) of an aliphatic chain or non-aromatic ring, as in methanol ($\ce{CH3OH}$) or ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$).
- **Phenols** carry one or more $\ce{-OH}$ groups bonded directly to carbon atom(s) of an aromatic ring, as in phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$).
- **Ethers** arise when a hydrocarbon hydrogen is replaced by an alkoxy ($\ce{R-O-}$) or aryloxy ($\ce{Ar-O-}$) group, as in dimethyl ether ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$). Equivalently, you can picture an ether as an alcohol or phenol whose hydroxyl hydrogen has been replaced by an alkyl ($\ce{R}$) or aryl ($\ce{Ar}$) group.

Sorting each compound into the right structural class makes its physical behaviour and chemical reactivity systematic rather than a list of isolated facts.

## Classifying alcohols by hydroxyl count and carbon hybridisation

Two questions classify every alcohol cleanly: first, **how many $\ce{-OH}$ groups** does the molecule carry, and second, **what hybridisation and neighbours** does the $\ce{-OH}$-bearing carbon have?

### Count the hydroxyl groups: mono, di, tri, and polyhydric

Both alcohols and phenols are sorted by the number of $\ce{-OH}$ groups present in one molecule:

- **Monohydric** compounds contain one $\ce{-OH}$ group, such as ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$).
- **Dihydric** compounds contain two $\ce{-OH}$ groups, such as ethane-1,2-diol ($\ce{HOCH2-CH2OH}$, commonly called ethylene glycol).
- **Trihydric** compounds contain three $\ce{-OH}$ groups, such as propane-1,2,3-triol ($\ce{CH2(OH)-CH(OH)-CH2OH}$, commonly called glycerol).
- **Polyhydric** compounds contain four or more $\ce{-OH}$ groups, as found in sugars and starch.

### Check the carbon carrying -OH: $sp^3$ versus $sp^2$

Monohydric alcohols split into two structural tracks according to the hybridisation of the carbon atom directly bonded to the $\ce{-OH}$ oxygen:

1. **Compounds with a $\ce{C_{sp^3}-OH}$ bond.** Here the $\ce{-OH}$ group is attached to a tetrahedral $sp^3$-hybridised carbon atom. Three important subclasses sit inside this track:
   - **Primary ($1^\circ$), secondary ($2^\circ$), and tertiary ($3^\circ$) alkyl alcohols:** Count how many other carbon atoms are directly bonded to the carbon carrying $\ce{-OH}$. In a primary ($1^\circ$) alcohol ($\ce{-CH2-OH}$ and methanol, $\ce{CH3OH}$), that carbon bonds to at most one other carbon. In a secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohol ($\ce{>CH-OH}$), it bonds to two carbons; in a tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohol ($\ce{>C-OH}$), it bonds to three carbons.
   - **Allylic alcohols:** The $\ce{-OH}$ group sits on an $sp^3$ carbon **immediately adjacent** to a carbon-carbon double bond ($\ce{C=C}$), called the allylic carbon. Depending on how many alkyl carbons are also attached to that $sp^3$ carbon, allylic alcohols can be primary ($\ce{CH2=CH-CH2-OH}$), secondary ($\ce{CH2=CH-CH(OH)-R}$), or tertiary ($\ce{CH2=CH-C(OH)(R)R'}$).
   - **Benzylic alcohols:** The $\ce{-OH}$ group sits on an $sp^3$ carbon **immediately adjacent** to an aromatic (benzene) ring. Just like allylic alcohols, benzylic alcohols occur as primary ($\ce{C6H5-CH2-OH}$), secondary ($\ce{C6H5-CH(OH)-R}$), and tertiary ($\ce{C6H5-C(OH)(R)R'}$) structures. Watch the spacing rule carefully: if even one $\ce{-CH2-}$ spacer sits between the benzene ring and the $\ce{C-OH}$ carbon (as in $\ce{C6H5-CH2-CH(OH)-CH3}$), the $\ce{-OH}$ carbon is no longer next to the ring, so the molecule is an ordinary secondary aliphatic alcohol rather than a benzylic alcohol.
2. **Compounds with a $\ce{C_{sp^2}-OH}$ bond.** Here the $\ce{-OH}$ group bonds directly to a trigonal planar $sp^2$-hybridised carbon of a $\ce{C=C}$ double bond or an aromatic ring. When attached to an acyclic or non-aromatic $\ce{C=C}$ carbon, the compound is a **vinylic alcohol**, such as ethenol ($\ce{CH2=CH-OH}$). When attached directly to an aromatic ring carbon, it forms the separate family of phenols.

  </Step>

One direct carbon neighbour gives a primary ($1^\circ$) alcohol ($\ce{-CH2-OH}$), two give a secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohol ($\ce{>CH-OH}$), and three give a tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohol ($\ce{>C-OH}$).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Slide a C=C double bond next door: allylic alcohols.** A $\ce{C=C}$ bond lights up right beside the $sp^3$ $\ce{C-OH}$ carbon. Because the hydroxyl carbon itself still carries four single bonds, its hybridisation stays $sp^3$. Adding alkyl carbons onto that same $\ce{C-OH}$ carbon steps cleanly through primary ($\ce{CH2=CH-CH2-OH}$), secondary, and tertiary allylic alcohols.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Swap the double bond for a benzene ring: benzylic alcohols.** With an aromatic ring directly attached to the $sp^3$ $\ce{C-OH}$ carbon, you get primary ($\ce{C6H5-CH2-OH}$), secondary, and tertiary benzylic alcohols. When an extra $\ce{-CH2-}$ spacer slips between the ring and $\ce{C-OH}$, the direct ring-to-carbon link breaks, shifting the classification back to a plain aliphatic alcohol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Move -OH onto the $sp^2$ carbon itself: vinylic alcohol.** Removing the spacer carbon brings oxygen directly onto one of the double-bonded carbons in $\ce{CH2=CH-OH}$. That trigonal planar carbon is $sp^2$ hybridised, placing vinylic alcohols in the $\ce{C_{sp^2}-OH}$ branch.
  </Step>

## Phenols and ethers: ring hydroxyls and oxygen bridges

### Phenols: counting -OH groups on the aromatic ring

Whenever an $\ce{-OH}$ group bonds directly to an $sp^2$ carbon of a benzene ring, the compound is a **phenol**, not an alcohol. Phenols are classified by how many $\ce{-OH}$ groups sit directly on the ring:

- **Monohydric phenols** bear one ring $\ce{-OH}$ group. Phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$) is the parent member, and 2-methylphenol ($\ce{o-CH3C6H4OH}$, $o$-cresol) is also monohydric because its second ring substituent is a methyl group rather than a hydroxyl group.
- **Dihydric phenols** bear two ring $\ce{-OH}$ groups, such as benzene-1,2-diol (catechol).
- **Trihydric phenols** bear three ring $\ce{-OH}$ groups, such as benzene-1,2,3-triol.

### Ethers: symmetrical versus unsymmetrical bridges

In an ether, a divalent oxygen atom links two carbon groups ($\ce{R-O-R'}$, $\ce{Ar-O-R}$, or $\ce{Ar-O-Ar'}$). Comparing the two groups on either side of oxygen produces two classes:

- **Simple (symmetrical) ethers** have two identical alkyl or aryl groups attached to oxygen, as in diethyl ether ($\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$) or dimethyl ether ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$).
- **Mixed (unsymmetrical) ethers** have two different groups attached to oxygen, as in ethyl methyl ether ($\ce{C2H5OCH3}$) or ethyl phenyl ether ($\ce{C2H5OC6H5}$).

<Scrolly animation="phenol-ether-classes">
  <Step>
    **Attach -OH directly to the aromatic ring: monohydric phenols.** In phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$) and 2-methylphenol ($\ce{o-CH3C6H4OH}$), the oxygen bonds straight to an $sp^2$ carbon of the benzene ring. Even with a methyl substituent beside it, only one $\ce{-OH}$ sits on the ring, keeping both compounds monohydric phenols.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Place two or three -OH groups on the ring: dihydric and trihydric phenols.** Adding a second $\ce{-OH}$ at carbon 2 produces benzene-1,2-diol (dihydric), and adding a third at carbon 3 produces benzene-1,2,3-triol (trihydric).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Mirror both sides of oxygen: simple (symmetrical) ethers.** Replacing the hydroxyl hydrogen of ethanol with a second ethyl group forms diethyl ether ($\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$). Both arms around the oxygen bridge are identical, making the ether symmetrical.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Break the match across oxygen: mixed (unsymmetrical) ethers.** Pairing an ethyl group with a methyl group ($\ce{C2H5OCH3}$) or a phenyl ring ($\ce{C2H5OC6H5}$) puts two unequal groups on oxygen, creating a mixed or unsymmetrical ether.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- **Alcohols** have $\ce{-OH}$ on an aliphatic carbon, **phenols** have $\ce{-OH}$ directly on an aromatic ring carbon, and **ethers** join two alkyl or aryl groups across an oxygen atom ($\ce{R-O-R'}$, $\ce{Ar-O-R}$, or $\ce{Ar-O-Ar'}$).
- Both alcohols and phenols are sorted as **monohydric**, **dihydric**, **trihydric**, or **polyhydric** according to the number of $\ce{-OH}$ groups in the molecule.
- Monohydric alcohols with a $\ce{C_{sp^3}-OH}$ bond divide into **primary ($1^\circ$), secondary ($2^\circ$), and tertiary ($3^\circ$)** alkyl alcohols, **allylic alcohols** ($\ce{-OH}$ on an $sp^3$ carbon next to $\ce{C=C}$), and **benzylic alcohols** ($\ce{-OH}$ on an $sp^3$ carbon next to an aromatic ring); allylic and benzylic alcohols also occur as $1^\circ$, $2^\circ$, and $3^\circ$.
- Monohydric alcohols with a $\ce{C_{sp^2}-OH}$ bond attached to a non-aromatic $\ce{C=C}$ carbon are **vinylic alcohols** (such as $\ce{CH2=CH-OH}$).
- **Ethers** are **simple (symmetrical)** when both groups on oxygen are identical and **mixed (unsymmetrical)** when the two groups differ.
</KeyIdeas>
