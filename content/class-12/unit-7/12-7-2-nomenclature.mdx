## How common and IUPAC names work

Organic nomenclature uses two parallel systems. Common names focus on the substituent fragments attached to the functional oxygen atom: you name the alkyl or aryl group and append the class word (*alcohol* or *ether*), or use traditional ring names for substituted phenols. The IUPAC system instead treats the longest carbon chain or ring as a numbered skeleton and states the exact position of the functional group and every substituent with locant numbers.

## Naming alcohols

### Common names of alcohols

In the common system, an alcohol is named by writing the common name of the alkyl group attached to the $-\ce{OH}$ group followed by the word **alcohol**. For example, $\ce{CH3OH}$ carries a methyl group, so its common name is methyl alcohol. Prefixes such as *n-*, *iso-*, *sec-*, and *tert-* distinguish straight-chain and branched isomers of propyl and butyl alcohols.

### IUPAC rules for acyclic and cyclic alcohols

To build the IUPAC name of an alcohol, follow four steps:

1. **Identify the parent alkane:** Choose the longest continuous carbon chain that includes the carbon atom bonded to the $-\ce{OH}$ group. Replace the trailing `-e` of the alkane name with the suffix `-ol` (for instance, methane becomes methanol).
2. **Number from the $-\ce{OH}$ end:** Number the parent chain from the end closer to the hydroxyl group so that the carbon carrying $-\ce{OH}$ receives the lowest possible locant.
3. **Locate substituents and multiple $-\ce{OH}$ groups:** Prefix any alkyl branches with their carbon numbers. For polyhydric alcohols containing two or three $-\ce{OH}$ groups, **retain the trailing `-e`** of the parent alkane and add the multiplicative suffix `-diol` or `-triol` with locants (for example, $\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}$ is ethane-1,2-diol).
4. **Cyclic alcohols:** Add the prefix *cyclo-* before the alkane stem, treat the carbon bonded to $-\ce{OH}$ as C-1, and number around the ring to give substituents the lowest locant (for example, 2-methylcyclopentanol).

## Naming phenols and benzenediols

The simplest aromatic hydroxy compound, $\ce{C6H5OH}$, is called **phenol** in both the common and IUPAC systems. Because a benzene ring can carry substituents at different positions relative to $-\ce{OH}$, common names use the prefixes *ortho-* (*o-* for 1,2-disubstituted), *meta-* (*m-* for 1,3-disubstituted), and *para-* (*p-* for 1,4-disubstituted). In the IUPAC system, the carbon attached to $-\ce{OH}$ is C-1, so *o-*, *m-*, and *p-*cresol become 2-methylphenol, 3-methylphenol, and 4-methylphenol.

When two $-\ce{OH}$ groups sit on a benzene ring, the compound is named systematically by keeping the final `-e` of benzene and adding `-diol` with locants: benzene-1,2-diol (common name: catechol), benzene-1,3-diol (resorcinol), and benzene-1,4-diol (hydroquinone or quinol).

<Scrolly animation="alcohol-phenol-naming">
  <Step>
  **Simple alkyl alcohols:** Compare $\ce{CH3OH}$ and $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$. In the common system, the alkyl group determines the name: methyl alcohol and isopropyl alcohol. In the IUPAC system, the parent alkanes methane and propane drop their final `-e` and take `-ol`, giving methanol and propan-2-ol with the locant 2 marking the middle carbon.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Branched chains and lowest $-\ce{OH}$ locant:** Take $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2OH}$, commonly called isobutyl alcohol. The longest carbon chain carrying $-\ce{OH}$ contains three carbons (propane), not four. Numbering starts at the carbon bonded to $-\ce{OH}$ as C-1, which places the methyl branch at C-2 and yields 2-methylpropan-1-ol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Retaining `-e` in polyhydric alcohols:** Look at $\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}$ (ethylene glycol) and $\ce{CH2(OH)CH(OH)CH2OH}$ (glycerol). Because two or three $-\ce{OH}$ groups are present, the multiplicative suffix starts with a consonant (`d` or `t`). Therefore the final `-e` of ethane and propane is kept, giving ethane-1,2-diol and propane-1,2,3-triol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Cyclic alcohols set $-\ce{OH}$ at C-1:** On a six-membered saturated ring with one hydroxyl group, the name is cyclohexanol. On a five-membered ring bearing a $-\ce{OH}$ group and an adjacent methyl group, the hydroxyl carbon is fixed as C-1 and numbering proceeds around the ring toward the substituent, giving 2-methylcyclopentanol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Cresols translate *o/m/p* into numbers:** Phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$) keeps the same name in both systems. Adding one methyl group creates the three cresol isomers: *o*-cresol (adjacent, 1,2), *m*-cresol (one carbon apart, 1,3), and *p*-cresol (opposite, 1,4). Numbering from the $-\ce{OH}$ carbon as C-1 converts them to 2-methylphenol, 3-methylphenol, and 4-methylphenol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Dihydroxybenzenes:** Placing two $-\ce{OH}$ groups on a benzene ring produces three benzenediols. Retention of `-e` in benzene gives benzene-1,2-diol (catechol), benzene-1,3-diol (resorcinol), and benzene-1,4-diol (hydroquinone or quinol).
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Naming ethers

### Common names of ethers

Common names list the two alkyl or aryl groups bonded to oxygen in alphabetical order, followed by the word **ether**. For example, $\ce{CH3OC2H5}$ combines ethyl and methyl, giving ethylmethyl ether, and $\ce{CH3OCH2CH2CH3}$ gives methyl *n*-propyl ether. When both groups are identical, add the prefix *di-* (as in dimethyl ether for $\ce{CH3OCH3}$ and diethyl ether for $\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$). Two aromatic ethers also carry widely used traditional names: **anisole** ($\ce{C6H5OCH3}$) and **phenetole** ($\ce{C6H5OCH2CH3}$).

### IUPAC names of ethers

In the IUPAC system, ethers have no `-ether` suffix. Instead, an ether is viewed as a hydrocarbon in which one hydrogen atom has been replaced by an alkoxy ($-\ce{OR}$) or aryloxy ($-\ce{OAr}$) substituent. Two rules govern every structure:

1. **The larger group is the parent hydrocarbon:** Compare the carbon skeletons on either side of oxygen. The larger alkyl or aryl group becomes the parent alkane, cycloalkane, or arene; the smaller group plus oxygen becomes the `-oxy` substituent.
2. **Lowest locants on the parent skeleton:** Number the parent chain or ring to give the lowest possible locant set to all substituents (alkoxy and alkyl groups alike). For $\ce{C6H5OCH3}$, the six-carbon benzene ring is larger than the one-carbon methyl group, so the systematic name is methoxybenzene (with anisole also accepted by IUPAC).

<Scrolly animation="ether-naming">
  <Step>
  **Alphabetical fragments in common names:** For $\ce{CH3OCH3}$ and $\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$, identical groups take the prefix `di-`, giving dimethyl ether and diethyl ether. When the two groups differ, as in $\ce{CH3OCH2CH2CH3}$ or $\ce{CH3OCH(CH3)2}$, the common name lists both groups alphabetically before the word ether: methyl *n*-propyl ether and methyl isopropyl ether.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Larger group sets the parent in IUPAC names:** Split $\ce{CH3OCH2CH2CH3}$ at the oxygen bridge. The propyl chain (3 carbons) outnumbers the methyl group (1 carbon), so propane is the parent and $-\ce{OCH3}$ becomes a methoxy substituent at C-1: 1-methoxypropane. Moving the oxygen attachment to the middle carbon gives 2-methoxypropane.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Aryl ethers with small alkyl groups:** In $\ce{C6H5OCH3}$ and $\ce{C6H5OCH2CH3}$, the six-carbon benzene ring is larger than the methyl or ethyl chain. Benzene serves as the parent, producing methoxybenzene (also accepted as anisole) and ethoxybenzene (common name: phenetole).
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Watch the parent flip when the chain grows:** Compare phenyl isopentyl ether, $\ce{C6H5OCH2CH2CH(CH3)2}$, with heptyl phenyl ether, $\ce{C6H5O(CH2)6CH3}$. The isopentyl group has 5 carbons, fewer than benzene (6), so benzene stays the parent and the name is 3-methylbutoxybenzene. A heptyl chain has 7 carbons, beating benzene, so heptane becomes the parent and the ring becomes a 1-phenoxy substituent: 1-phenoxyheptane.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Diethers and substituted cycloalkane ethers:** In $\ce{CH3OCH2CH2OCH3}$, two methoxy substituents sit on carbons 1 and 2 of ethane, giving 1,2-dimethoxyethane. On a cyclohexane ring carrying two methyl groups on one carbon and an ethoxy group on the neighbouring carbon, numbering from the gem-dimethyl carbon gives the locant set `{1, 1, 2}` rather than `{1, 2, 2}`, producing 2-ethoxy-1,1-dimethylcyclohexane.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Common names of alcohols and ethers list the attached alkyl or aryl groups (alphabetically for mixed ethers, with *di-* for identical groups), whereas IUPAC names always number a parent carbon skeleton.
- For alcohols, select the longest carbon chain bearing $-\ce{OH}$, number from the $-\ce{OH}$ end, replace `-e` with `-ol`, and retain `-e` before `-diol` or `-triol` in polyhydric alcohols.
- In cyclic alcohols, the carbon attached to $-\ce{OH}$ is always C-1; in substituted phenols, common *o-*, *m-*, and *p-* prefixes correspond to locants 2, 3, and 4 relative to the $-\ce{OH}$ carbon.
- Benzenediols retain the `-e` of benzene to form benzene-1,2-diol (catechol), benzene-1,3-diol (resorcinol), and benzene-1,4-diol (hydroquinone or quinol).
- For ethers in the IUPAC system, the larger carbon group sets the parent hydrocarbon and the smaller $-\ce{OR}$ or $-\ce{OAr}$ group becomes an alkoxy or phenoxy substituent, with methoxybenzene also accepted as anisole.
</KeyIdeas>
