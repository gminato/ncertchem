## Two workhorse alcohols with one shared group

Among all alcohols, two dominate global industry: **methanol** ($\ce{CH3OH}$) and **ethanol** ($\ce{C2H5OH}$). Both are colourless liquids at room temperature because intermolecular hydrogen bonding through their $-\ce{OH}$ groups holds their molecules together. Ethanol boils at $351\text{ K}$, which is $14\text{ K}$ higher than methanol at $337\text{ K}$, because ethanol's larger two-carbon chain adds stronger van der Waals dispersion attractions alongside hydrogen bonding.

Despite that modest difference in structure, their manufacturing routes, toxicity profiles, and industrial safeguards could hardly be more distinct.

## Methanol: from wood spirit to high-pressure synthesis

Early chemists called methanol **wood spirit** because it was collected by the **destructive distillation of wood**, heating wood strongly in the absence of air. Today, almost all commercial methanol is synthesised from synthesis gas by the **catalytic hydrogenation of carbon monoxide** under high pressure and temperature over a mixed metal-oxide catalyst of zinc oxide and chromium(III) oxide ($\ce{ZnO-Cr2O3}$):

$$
\ce{CO + 2H2 ->[\ce{ZnO-Cr2O3}][200\text{-}300\text{ atm, } 573\text{-}673\text{ K}] CH3OH}
$$

Notice how demanding the reactor conditions are: pressures of $200\text{-}300\text{ atm}$ push the gas mixture toward fewer moles of product ($3\text{ mol}$ of gas become $1\text{ mol}$ of methanol), while $573\text{-}673\text{ K}$ supplies enough thermal energy for the $\ce{ZnO-Cr2O3}$ surface to drive a rapid conversion.

Methanol boils at $337\text{ K}$ and is **severely poisonous**. Swallowing even a small amount damages the optic nerve and causes blindness, and larger doses can be fatal (in the body, enzymes oxidise methanol into methanal, $\ce{HCHO}$, and methanoic acid, $\ce{HCOOH}$). In industry, methanol serves as a versatile solvent in paints and varnishes, and its single largest chemical application is as the feedstock for making **formaldehyde** ($\ce{HCHO}$).

## Ethanol: two-enzyme fermentation and its chemical limits

**Fermentation** of sugars is the oldest commercial method for making ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$). Raw sugar comes from molasses (the syrup left after sugar refining), sugarcane, or ripe fruits such as grapes. Turning table sugar (sucrose, $\ce{C12H22O11}$) into ethanol takes two distinct enzyme-catalysed steps:

1. **Hydrolysis of sucrose by invertase:** The enzyme **invertase** splits sucrose with water into two simpler six-carbon sugars, **glucose** and **fructose**, both of which share the molecular formula $\ce{C6H12O6}$:

$$
\ce{C12H22O11 + H2O ->[\text{Invertase}] C6H12O6\text{ (glucose)} + C6H12O6\text{ (fructose)}}
$$

2. **Fermentation of hexoses by zymase:** A second enzyme, **zymase** (found inside yeast cells), converts glucose and fructose into ethanol while releasing bubbles of carbon dioxide gas:

$$
\ce{C6H12O6 ->[\text{Zymase}] 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}
$$

Wine making shows both ingredients working together in nature. As grapes ripen, sugar builds up inside the fruit while wild yeast grows on the outer skin. Crushing the grapes mixes the sugar juice with the yeast enzymes so fermentation starts on its own.

Two strict chemical conditions keep the fermentation vat on track:

- **Anaerobic conditions (no air):** Zymase operates in the absence of air while releasing $\ce{CO2}$. If air leaks into the vessel, atmospheric oxygen oxidises ethanol into **ethanoic acid** ($\ce{CH3COOH}$), ruining the drink with a sour, vinegar-like taste.
- **The 14 percent inhibition limit:** Ethanol itself acts as a brake on the yeast enzyme. Once the concentration of alcohol in the fermenting liquid rises above **$14\%$**, zymase is inhibited and fermentation stops. Higher-strength spirits cannot be produced by direct fermentation alone; they require subsequent distillation.

## Ethanol's properties, effects, denaturation, and modern production

Pure ethanol is a colourless liquid with a boiling point of $351\text{ K}$. Industry relies on it both as a major solvent in the paint sector and as a versatile building block for synthesising many other carbon compounds.

Inside the human body, ingested ethanol acts directly on the **central nervous system**. In moderate amounts, it weakens judgment and lowers inhibitions. Higher concentrations trigger nausea and loss of consciousness, and very high concentrations interfere with spontaneous respiration and can prove fatal.

Because industrial ethanol is identical chemically to beverage ethanol, commercial alcohol is made deliberately unfit for drinking through a process called **denaturation of alcohol**. Two standard additives accomplish this:

- **Copper sulphate**, which gives the liquid an unmistakable blue colour.
- **Pyridine**, a foul-smelling liquid that makes it repulsive to taste or smell.

Finally, while fermentation remains essential for beverages and bio-based ethanol, vast industrial quantities of ethanol are now produced petrochemically by the **acid-catalysed hydration of ethene** ($\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O ->[\ce{H+}] CH3CH2OH}$), the alkene hydration route examined in Section 7.4.

<KeyIdeas>
- Methanol ($\ce{CH3OH}$, b.p. $337\text{ K}$), historically known as wood spirit, is produced today by catalytic hydrogenation of $\ce{CO}$ over a $\ce{ZnO-Cr2O3}$ catalyst at $200\text{-}300\text{ atm}$ and $573\text{-}673\text{ K}$.
- Methanol is severely poisonous (small amounts cause blindness and larger amounts cause death) and is used as a solvent in paints and varnishes and chiefly for manufacturing formaldehyde.
- Ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$, b.p. $351\text{ K}$) is obtained by fermenting sugars: invertase hydrolyses sucrose ($\ce{C12H22O11}$) to glucose and fructose ($\ce{C6H12O6}$), and yeast zymase ferments those hexoses into ethanol and $\ce{CO2}$.
- Fermentation requires anaerobic conditions: zymase is inhibited once ethanol exceeds $14\%$, and air oxidises ethanol to ethanoic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$), spoiling the taste.
- Large amounts of ethanol are also made by hydration of ethene, and commercial alcohol is denatured by adding copper sulphate for colour and pyridine for a foul odour.
</KeyIdeas>
