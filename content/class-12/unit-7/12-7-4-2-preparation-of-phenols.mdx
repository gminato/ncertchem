<Hook>
Early nineteenth-century chemists first isolated phenol, then called carbolic acid, from coal tar. Today, almost all phenol is synthesised from petroleum feedstocks. You cannot simply stir benzene with hydroxide ions to attach an $\ce{-OH}$ group, because an electron-rich aromatic ring resists direct nucleophilic attack. How do chemists open the door to the ring, and why does one industrial route supply most of the world's phenol while co-producing nail-polish remover?
</Hook>

## Why preparing phenol is harder than preparing an alcohol

In aliphatic chemistry, warm aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$ readily converts a haloalkane into an alcohol. On a benzene ring, however, the carbon bearing a substituent is $sp^2$ hybridised, and lone pairs on a halogen overlap with the aromatic $\pi$ cloud to give the carbon-halogen bond partial double-bond character. Direct substitution by hydroxide fails under mild laboratory conditions.

To overcome the ring's stability, chemists rely on one of four strategies: extreme temperature and pressure on a haloarene, conversion to a sulphonic acid followed by molten alkali fusion, transformation of an aryl amine into an unstable diazonium leaving group ($\ce{N2}$), or oxidation and acid cleavage of cumene.

## 1. From haloarenes (alkali fusion of chlorobenzene)

Chlorobenzene ($\ce{C6H5Cl}$) reacts with sodium hydroxide only under forcing conditions: heating at $623\text{ K}$ and $300\text{ atm}$ (the NCERT text also notes $320\text{ atm}$, while the reaction arrow prints $300\text{ atm}$). Because the reaction medium is strongly basic, the phenol formed immediately loses its acidic proton to excess $\ce{NaOH}$, producing sodium phenoxide ($\ce{C6H5O- Na+}$). Acidifying this sodium salt with dilute $\ce{HCl}$ liberates free phenol:

$$
\ce{C6H5Cl + NaOH ->[\text{623 K, 300 atm}] C6H5O-Na+ ->[\text{HCl}] C6H5OH}
$$

Notice that two distinct stages are required. Stopping after the high-pressure fusion leaves you with an ionic salt dissolved in water rather than neutral phenol; adding $\ce{HCl}$ protonates the phenoxide ion to complete the preparation.

## 2. From benzenesulphonic acid

Starting directly from benzene ($\ce{C6H6}$), an electrophilic aromatic substitution introduces a sulphonic acid group first. Treating benzene with oleum (fuming sulphuric acid) gives benzenesulphonic acid ($\ce{C6H5SO3H}$). Heating benzenesulphonic acid with molten sodium hydroxide converts it into sodium phenoxide, and subsequent acidification ($\ce{H+}$) yields phenol:

$$
\ce{C6H6 ->[\text{Oleum}] C6H5SO3H ->[\text{(i) NaOH, heat}][\text{(ii) H+}] C6H5OH}
$$

Just like the haloarene route, alkali fusion creates sodium phenoxide first, so an acid workup ($\ce{H+}$) is essential at the end to regenerate the $\ce{-OH}$ group.

## 3. From diazonium salts

A much milder laboratory route starts from an aromatic primary amine such as aniline ($\ce{C6H5NH2}$). Treating aniline with nitrous acid, generated in situ from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid ($\ce{NaNO2 + HCl}$) at $273\text{-}278\text{ K}$, converts the amino group into a diazonium salt, benzene diazonium chloride ($\ce{C6H5N2+ Cl-}$).

Keeping the mixture cold ($273\text{-}278\text{ K}$) prevents the diazonium salt from decomposing before it finishes forming. Once formed, gentle warming with water or dilute acid hydrolyses the diazonium salt cleanly into phenol, releasing nitrogen gas and hydrogen chloride:

$$
\ce{C6H5NH2 ->[\text{NaNO2 + HCl}][\text{273-278 K}] C6H5N2+Cl- ->[\text{H2O, warm}] C6H5OH + N2 + HCl}
$$

Why does this hydrolysis happen on simple warming when chlorobenzene needed $623\text{ K}$ and $300\text{ atm}$? The $\ce{-N2+}$ group departs as $\ce{N2}$, an extraordinarily stable gas molecule that bubbles out of solution and drives the conversion forward.

<Scrolly animation="phenol-prep-routes">
  <Step>
    **Halogen route (forcing conditions):** Chlorobenzene sits under intense heat and pressure ($623\text{ K}$, $300\text{ atm}$) with $\ce{NaOH}$. The tough aryl $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond finally gives way, forming sodium phenoxide ($\ce{C6H5O- Na+}$). Adding $\ce{HCl}$ protonates the oxygen and yields neutral phenol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Sulphonic acid route:** Benzene reacts with oleum to attach an $\ce{-SO3H}$ group. Heating benzenesulphonic acid with molten $\ce{NaOH}$ replaces the sulphonate group with $\ce{-O- Na+}$, and an $\ce{H+}$ acid wash restores phenol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Diazonium route (cold then warm):** Aniline meets $\ce{NaNO2 + HCl}$ in an ice bath held at $273\text{-}278\text{ K}$, turning $\ce{-NH2}$ into $\ce{-N2+ Cl-}$. Warming the aqueous salt lets $\ce{N2}$ gas escape, leaving phenol and $\ce{HCl}$ behind without extreme pressure.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Comparing workups:** In both the haloarene and sulphonic acid routes, strongly basic conditions trap the product as sodium phenoxide until acid is added. In the diazonium hydrolysis, water and gentle heat form phenol directly in acidic solution.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## 4. From cumene (the dominant industrial route)

Most of the world's phenol is manufactured not from chlorobenzene or aniline, but from the hydrocarbon **cumene** (isopropylbenzene). Cumene carries an isopropyl group, $\ce{-CH(CH3)2}$, attached to a benzene ring.

In the first step, cumene is oxidised by oxygen from air to form **cumene hydroperoxide**, where an $\ce{-O-O-H}$ peroxide unit sits on the tertiary benzylic carbon. In the second step, treating cumene hydroperoxide with dilute aqueous acid ($\ce{H+ / H2O}$) cleaves and rearranges the molecule into two valuable products: **phenol** and **acetone** ($\ce{CH3COCH3}$):

$$
\ce{C6H5-CH(CH3)2 ->[\text{O2}] C6H5-C(CH3)2-O-O-H ->[\text{H+, H2O}] C6H5OH + CH3COCH3}
$$

This two-step sequence dominates global industry for two reasons: atmospheric oxygen is an inexpensive oxidant, and the by-product acetone is recovered in large quantities as a major industrial chemical in its own right.

<Scrolly animation="cumene-hydroperoxide-cleavage">
  <Step>
    **The starting hydrocarbon:** Cumene (isopropylbenzene) displays a tertiary benzylic $\ce{C-H}$ bond flanked by two methyl groups and the benzene ring. That benzylic $\ce{C-H}$ bond is the reactive site for oxidation.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Air oxidation:** An $\ce{O2}$ molecule inserts into the benzylic $\ce{C-H}$ bond, building cumene hydroperoxide with a reactive $\ce{C-O-O-H}$ chain.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Acid-catalysed cleavage:** Dilute acid protonates the outer oxygen of the peroxide. As water departs, the phenyl ring shifts onto the remaining oxygen while the weak $\ce{O-O}$ bond breaks.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Dual industrial payoff:** Water completes the hydrolysis, splitting the intermediate cleanly into phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$) and acetone ($\ce{CH3COCH3}$), so every mole of cumene yields two useful organic products.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- **From haloarenes:** Fusing chlorobenzene with $\ce{NaOH}$ at $623\text{ K}$ and $300\text{ atm}$ (text states $320\text{ atm}$) gives sodium phenoxide, which is acidified with $\ce{HCl}$ to yield phenol.
- **From benzenesulphonic acid:** Benzene is sulphonated with oleum to $\ce{C6H5SO3H}$, heated with molten $\ce{NaOH}$ to form sodium phenoxide, and acidified with $\ce{H+}$ to liberate phenol.
- **From diazonium salts:** Diazotising aniline with $\ce{NaNO2 + HCl}$ at $273\text{-}278\text{ K}$ produces $\ce{C6H5N2+Cl-}$, which hydrolyses on warming with $\ce{H2O}$ or dilute acid to give phenol, $\ce{N2}$, and $\ce{HCl}$.
- **From cumene (industrial route):** Air oxidation of isopropylbenzene forms cumene hydroperoxide, and dilute acid ($\ce{H+ / H2O}$) cleaves it into phenol and the commercially valuable by-product acetone ($\ce{CH3COCH3}$).
</KeyIdeas>
