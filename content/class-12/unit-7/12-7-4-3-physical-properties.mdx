<Hook>
Ethanol ($\ce{CH3CH2OH}$) and methoxymethane ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$) are built from the exact same atoms and share an identical molecular mass of $46\text{ u}$. Yet methoxymethane is a gas that boils at $248\text{ K}$, whereas ethanol stays liquid all the way to $351\text{ K}$ and mixes with water in every proportion. How can moving one oxygen atom between a carbon and a hydrogen transform a gas into a high-boiling, water-loving liquid?
</Hook>

## Two halves of one molecule

Every alcohol ($\ce{R-OH}$) and every phenol ($\ce{Ar-OH}$) is made of two chemically distinct parts: a polar hydroxyl group ($\ce{-OH}$) and a nonpolar hydrocarbon portion, either an alkyl group ($\ce{R-}$) or an aryl ring ($\ce{Ar-}$).

The hydroxyl group controls the signature physical behaviour of both families because oxygen is strongly electronegative and pulls shared electrons away from hydrogen. Meanwhile, the size and shape of the alkyl or aryl group tune those baseline properties by changing the surface area available for weak intermolecular contacts and by introducing a water-repelling hydrocarbon region.

## Boiling points and intermolecular hydrogen bonding

Because the $\ce{O-H}$ bond is strongly polar, the hydrogen atom carries a partial positive charge ($\delta^+$) and the oxygen atom carries a partial negative charge ($\delta^-$) along with two unshared electron pairs. In liquid alcohols and phenols, the $\delta^+$ hydrogen of one molecule attracts a lone pair on the $\delta^-$ oxygen of a neighbour, linking molecules into chains and networks through **intermolecular hydrogen bonding** ($\ce{O-H\cdots O}$).

Breaking these hydrogen bonds requires considerable thermal energy. As a result, alcohols and phenols boil at substantially higher temperatures than hydrocarbons, ethers, haloalkanes, and haloarenes of comparable molecular mass.

A direct three-way comparison at molecular mass $44\text{ to }46\text{ u}$ makes this gap unmistakable:

- **Propane ($\ce{CH3CH2CH3}$, mass $44\text{ u}$, b.p. $231\text{ K}$):** A nonpolar alkane held together only by weak van der Waals dispersion forces.
- **Methoxymethane ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$, mass $46\text{ u}$, b.p. $248\text{ K}$):** An ether with polar $\ce{C-O}$ bonds and dipole-dipole attractions, but no $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen donor, so it boils only $17\text{ K}$ above propane.
- **Ethanol ($\ce{CH3CH2OH}$, mass $46\text{ u}$, b.p. $351\text{ K}$):** An alcohol capable of intermolecular hydrogen bonding, jumping $103\text{ K}$ above its isomer methoxymethane and $120\text{ K}$ above propane.

Two structural trends within alcohols and phenols further shift the boiling point:

1. **Increasing carbon chain length raises the boiling point.** Adding $\ce{-CH2-}$ units increases the number of electrons and total molecular surface area, strengthening van der Waals attractions between neighbouring molecules. Thus, boiling points climb steadily along the series from methanol ($337\text{ K}$) to ethanol ($351\text{ K}$) to butan-1-ol ($390\text{ K}$).
2. **Branching lowers the boiling point.** Among isomeric alcohols with the same molecular formula, a branched carbon skeleton packs into a more compact, roughly spherical shape than an extended straight chain. Less surface area touches neighbouring molecules, van der Waals forces weaken, and the boiling point drops from an unbranched primary alcohol to a secondary isomer to a compact tertiary isomer.

## Solubility in water

Alcohols and phenols dissolve in water for the same structural reason they boil high: their $\ce{-OH}$ group forms hydrogen bonds directly with $\ce{H2O}$ molecules. Water molecules donate hydrogen bonds to the two lone pairs on the hydroxyl oxygen, while the hydroxyl hydrogen donates a hydrogen bond to water's oxygen.

However, the hydrocarbon group pulls in the opposite direction. An alkyl chain or benzene ring is **hydrophobic** and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water; instead, it forces surrounding water molecules to reorganize around it. As the alkyl or aryl group grows larger, this hydrophobic disruption outweighs the pull of the single $\ce{-OH}$ group and solubility drops sharply. Lower-molecular-mass alcohols such as methanol and ethanol are miscible with water in all proportions, whereas larger alcohols such as butan-1-ol ($9\text{ g per } 100\text{ mL}$ of water) and phenols with bulky aromatic rings have much more limited solubility.

<Scrolly animation="alcohol-water-solubility">
  <Step>
    **Bridging into water's network:** A propan-1-ol molecule sits surrounded by three $\ce{H2O}$ molecules, forming dashed hydrogen bonds from its hydroxyl proton to one water oxygen and from two other water protons to the two lone pairs on its oxygen atom.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Hydrophilic head vs hydrophobic tail:** In methanol and ethanol, the small alkyl group barely perturbs the surrounding water network, so $\ce{-OH}$ hydration wins completely and the liquids mix in all proportions.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Larger chains push water apart:** Expanding the hydrocarbon portion to a four-carbon butyl chain (butan-1-ol) or a six-carbon phenyl ring (phenol) creates a wide water-excluding region around the molecule, cutting solubility dramatically compared with lower alcohols.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Alcohols ($\ce{R-OH}$) and phenols ($\ce{Ar-OH}$) combine a polar $\ce{-OH}$ group that governs hydrogen bonding with a hydrocarbon group ($\ce{R}$ or $\ce{Ar}$) that tunes van der Waals contact and hydrophobicity.
- Intermolecular $\ce{O-H\cdots O}$ hydrogen bonding makes alcohols and phenols boil far higher than hydrocarbons, ethers, haloalkanes, and haloarenes of comparable molecular mass, as seen in ethanol ($351\text{ K}$) versus methoxymethane ($248\text{ K}$) and propane ($231\text{ K}$).
- Within alcohols, boiling points increase with carbon count (stronger van der Waals forces) and decrease with chain branching (smaller molecular surface area).
- Solubility in water arises from hydrogen bonding between $\ce{-OH}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ molecules, and decreases as the hydrophobic alkyl or aryl group grows larger.
</KeyIdeas>
