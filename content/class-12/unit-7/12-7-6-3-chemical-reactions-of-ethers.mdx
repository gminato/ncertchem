<Hook>
Why does anisole surrender its methyl group to hot concentrated $\ce{HI}$ while keeping its phenyl-oxygen bond intact, yet react so rapidly with bromine in ethanoic acid that it needs no Lewis acid catalyst at all? Both outcomes trace back to the same oxygen atom and how its electrons interact with the carbons beside it.
</Hook>

## Why ethers need harsh conditions to cleave

Among common organic classes, ethers are unusually unreactive. They carry no acidic $\ce{O-H}$ proton, and an alkoxide ion ($-\ce{OR}$) is a poor leaving group on its own, so mild acids, bases, and dilute reagents leave the $\ce{C-O}$ linkage untouched.

Splitting that $\ce{C-O}$ bond takes drastic conditions: heating the ether with excess concentrated hydrogen halide. Not all hydrogen halides are equally effective; their reactivity follows the order:

$$
\ce{HI > HBr > HCl}
$$

Concentrated $\ce{HI}$ or $\ce{HBr}$ at high temperature works best because both are strong enough acids to protonate the ether oxygen and supply strongly nucleophilic halide ions ($\ce{I-}$ or $\ce{Br-}$).

With a symmetrical dialkyl ether, one equivalent of $\ce{HX}$ yields an alkyl halide and an alcohol, and excess $\ce{HX}$ at high temperature converts that alcohol into a second alkyl halide molecule:

$$
\ce{R-O-R + HX -> R-X + R-OH}
$$

$$
\ce{R-OH + HX -> R-X + H2O}
$$

When an ether carries two different alkyl groups, one side becomes the alkyl halide and the other leaves as the alcohol:

$$
\ce{R-O-R' + HX -> R-X + R'-OH}
$$

Alkyl aryl ethers behave differently from purely aliphatic ethers. Because the bond between the aromatic ring carbon and oxygen is much stronger than the bond to the alkyl group, cleavage always occurs at the alkyl-oxygen bond to give phenol and an alkyl halide:

$$
\ce{C6H5-O-R + HX -> C6H5-OH + R-X}
$$

## How the mechanism decides which bond breaks

The cleavage of ethers by concentrated $\ce{HI}$ always begins with protonation, yet the second step forks between $\mathrm{S_N2}$ and $\mathrm{S_N1}$ depending on the carbon attached to oxygen.

### Primary and secondary alkyl ethers: the $\mathrm{S_N2}$ route

Take ethyl methyl ether ($\ce{CH3-O-CH2CH3}$) reacting with concentrated $\ce{HI}$:

1. **Step 1 (Protonation):** A lone pair on oxygen captures a proton from $\ce{HI}$ in a reversible equilibrium, converting the poor $-\ce{OR}$ leaving group into a good neutral alcohol leaving group inside an oxonium ion:

$$
\ce{CH3-O-CH2CH3 + H-I <=> CH3-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH2CH3 + I-}
$$

2. **Step 2 ($\mathrm{S_N2}$ displacement):** Iodide is a good nucleophile. It attacks the less sterically hindered (less substituted) carbon of the oxonium ion from the back side, pushing out ethanol through a concerted transition state:

$$
\ce{I- + CH3-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH2CH3 -> CH3I + CH3CH2OH}
$$

When an unsymmetrical ether contains only primary or secondary alkyl groups, the lower (less substituted) alkyl group forms the alkyl iodide, while the bulkier group departs as the alcohol.

3. **Step 3 (Excess $\ce{HI}$ at high temperature):** If $\ce{HI}$ is present in excess and the mixture is heated strongly, the ethanol formed in Step 2 is protonated to an ethyl oxonium ion and attacked by a second iodide ion:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2-O-H + H-I <=> CH3CH2-\overset{+}{O}H2 + I-}
$$

$$
\ce{I- + CH3CH2-\overset{+}{O}H2 -> CH3CH2I + H2O}
$$

### Tertiary alkyl ethers: the $\mathrm{S_N1}$ reversal

Introduce a tertiary alkyl group, as in *tert*-butyl methyl ether ($\ce{(CH3)3C-O-CH3}$), and the regioselectivity flips completely:

$$
\ce{(CH3)3C-O-CH3 + HI -> CH3OH + (CH3)3C-I}
$$

Here the **tertiary** group becomes the alkyl iodide, not the methyl group. After protonation forms $\ce{(CH3)3C-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH3}$, backside attack at the crowded tertiary carbon is blocked. Instead, the $\ce{C-O}$ bond breaks slowly on its own because departure of $\ce{CH3OH}$ generates a stable tertiary carbocation, $\ce{(CH3)3C+}$:

$$
\ce{(CH3)3C-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH3 ->[\text{slow}] (CH3)3C+ + CH3OH}
$$

$$
\ce{(CH3)3C+ + I- ->[\text{fast}] (CH3)3C-I}
$$

Because carbocation stability drives the rate-determining step, tertiary alkyl ethers cleave by an $\mathrm{S_N1}$ mechanism.

### Alkyl aryl ethers: why phenol never turns into a halide

When anisole ($\ce{C6H5-O-CH3}$) is protonated by $\ce{HI}$, it forms the methylphenyl oxonium ion, $\ce{C6H5-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH3}$. Two electronic factors make its $\ce{O-C6H5}$ bond markedly stronger than its $\ce{O-CH3}$ bond:

- The carbon atom of the phenyl ring is $sp^2$ hybridised, pulling bonding electrons closer than an $sp^3$ methyl carbon.
- Conjugation between an oxygen lone pair and the aromatic $\pi$ system gives the $\ce{O-C6H5}$ bond partial double-bond character.

Iodide therefore attacks exclusively at the methyl carbon, breaking the $\ce{O-CH3}$ bond to produce $\ce{CH3I}$ and phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$). Even with excess $\ce{HI}$, phenol does not convert into iodobenzene because the $sp^2$ ring carbon cannot undergo nucleophilic substitution.

## Electrophilic substitution on aromatic ethers

When an ether oxygen sits directly on a benzene ring, as in anisole, the $-\ce{OR}$ group governs how the ring reacts with electrophiles. Just like the $-\ce{OH}$ group of phenol, an alkoxy group **activates** the benzene ring and is ***ortho, para* directing**.

### Why $-\ce{OR}$ activates the *ortho* and *para* positions

A lone pair on the ether oxygen overlaps with the $\pi$ cloud of the benzene ring. Across the resonance contributors of an aryl alkyl ether, pushing that oxygen lone pair toward the ring creates a $\ce{C=O+R}$ double bond and places a formal negative charge specifically on the two *ortho* carbons and the *para* carbon, while the *meta* carbons never carry that excess electron density.

Because the *ortho* and *para* carbons are enriched with electron density, incoming electrophiles attack there rapidly, and the ring reacts much more readily than benzene itself.

### Characteristic ring reactions of anisole

1. **Halogenation (Bromination):**
   Anisole undergoes bromination with $\ce{Br2}$ in ethanoic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$) **even without an iron(III) bromide ($\ce{FeBr3}$) catalyst**, because the $-\ce{OCH3}$ group strongly activates the ring. The *para* isomer (*p*-bromoanisole) is obtained in **90% yield** as the major product alongside *o*-bromoanisole as the minor product:

$$
\ce{C6H5OCH3 + Br2 ->[\text{CH3COOH}] p-BrC6H4OCH3 \text{ (90\%, major)} + o-BrC6H4OCH3 \text{ (minor)}}
$$

2. **Friedel-Crafts reactions:**
   Treating anisole with an alkyl halide or an acyl halide in the presence of anhydrous aluminium chloride ($\ce{AlCl3}$, a Lewis acid catalyst) introduces the alkyl or acyl group at the *ortho* and *para* positions:
   - **Alkylation:** With chloromethane ($\ce{CH3Cl}$) and anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ in carbon disulphide ($\ce{CS2}$), anisole gives **4-methoxytoluene** (major) and **2-methoxytoluene** (minor).
   - **Acylation:** With ethanoyl chloride ($\ce{CH3COCl}$) and anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$, anisole gives **4-methoxyacetophenone** (major) and **2-methoxyacetophenone** (minor).

3. **Nitration:**
   Reacting anisole with a mixture of concentrated sulphuric acid ($\ce{H2SO4}$) and concentrated nitric acid ($\ce{HNO3}$) yields a mixture of **4-nitroanisole** (major) and **2-nitroanisole** (minor).

<KeyIdeas>
- Ethers are unreactive toward mild reagents and cleave only with concentrated hydrogen halides at high temperature in the reactivity order $\ce{HI > HBr > HCl}$.
- Primary and secondary mixed ethers cleave by an $\mathrm{S_N2}$ pathway where $\ce{I-}$ attacks the less substituted alkyl carbon; with excess $\ce{HI}$ and heat, the resulting alcohol also converts to an alkyl iodide.
- Ethers containing a tertiary alkyl group switch to an $\mathrm{S_N1}$ pathway because departure of the alcohol generates a stable tertiary carbocation, producing the tertiary alkyl iodide.
- Alkyl aryl ethers such as anisole cleave at the weaker $\ce{O-R}$ bond to give phenol and an alkyl halide, because the aryl carbon is $sp^2$ hybridised with partial double-bond character and phenol resists nucleophilic substitution.
- An alkoxy group ($-\ce{OR}$) strongly activates the benzene ring and directs electrophiles *ortho* and *para* via resonance, enabling catalyst-free bromination of anisole (90% *para*), Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation, and nitration.
</KeyIdeas>
