<Hook>
Ethoxyethane and butan-1-ol share the same molecular formula, $\ce{C4H10O}$, and the exact same molar mass. Yet ethoxyethane boils at $307.6\text{ K}$, barely different from n-pentane at $309.1\text{ K}$, while butan-1-ol refuses to boil until $390\text{ K}$. Drop both oxygen compounds into water, however, and ethoxyethane dissolves nearly as well as the alcohol while n-pentane stays apart. How can one oxygen bridge behave like a hydrocarbon in the pure liquid and like an alcohol in water, and how do chemists forge that $\ce{C-O-C}$ bridge without turning the reactants into an alkene instead?
</Hook>

## Preparation by dehydration of alcohols

Alcohols lose water when heated with protic acids such as sulphuric acid ($\ce{H2SO4}$) or phosphoric acid ($\ce{H3PO4}$). Two reaction paths compete inside the flask: bimolecular substitution, which links two alcohol molecules into an ether, and elimination, which produces an alkene. Reaction temperature tips the balance.

With ethanol and concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$:

- Heating at **$413\text{ K}$** favours substitution and yields **ethoxyethane** ($\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$, commonly called diethyl ether) as the main product.
- Raising the temperature to **$443\text{ K}$** favours elimination and yields **ethene** ($\ce{CH2=CH2}$).

$$
\ce{CH3CH2OH ->[\ce{H2SO4},\; 413\text{ K}] C2H5OC2H5} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \ce{CH3CH2OH ->[\ce{H2SO4},\; 443\text{ K}] CH2=CH2 + H2O}
$$

Historically, diethyl ether served widely as an inhalation anaesthetic, though its slow action and unpleasant recovery period eventually led medicine to replace it with safer alternatives.

### How the $\mathrm{S_N2}$ dehydration mechanism works

At $413\text{ K}$, ether formation proceeds by a bimolecular nucleophilic substitution ($\mathrm{S_N2}$) pathway in three steps, where a neutral ethanol molecule attacks a protonated ethanol molecule:

1. **Protonation of the alcohol:** An oxygen lone pair accepts a proton from the acid catalyst to convert the poor $-\ce{OH}$ leaving group into a good $-\ce{OH2+}$ leaving group.

$$
\ce{CH3-CH2-OH + H+ -> CH3-CH2-\overset{+}{O}H2}
$$

2. **Nucleophilic $\mathrm{S_N2}$ attack:** A second ethanol molecule uses an oxygen lone pair to attack the $\alpha$-carbon of the protonated alcohol from the back, displacing a neutral water molecule.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2-OH + CH3-CH2-\overset{+}{O}H2 -> CH3CH2-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH2CH3 + H2O}
$$

3. **Deprotonation:** The protonated ether transfers $\ce{H+}$ back to the medium, freeing the neutral ether and regenerating the acid catalyst.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH2CH3 -> CH3CH2-O-CH2CH3 + H+}
$$

### Scope and limitations of acid-catalysed dehydration

Acid dehydration is a clean route only for **symmetrical ethers** carrying **unhindered primary alkyl groups** at controlled low temperatures. Two important limitations restrict its use:

- **Secondary and tertiary alcohols fail to give ethers:** With branched $2^\circ$ or $3^\circ$ alcohols, dehydration shifts toward an $\mathrm{S_N1}$-type pathway in which elimination easily outpaces substitution, so alkenes form instead of ethers.
- **Unsymmetrical ethers give mixtures:** Trying to make ethyl methyl ether ($\ce{CH3-O-CH2CH3}$) by dehydrating a mixture of methanol and ethanol lets either alcohol protonate and either alcohol act as the nucleophile. That creates three competing ethers ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$, $\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$, and $\ce{CH3OC2H5}$) that are tedious to separate.

<Scrolly animation="ether-dehydration-vs-alkene">
  <Step>
    **Protonating the leaving group:** Ethanol meets $\ce{H+}$ from $\ce{H2SO4}$. An oxygen lone pair captures the proton, turning the hydroxyl group into $-\ce{OH2+}$ so that water can leave cleanly in the next step.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Bimolecular attack at 413 K:** Keeping the temperature at $413\text{ K}$ lets a second ethanol molecule attack the carbon bonded to $-\ce{OH2+}$ via an $\mathrm{S_N2}$ displacement. Water departs, and losing $\ce{H+}$ from the oxonium intermediate yields ethoxyethane ($\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Elimination takes over at 443 K:** Raising the temperature to $443\text{ K}$ shifts the pathway to elimination. Instead of another ethanol attacking carbon, a $\beta$-proton leaves and a $\pi$ bond forms, giving ethene ($\ce{CH2=CH2}$) and water.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Why branching and mixtures fail:** Replacing a $1^\circ$ alcohol with a $2^\circ$ or $3^\circ$ alcohol accelerates elimination over substitution to give alkenes, while mixing two $1^\circ$ alcohols such as $\ce{CH3OH}$ and $\ce{C2H5OH}$ yields all three possible ethers ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$, $\ce{CH3OC2H5}$, and $\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$).
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Williamson synthesis

To build **both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers** selectively, including ethers containing secondary or tertiary alkyl groups, chemists turn to the **Williamson synthesis**, named after Alexander William Williamson (1824-1904), who became Professor of Chemistry at University College, London in 1849.

In this laboratory method, an alkyl halide reacts with a sodium alkoxide:

$$
\ce{R-X + R'-O^-Na+ -> R-O-R' + NaX}
$$

The reaction proceeds by **$\mathrm{S_N2}$ nucleophilic attack** of the alkoxide ion ($\ce{R'-O-}$) on the alkyl halide ($\ce{R-X}$), displacing halide ($\ce{X-}$).

### Why the halide must be primary

Alkoxide ions are strong nucleophiles, but they are also **strong bases**. Because $\mathrm{S_N2}$ attack is sensitive to steric crowding, the structure of the alkyl halide decides whether substitution or elimination wins:

- **Primary alkyl halide ($1^\circ$ $\ce{R-X}$): substitution dominates.** Unhindered primary halides undergo smooth $\mathrm{S_N2}$ displacement even when the alkoxide itself is bulky. For example, sodium tert-butoxide reacts cleanly with bromomethane to produce tert-butyl methyl ether (2-methoxy-2-methylpropane):

$$
\ce{(CH3)3C-O^-Na+ + CH3-Br -> (CH3)3C-O-CH3 + NaBr}
$$

- **Secondary and tertiary alkyl halides: elimination competes or dominates.** With a $2^\circ$ halide, elimination competes strongly with substitution. With a **$3^\circ$ alkyl halide**, steric hindrance blocks backside $\mathrm{S_N2}$ attack completely, so the alkoxide acts purely as a base, abstracting a $\beta$-proton to form **an alkene as the sole organic product** with zero ether. Reversing the partners above illustrates this pitfall: sodium methoxide with tert-butyl bromide yields exclusively 2-methylpropene:

$$
\ce{(CH3)3C-Br + CH3O^-Na+ -> CH2=C(CH3)2 + NaBr + CH3OH}
$$

### Making alkyl aryl ethers from phenols

Phenols also undergo Williamson synthesis cleanly when converted first into the **phenoxide ion**. Treating phenol with aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$ generates sodium phenoxide ($\ce{C6H5O^-Na+}$), which then attacks an alkyl halide ($\ce{R-X}$) by $\mathrm{S_N2}$ substitution to produce an alkyl aryl ether ($\ce{C6H5-O-R}$), such as methoxybenzene (anisole) when $\ce{R-X}$ is a methyl halide:

$$
\ce{C6H5-OH + NaOH -> C6H5-O^-Na+ ->[\ce{R-X}] C6H5-O-R + NaX}
$$

<Scrolly animation="williamson-sn2-vs-e2">
  <Step>
    **The general $\mathrm{S_N2}$ displacement:** An alkoxide oxygen lone pair attacks the carbon of a primary alkyl halide ($\ce{R-X}$) from the side opposite the leaving halide, forming a new $\ce{C-O}$ bond while $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{X-}$ pair up as $\ce{NaX}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Bulky alkoxide on a primary halide succeeds:** Sodium tert-butoxide ($\ce{(CH3)3C-O^-Na+}$) approaches bromomethane ($\ce{CH3-Br}$). Because the methyl carbon is unhindered and carries no $\beta$-hydrogens, $\mathrm{S_N2}$ substitution proceeds cleanly to tert-butyl methyl ether ($\ce{(CH3)3C-O-CH3}$).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Swapping partners triggers elimination:** With tert-butyl bromide ($\ce{(CH3)3C-Br}$) and sodium methoxide ($\ce{CH3O^-Na+}$), three methyl groups block backside attack. Methoxide abstracts a $\beta$-hydrogen instead, ejecting $\ce{Br-}$ and producing 2-methylpropene ($\ce{CH2=C(CH3)2}$), $\ce{NaBr}$, and $\ce{CH3OH}$ with no ether formed.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **From phenol to alkyl aryl ether:** Phenol reacts with $\ce{NaOH}$ to form the resonance-stabilised sodium phenoxide ($\ce{C6H5O^-Na+}$). The phenoxide oxygen then displaces halide from $\ce{R-X}$ in an $\mathrm{S_N2}$ step to yield the alkyl aryl ether $\ce{C6H5-O-R}$.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Physical properties of ethers

### Polarity and boiling points

In an ether, each $\ce{C-O}$ bond is polar because oxygen is more electronegative than carbon. Because the $\ce{C-O-C}$ bond angle is bent (for example, $111.7^\circ$ in methoxymethane), the two $\ce{C-O}$ bond dipoles do not cancel, giving ether molecules a **net dipole moment**.

Despite this polarity, ethers boil far below alcohols of similar molar mass and close to alkanes of comparable molar mass. The reason lies in hydrogen bonding:

- **Alcohols** carry an $-\ce{O-H}$ group, so molecules link together in the pure liquid through strong intermolecular $\ce{O-H\cdots O}$ hydrogen bonds.
- **Ethers** have no hydrogen attached to oxygen, so pure ether molecules cannot hydrogen-bond with one another. Their weak dipole-dipole attractions add little beyond ordinary van der Waals forces.

| Compound | Formula | Boiling point ($\text{K}$) | Miscibility in water ($\text{g per 100 mL}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| n-Pentane | $\ce{CH3(CH2)3CH3}$ | $309.1$ | Essentially immiscible |
| Ethoxyethane | $\ce{C2H5-O-C2H5}$ | $307.6$ | $7.5$ |
| Butan-1-ol | $\ce{CH3(CH2)3-OH}$ | $390$ | $9$ |

Notice that ethoxyethane ($307.6\text{ K}$) and n-pentane ($309.1\text{ K}$) sit within $1.5\text{ K}$ of each other, whereas butan-1-ol boils at $390\text{ K}$, more than $80\text{ K}$ higher.

### Miscibility with water

When an ether enters water, the picture changes completely. Although an ether molecule cannot donate a hydrogen bond to another ether molecule, its oxygen atom still holds two lone pairs and **accepts hydrogen bonds from water's $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogens** ($\ce{R2O\cdots H-O-H}$).

As a result, the water miscibility of lower ethers closely tracks that of alcohols of similar molar mass:

- **Ethoxyethane** dissolves to the extent of **$7.5\text{ g per 100 mL}$** of water, very close to **butan-1-ol** at **$9\text{ g per 100 mL}$** of water.
- **n-Pentane** lacks an oxygen atom, cannot form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, and remains **essentially immiscible**.

<Scrolly animation="ether-hbond-boiling-miscibility">
  <Step>
    **Net dipole without self-hydrogen bonding:** In an ether, two polar $\ce{C-O}$ bonds meet at a bent angle around oxygen, creating a net molecular dipole. However, with no $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen present, neighbouring ether molecules cannot form hydrogen bonds with one another.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Boiling points side by side:** Comparing molecules of similar molar mass puts n-pentane at $309.1\text{ K}$ and ethoxyethane right beside it at $307.6\text{ K}$, while butan-1-ol towers above both at $390\text{ K}$ because extra thermal energy is required to break its intermolecular hydrogen-bond network.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Hydrogen bonding with water flips solubility:** In aqueous solution, water molecules donate $\ce{O-H}$ hydrogen bonds to the lone pairs on ethoxyethane's oxygen atom, lifting its solubility to $7.5\text{ g per 100 mL}$, close to butan-1-ol ($9\text{ g per 100 mL}$), while n-pentane stays separated as an immiscible layer.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- Acid-catalysed dehydration of ethanol with $\ce{H2SO4}$ is temperature-controlled: $413\text{ K}$ gives ethoxyethane ($\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$) via an $\mathrm{S_N2}$ pathway, whereas $443\text{ K}$ gives ethene ($\ce{CH2=CH2}$); the method works cleanly only for symmetrical ethers from unhindered primary alcohols.
- The Williamson synthesis ($\ce{R-X}$ + $\ce{R'-O^-Na+}$ $\rightarrow$ $\ce{R-O-R'}$ + $\ce{NaX}$) builds both symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers by $\mathrm{S_N2}$ displacement and requires a primary alkyl halide; with tertiary alkyl halides, strong-base elimination yields exclusively an alkene.
- Phenols form alkyl aryl ethers through sodium phenoxide ($\ce{C6H5O^-Na+}$) reacting with an alkyl halide ($\ce{R-X}$).
- Ethers have a net dipole moment from their bent polar $\ce{C-O-C}$ bonds, yet lack $\ce{O-H}$ self-hydrogen bonding, so their boiling points match alkanes of comparable molar mass ($307.6\text{ K}$ for ethoxyethane vs $309.1\text{ K}$ for n-pentane, far below $390\text{ K}$ for butan-1-ol) while their oxygen lone pairs accept hydrogen bonds from water to give alcohol-like miscibility ($7.5\text{ g}$ vs $9\text{ g}$ per $100\text{ mL}$ water).
</KeyIdeas>
