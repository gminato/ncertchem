<Hook>
Methanol, phenol, and methoxymethane all place an oxygen atom between carbon and a second partner. Swap a saturated methyl carbon for a benzene ring and the C-O bond suddenly tightens by 6 pm; swap the hydrogen on oxygen for a second methyl group and the angle around oxygen swings open by nearly three degrees. Why does one oxygen centre respond so sharply to its neighbours?
</Hook>

## Starting from oxygen and its four electron pairs

In alcohols, phenols, and ethers, the central oxygen atom forms two single ($\sigma$) bonds and keeps two unshared electron pairs (lone pairs). That gives four electron pairs around oxygen in every case. Four electron domains repel each other toward the corners of a tetrahedron, so the oxygen atom sits in an approximately $sp^3$-like tetrahedral environment whose ideal angle is $109^\circ 28'$ (about $109.5^\circ$).

If nothing else changed, every $\text{C-O-H}$ or $\text{C-O-C}$ bond angle would sit right near $109^\circ 28'$, and every $\text{C-O}$ bond length would look identical. Measurements of the three representative molecules from NCERT Fig. 7.1, methanol ($\ce{CH3OH}$), phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$), and methoxymethane ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$), show a much richer story. Two effects reshape the geometry around oxygen: how the attached carbon is hybridised (and whether it conjugates with oxygen), and how much space the groups attached to oxygen demand.

## Alcohols: $sp^3$-$sp^3$ overlap and lone-pair compression

In an aliphatic alcohol such as methanol ($\ce{CH3OH}$), the carbon carrying the hydroxyl ($-\text{OH}$) group forms four single bonds, so it uses $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals. Oxygen likewise uses $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals: two form $\sigma$ bonds (one to carbon by $sp^3$-$sp^3$ overlap and one to hydrogen by $sp^3$-$1s$ overlap), while the remaining two hold lone pairs of electrons.

Why is the $\text{C-O-H}$ angle ($108.9^\circ$) slightly smaller than the regular tetrahedral angle ($109^\circ 28'$)? A lone pair stays closer to the oxygen nucleus and spreads over a wider angular region than a shared bonding pair. Repulsion between the two unshared electron pairs on oxygen is stronger than repulsion between the $\text{C-O}$ and $\text{O-H}$ bond pairs, so the lone pairs press the two bonds slightly closer together.

## Phenols: conjugation and $sp^2$ carbon contraction

In phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$), the $-\text{OH}$ oxygen is attached directly to an $sp^2$ hybridised carbon of the benzene ring, with a $\text{C-O-H}$ angle of $109^\circ$. Its standout structural feature is the $\text{C-O}$ bond length of $136\ \text{pm}$, which is $6\ \text{pm}$ shorter than the $142\ \text{pm}$ $\text{C-O}$ bond in methanol.

Two separate structural factors act in the same direction to shorten and strengthen the $\text{C-O}$ bond in phenol:

1. **Partial double-bond character by conjugation:** One of the lone pairs on oxygen lies in an orbital aligned with the $\pi$ orbitals of the aromatic ring. Delocalising that lone pair into the ring places partial $\pi$ bonding between carbon and oxygen (a feature that also governs phenol's acidity and ring reactivity in Section 7.4.4). Because double bonds are shorter than single bonds, any partial double-bond character pulls carbon and oxygen closer together.
2. **Higher $s$-character of an $sp^2$ carbon orbital:** An $sp^2$ hybrid orbital has $33.3\%$ $s$-character, compared with $25\%$ $s$-character in an $sp^3$ orbital. Because $s$ orbitals sit closer to the nucleus than $p$ orbitals, the $sp^2$ carbon orbital forms a shorter, tighter $\sigma$ bond with oxygen than the $sp^3$ carbon of methanol.

## Ethers: bulky alkyl groups open the angle

In methoxymethane ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$), oxygen still carries two bonding pairs and two lone pairs in an approximately tetrahedral arrangement, and each attached methyl carbon is $sp^3$ hybridised without aromatic conjugation. Consequently, the $\text{C-O}$ bond length is $141\ \text{pm}$, nearly identical to the $142\ \text{pm}$ $\text{C-O}$ bond in alcohols.

What changes sharply is the $\text{C-O-C}$ bond angle, which opens up to $111.7^\circ$ (exceeding the tetrahedral $109^\circ 28'$). In methanol, one side of oxygen holds only a tiny hydrogen atom, so lone-pair repulsion wins and narrows the angle to $108.9^\circ$. In an ether, oxygen holds two bulky alkyl ($-\text{R}$) groups. Repulsive interaction between the two alkyl groups pushes the two $\text{C-O}$ bonds apart, widening the $\text{C-O-C}$ angle beyond $109^\circ 28'$.

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- In alcohols ($\ce{CH3OH}$), oxygen bonds to an $sp^3$ carbon through an $sp^3$-$sp^3$ $\sigma$ overlap ($\text{C-O} = 142\ \text{pm}$, $\text{O-H} = 96\ \text{pm}$), and repulsion between oxygen's two lone pairs narrows the $\text{C-O-H}$ angle to $108.9^\circ$, slightly less than the tetrahedral angle ($109^\circ 28'$).
- In phenols ($\ce{C6H5OH}$), the $-\text{OH}$ group sits on an $sp^2$ carbon of the aromatic ring ($\text{C-O-H} = 109^\circ$), and the $\text{C-O}$ bond shortens to $136\ \text{pm}$.
- Phenol's shorter $\text{C-O}$ bond results from two effects: partial double-bond character caused by conjugation of an oxygen lone pair with the aromatic ring, and the $sp^2$ hybridised state of the ring carbon.
- In ethers ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$), the four electron pairs on oxygen stay approximately tetrahedral with a $\text{C-O}$ length of $141\ \text{pm}$ (almost the same as in alcohols), while repulsion between the two bulky alkyl ($-\text{R}$) groups widens the $\text{C-O-C}$ angle to $111.7^\circ$, slightly above $109^\circ 28'$.
</KeyIdeas>
