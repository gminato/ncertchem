## Why alcohols cleave at C-O while phenols hold on

In an alcohol, the hydroxyl oxygen is attached to an $\text{sp}^3$-hybridised alkyl carbon by a polar $\ce{C-O}$ single bond. Once protonated or converted into a better leaving group, that $\ce{C-O}$ bond can cleave cleanly. Phenols behave differently: the $\ce{C-O}$ bond between the aromatic ring and oxygen carries partial double-bond character from resonance, and the $\text{sp}^2$ ring carbon resists nucleophilic displacement and elimination. As a result, the $\ce{C-O}$ cleavage reactions in this section belong to alcohols; phenols undergo $\ce{C-O}$ bond cleavage only under special reducing conditions with zinc dust.

## 1. Reaction with hydrogen halides and the Lucas test

Alcohols react with hydrogen halides ($\ce{HX}$) to give alkyl halides and water:

$$
\ce{ROH + HX -> R-X + H2O}
$$

Because primary ($1^\circ$), secondary ($2^\circ$), and tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohols cleave the $\ce{C-O}$ bond at very different rates, their reaction with concentrated hydrochloric acid forms the basis of a classic laboratory distinction known as the **Lucas test**.

**Lucas reagent** is a mixture of concentrated $\ce{HCl}$ and anhydrous zinc chloride ($\ce{ZnCl2}$). The test works because of a sharp solubility contrast:

- **Alcohols** dissolve completely in Lucas reagent, forming a clear, homogeneous solution.
- **Alkyl chlorides** ($\ce{R-Cl}$) are immiscible with the aqueous acid medium, so as soon as they form, fine droplets separate out and make the mixture **turbid** (cloudy).

Zinc chloride coordinates to the oxygen atom of the alcohol, weakening the $\ce{C-O}$ bond and assisting its cleavage. At room temperature, the three classes of alcohols respond differently:

- **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohols** form the most stable carbocations and convert to alkyl chlorides rapidly, producing **immediate turbidity**.
- **Secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohols** react at an intermediate pace, showing turbidity after standing for a short time.
- **Primary ($1^\circ$) alcohols** react too slowly at room temperature to form an immiscible layer, so they produce **no turbidity at room temperature**.

## 2. Reaction with phosphorus trihalides

Treating an alcohol with phosphorus tribromide ($\ce{PBr3}$) replaces the $-\ce{OH}$ group with bromine to yield the corresponding alkyl bromide (covered in detail in Unit 6):

$$
\ce{3ROH + PBr3 -> 3R-Br + H3PO3}
$$

This route converts alcohols cleanly into bromoalkanes without requiring strongly acidic heating conditions.

## 3. Acid-catalysed dehydration to alkenes

Heating an alcohol with a protic acid such as concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ or phosphoric acid ($\ce{H3PO4}$), or passing it over catalysts like anhydrous zinc chloride or alumina ($\ce{Al2O3}$), removes a molecule of water across adjacent carbons to form an alkene:

$$
\ce{-CH(R)-CH(OH)- ->[\ce{H+}][\text{Heat}] -C(R)=CH- + H2O}
$$

Notice how dramatically the required acid concentration and temperature drop as the alcohol moves from primary to secondary to tertiary:

- **Primary ($1^\circ$) alcohol (ethanol):** requires concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ at $443\text{ K}$:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2OH ->[\text{conc. }\ce{H2SO4}][443\text{ K}] CH2=CH2 + H2O}
$$

- **Secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohol (propan-2-ol):** dehydrates under milder conditions with $85\%\ \ce{H3PO4}$ at $440\text{ K}$:

$$
\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 ->[85\%\ \ce{H3PO4}][440\text{ K}] CH3-CH=CH2 + H2O}
$$

- **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohol (2-methylpropan-2-ol):** dehydrates readily with dilute $20\%\ \ce{H3PO4}$ at just $358\text{ K}$:

$$
\ce{(CH3)3COH ->[20\%\ \ce{H3PO4}][358\text{ K}] CH2=C(CH3)CH3 + H2O}
$$

Thus, the relative ease of dehydration of alcohols follows the order:

$$
\text{Tertiary} > \text{Secondary} > \text{Primary}
$$

### Mechanism of ethanol dehydration

The acid-catalysed dehydration of ethanol proceeds through three reversible steps via a carbocation intermediate.

<Scrolly animation="ethanol-dehydration-mechanism">
  <Step>
    **Step 1 (Fast): Protonation of the alcohol.** An oxygen lone pair on ethanol ($\ce{CH3CH2OH}$) accepts a proton ($\ce{H+}$) from the acid catalyst. A curved arrow moves from the oxygen lone pair to $\ce{H+}$, transforming the poor $-\ce{OH}$ leaving group into the protonated alcohol (ethyl oxonium ion, $\ce{CH3CH2-\overset{+}{O}H2}$).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Step 2 (Slow, rate-determining step): Carbocation formation.** The polarised $\ce{C-O}$ bond breaks heterolytically: the bonding electron pair retreats onto the positively charged oxygen, releasing a neutral $\ce{H2O}$ molecule and leaving behind an ethyl carbocation ($\ce{CH3CH2+}$). Because breaking the $\ce{C-O}$ bond to form an electron-deficient carbocation has the highest energy barrier, this is the slowest step and controls the overall reaction rate.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Step 3 (Fast): Elimination of a proton.** An adjacent $\beta$-carbon loses a proton ($\ce{H+}$), and the $\ce{C-H}$ bonding electrons swing inward to build the $\pi$ bond of ethene ($\ce{CH2=CH2}$). The $\ce{H+}$ ion consumed in Step 1 is regenerated here, confirming that the acid acts purely as a catalyst. Removing ethene gas as it forms shifts the equilibrium toward products.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Why $3^\circ$ alcohols win:** Replacing ethanol with propan-2-ol or 2-methylpropan-2-ol lowers the energy barrier of Step 2 because $3^\circ$ carbocations are far more stable than $2^\circ$, which in turn outrank $1^\circ$ carbocations. That stability explains why $20\%\ \ce{H3PO4}$ at $358\text{ K}$ suffices for 2-methylpropan-2-ol, whereas ethanol demands concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ at $443\text{ K}$.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## 4. Oxidation and dehydrogenation

Oxidation of an alcohol does not break the $\ce{C-O}$ bond; instead, it constructs a $\ce{C=O}$ double bond by cleaving the $\ce{O-H}$ bond and a $\ce{C-H}$ bond on the same carbon (the $\alpha$-carbon). Because one hydrogen leaves from oxygen and one from carbon, this transformation is also a **dehydrogenation** (loss of $\ce{H2}$).

$$
\ce{H-\overset{|}{C}-O-H -> >C=O}
$$

Whether an oxidation stops at an aldehyde, advances to a carboxylic acid, yields a ketone, or fails altogether depends on two factors: the **class of the alcohol** ($1^\circ$, $2^\circ$, or $3^\circ$) and the **strength of the oxidising agent**.

### Primary alcohols: stopping at the aldehyde or going to the acid

A primary alcohol ($\ce{RCH2OH}$) carries two $\alpha$-hydrogens, so it can oxidise in two stages: first to an aldehyde ($\ce{RCHO}$), and then further to a carboxylic acid ($\ce{RCOOH}$).

- **Strong oxidising agents** such as acidified potassium permanganate ($\ce{KMnO4}$) push the oxidation straight through to the **carboxylic acid**.
- **Anhydrous $\ce{CrO3}$** (chromic anhydride in an anhydrous medium) stops the oxidation at the **aldehyde**:

$$
\ce{RCH2OH ->[\ce{CrO3}] RCHO}
$$

- **Pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC)**, a complex of $\ce{CrO3}$ with pyridine and $\ce{HCl}$, is an even better reagent for isolating aldehydes in good yield. Importantly, PCC oxidises the primary alcohol group selectively without attacking carbon-carbon double bonds in the chain:

$$
\ce{CH3-CH=CH-CH2OH ->[\text{PCC}] CH3-CH=CH-CHO}
$$

### Secondary and tertiary alcohols

- **Secondary ($2^\circ$) alcohols** carry one $\alpha$-hydrogen. Chromic anhydride ($\ce{CrO3}$) oxidises them cleanly to **ketones**:

$$
\ce{R-CH(OH)-R' ->[\ce{CrO3}] R-CO-R'}
$$

- **Tertiary ($3^\circ$) alcohols** have **no $\alpha$-hydrogen** bonded to the carbinol carbon, so they resist mild oxidation completely. Under harsh conditions, such as strong oxidising agents ($\ce{KMnO4}$) at elevated temperatures, carbon-carbon bonds break instead, yielding a mixture of carboxylic acids with fewer carbon atoms than the starting alcohol.

### Vapour-phase action over heated copper at 573 K

Passing alcohol vapours over heated copper at $573\text{ K}$ reveals a sharp split between dehydrogenation and dehydration:

- **Primary alcohol:** dehydrogenation yields an **aldehyde**:

$$
\ce{RCH2OH ->[\ce{Cu}][573\text{ K}] RCHO}
$$

- **Secondary alcohol:** dehydrogenation yields a **ketone**:

$$
\ce{R-CH(OH)-R' ->[\ce{Cu}][573\text{ K}] R-CO-R'}
$$

- **Tertiary alcohol:** lacking an $\alpha$-hydrogen for dehydrogenation, 2-methylpropan-2-ol undergoes **dehydration** instead to give an alkene (2-methylpropene):

$$
\ce{(CH3)3COH ->[\ce{Cu}][573\text{ K}] CH3-C(=CH2)-CH3}
$$

<Scrolly animation="alcohol-oxidation-pathways">
  <Step>
    **The $\alpha$-hydrogen rule:** Zoom in on the carbinol carbon. Oxidation converts $-\ce{CH(OH)}-$ into $-\ce{C=O}$ by breaking both the $\ce{O-H}$ bond and one $\alpha$-$\ce{C-H}$ bond. Without at least one hydrogen directly on the carbon holding $-\ce{OH}$, mild oxidation cannot form a carbonyl group.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Primary alcohol branching:** Start with a primary alcohol, which holds two $\alpha$-hydrogens. Acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ drives it past the aldehyde stage straight to a carboxylic acid. Switching to anhydrous $\ce{CrO3}$ or PCC halts the sequence at the aldehyde, and PCC leaves the $\ce{C=C}$ double bond of $\ce{CH3-CH=CH-CH2OH}$ untouched.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Secondary vs tertiary:** A secondary alcohol has one $\alpha$-hydrogen, so $\ce{CrO3}$ converts it smoothly to a ketone and stops. A tertiary alcohol has zero $\alpha$-hydrogens: mild reagents leave it unchanged, while hot $\ce{KMnO4}$ shatters $\ce{C-C}$ bonds into shorter carboxylic acids.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Heated copper at $573\text{ K}$:** Pass vapours across hot copper at $573\text{ K}$. Primary and secondary alcohols shed $\ce{H2}$ (dehydrogenation) to become aldehydes and ketones. Tertiary 2-methylpropan-2-ol cannot shed $\ce{H2}$, so it sheds $\ce{H2O}$ (dehydration) across a $\ce{C-C}$ bond to form 2-methylpropene.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

### Biological oxidation and methanol poisoning

Inside the human body, enzymes oxidise ethanol and methanol along the same sequence: alcohol to aldehyde, then aldehyde to carboxylic acid. If someone accidentally drinks denatured alcohol (ethanol mixed with methanol), methanol is oxidised first to **methanal** ($\ce{HCHO}$) and then to **methanoic acid** ($\ce{HCOOH}$), which damages the optic nerve and can cause blindness or death.

The emergency treatment uses competitive enzyme swamping: physicians administer intravenous infusions of diluted ethanol. Because ethanol floods the enzyme responsible for oxidising the aldehyde ($\ce{HCHO}$) to the acid, further conversion of methanal is slowed, giving the kidneys time to excrete the unchanged methanol.

<KeyIdeas>
- In the **Lucas test** ($\text{conc. }\ce{HCl} + \text{anhyd. }\ce{ZnCl2}$), alcohols dissolve while alkyl halides cause turbidity: $3^\circ$ alcohols turn turbid immediately, whereas $1^\circ$ alcohols show no turbidity at room temperature.
- Acid-catalysed dehydration follows the ease order $\text{Tertiary} > \text{Secondary} > \text{Primary}$, matching carbocation stability: ethanol ($\text{conc. }\ce{H2SO4}, 443\text{ K}$), propan-2-ol ($85\%\ \ce{H3PO4}, 440\text{ K}$), and 2-methylpropan-2-ol ($20\%\ \ce{H3PO4}, 358\text{ K}$).
- Ethanol dehydration proceeds in three steps: fast protonation to the ethyl oxonium ion, slow rate-determining $\ce{C-O}$ cleavage to the ethyl carbocation, and fast $\beta$-proton loss to give ethene while regenerating $\ce{H+}$.
- Alcohol oxidation breaks the $\ce{O-H}$ and $\alpha$-$\ce{C-H}$ bonds to form $\ce{C=O}$: acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ converts $1^\circ$ alcohols directly to carboxylic acids, whereas anhydrous $\ce{CrO3}$ and $\ce{PCC}$ stop at aldehydes (with $\ce{PCC}$ preserving $\ce{C=C}$ double bonds), and $\ce{CrO3}$ oxidises $2^\circ$ alcohols to ketones.
- Over heated copper at $573\text{ K}$, $1^\circ$ and $2^\circ$ alcohols undergo dehydrogenation to aldehydes and ketones, whereas $3^\circ$ alcohols (with no $\alpha$-hydrogen) undergo dehydration to alkenes.
</KeyIdeas>
