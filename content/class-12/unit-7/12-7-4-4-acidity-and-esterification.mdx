Repeat that test with phenol and it dissolves readily in aqueous alkali. Why can phenol give up a proton so much more easily than ethanol, and how does swapping one ring substituent shift that acidity by orders of magnitude?

## Two ways an alcohol can react

Alcohols are versatile reagents because they can act as either nucleophiles or electrophiles depending on which bond breaks:

- **As nucleophiles (cleavage of the $\ce{O-H}$ bond):** Oxygen uses an unshared electron pair to attack an electron-deficient carbon, and the proton leaves at the end:

$$
\ce{R-O-H + ^+C\bond{-} -> R-\overset{+}{O}(H)-C\bond{-} -> R-O-C\bond{-} + H+}
$$

- **As electrophiles (cleavage of the $\ce{C-O}$ bond):** Neutral $\ce{-OH}$ is a poor leaving group, so an alcohol must first be protonated to form an oxonium ion before a nucleophile displaces water:

$$
\ce{R-CH2-OH + H+ -> R-CH2-\overset{+}{O}H2}
$$

$$
\ce{Br- + CH2(R)-\overset{+}{O}H2 -> Br-CH2-R + H2O}
$$

On this basis, reactions of alcohols and phenols fall into two classes: those involving $\ce{O-H}$ bond cleavage (acidity and esterification, covered here) and those involving $\ce{C-O}$ bond cleavage (covered in the next section for alcohols; phenols undergo $\ce{C-O}$ cleavage only with zinc).

## Proving acidity: reactions with active metals and alkali

Both alcohols and phenols react with active metals such as sodium ($\ce{Na}$), potassium ($\ce{K}$), and aluminium ($\ce{Al}$) to liberate hydrogen gas and form metal alkoxides or phenoxides:

$$
\ce{2R-O-H + 2Na -> 2R-O-Na + H2}
$$

$$
\ce{6(CH3)3C-OH + 2Al -> 2((CH3)3C-O)3Al + 3H2}
$$

$$
\ce{2C6H5-OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5-ONa + H2}
$$

In these equations, *tert*-butyl alcohol reacts with aluminium to yield aluminium *tert*-butoxide, and phenol reacts with sodium to yield sodium phenoxide. Phenols go a step further: unlike alcohols, they also react with aqueous sodium hydroxide to form sodium phenoxide and water:

$$
\ce{C6H5-OH + NaOH -> C6H5-ONa + H2O}
$$

These reactions establish that alcohols and phenols behave as Brønsted acids, donating a proton to a stronger base ($\ce{B:}$):

$$
\ce{B: + H-O-R -> B-H + :O^--R}
$$

Here $\ce{B-H}$ is the conjugate acid of the base and $\ce{R-O^-}$ is the conjugate base of the alcohol.

## Acidity of alcohols: inductive effects and comparison with water

The acidic character of an alcohol stems from the polar $\ce{O-H}$ bond. Alkyl groups such as $\ce{-CH3}$ and $\ce{-C2H5}$ are electron-releasing ($+I$ inductive effect). As more alkyl groups attach to the carbon bearing $\ce{-OH}$, they push greater electron density toward oxygen. That extra density lowers the polarity of the $\ce{O-H}$ bond and destabilises the negative charge on the resulting alkoxide ion, decreasing acid strength:

$$
\text{Primary } (\ce{R-CH2OH}) > \text{Secondary } (\ce{R2CHOH}) \gg \text{Tertiary } (\ce{R3COH})
$$

Even primary alcohols are weaker acids than water. Adding water to a metal alkoxide immediately protonates the alkoxide back to the alcohol:

$$
\ce{R-O^- + H-O-H -> R-O-H + OH-}
$$

Because water donates a proton to $\ce{R-O^-}$, water is the stronger Brønsted acid (better proton donor) than an alcohol. Equally, the alkoxide ion is a stronger proton acceptor than hydroxide ion, making sodium ethoxide a stronger base than sodium hydroxide.

Alcohols also act as Brønsted bases because the unshared electron pairs on oxygen can accept a proton from strong acids.

## Why phenols are far stronger acids than alcohols

Phenols react with aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$ while alcohols do not, showing the Brønsted acid strength order:

$$
\text{Phenols} > \text{Water} > \text{Alcohols}
$$

Two structural features explain why phenol is vastly more acidic than an alcohol:

1. **Higher electronegativity of the $sp^2$ ring carbon:** In phenol, the $\ce{-OH}$ group bonds directly to an $sp^2$ hybridised carbon of the benzene ring rather than an $sp^3$ alkyl carbon. An $sp^2$ carbon has greater $s$ character and higher electronegativity, so it withdraws electron density from oxygen, increases the polarity of the $\ce{O-H}$ bond, and facilitates proton loss. (Delocalisation of an oxygen lone pair into the ring in neutral phenol also gives oxygen partial positive character.)
2. **Resonance stabilisation of the phenoxide ion:** When an alcohol ionises ($\ce{R-O-H <=> R-O^- + H+}$), the negative charge in $\ce{R-O^-}$ stays localised on a single oxygen atom. When phenol ionises ($\ce{C6H5-OH <=> C6H5-O^- + H+}$), the phenoxide ion delocalises its negative charge onto the *ortho* and *para* carbons of the benzene ring across five resonance structures (I to V). Neutral phenol also has five resonance contributors, but its ring-delocalised structures create charge separation ($+$ on oxygen and $-$ on carbon), whereas the phenoxide ion disperses an already existing negative charge without creating charge separation. Because phenoxide is stabilised much more strongly than neutral phenol, ionisation is strongly favoured.

## Substituted phenols and $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ values

Recall that a **larger $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ indicates a weaker acid**, while a **smaller $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ indicates a stronger acid**. Substituents on the phenol ring shift $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ in predictable ways:

- **Electron-withdrawing groups** such as $\ce{-NO2}$ stabilise the phenoxide ion and increase acid strength. The boost is strongest when $\ce{-NO2}$ occupies an **ortho** or **para** position, because resonance places negative charge directly on those carbons. At the **meta** position, the nitro group still withdraws electrons inductively, so *m*-nitrophenol ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 8.3$) is more acidic than phenol ($10.0$), though weaker than *o*-nitrophenol ($7.2$) and *p*-nitrophenol ($7.1$).
- **Electron-releasing groups** such as $\ce{-CH3}$ destabilise the phenoxide ion and reduce acid strength, making all three cresols slightly weaker acids than unsubstituted phenol.

| Compound | Formula | $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :---: |
| *o*-Nitrophenol | $\ce{o-O2N-C6H4-OH}$ | 7.2 |
| *m*-Nitrophenol | $\ce{m-O2N-C6H4-OH}$ | 8.3 |
| *p*-Nitrophenol | $\ce{p-O2N-C6H4-OH}$ | 7.1 |
| Phenol | $\ce{C6H5-OH}$ | 10.0 |
| *o*-Cresol | $\ce{o-CH3-C6H4-OH}$ | 10.2 |
| *m*-Cresol | $\ce{m-CH3C6H4-OH}$ | 10.1 |
| *p*-Cresol | $\ce{p-CH3-C6H4-OH}$ | 10.2 |
| Ethanol | $\ce{C2H5OH}$ | 15.9 |

Comparing phenol ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 10.0$) with ethanol ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 15.9$) shows a gap of $5.9$ units on a logarithmic scale, meaning phenol is roughly a million times ($10^{5.9} \approx 10^6$) more acidic than ethanol.

## Esterification and acetylation

Alcohols and phenols react with **carboxylic acids**, **acid anhydrides**, and **acid chlorides** by cleaving the $\ce{O-H}$ bond to form **esters**:

$$
\ce{Ar/R-OH + R'-COOH <=>[H+] Ar/R-OCOR' + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{Ar/R-OH + (R'CO)2O <=>[H+] Ar/R-OCOR' + R'COOH}
$$

$$
\ce{R/Ar-OH + R'COCl ->[\text{Pyridine}] R/Ar-OCOR' + HCl}
$$

Two practical strategies shift these reactions toward high ester yields:

- **Carboxylic acids and acid anhydrides:** A small amount of concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ acts as the acid catalyst ($\ce{H+}$). Because the reaction is reversible, water formed in the carboxylic acid route is removed continuously as it forms to drive equilibrium toward the ester.
- **Acid chlorides:** The reaction is carried out in the presence of **pyridine**, a base that neutralises $\ce{HCl}$ as it forms and pulls the equilibrium to the right.

Introducing an **acetyl group** ($\ce{CH3CO-}$) into an alcohol or phenol is called **acetylation**. Acetylating **salicylic acid** (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) with **acetic anhydride** in the presence of $\ce{H+}$ converts its phenolic $\ce{-OH}$ group into an ester, producing **acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin)** alongside acetic acid:

$$
\ce{HO-C6H4-COOH + (CH3CO)2O ->[H+] CH3COO-C6H4-COOH + CH3COOH}
$$

Aspirin is widely used in medicine for its analgesic (pain-relieving), anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic (fever-reducing) properties.

  </Step>

<KeyIdeas>
- Alcohols act as nucleophiles when the $\ce{O-H}$ bond breaks and as electrophiles (after protonation) when the $\ce{C-O}$ bond breaks.
- Active metals ($\ce{Na}$, $\ce{K}$, $\ce{Al}$) convert both alcohols and phenols into alkoxides/phenoxides and $\ce{H2}$, whereas aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$ converts only phenols into sodium phenoxide; Brønsted acid strength follows $\text{Phenols} > \text{Water} > \text{Alcohols}$, and within alcohols follows $\text{Primary} > \text{Secondary} \gg \text{Tertiary}$.
- Phenol ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 10.0$) is roughly a million times more acidic than ethanol ($\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} = 15.9$) because its $sp^2$ carbon withdraws electron density and the phenoxide ion delocalises negative charge onto oxygen and the *ortho* and *para* carbons without charge separation.
- Electron-withdrawing groups such as $\ce{-NO2}$ lower $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ (most strongly at *ortho* and *para*, giving $7.2$ and $7.1$ vs $8.3$ for *meta*), while electron-releasing alkyl groups in cresols raise $\mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a}$ slightly ($10.1$ to $10.2$).
- Alcohols and phenols form esters with carboxylic acids or acid anhydrides (catalysed by conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$, removing $\ce{H2O}$ to shift equilibrium right) and with acid chlorides (using pyridine to neutralise $\ce{HCl}$); acetylating salicylic acid with $\ce{(CH3CO)2O}$ / $\ce{H+}$ yields acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin).
</KeyIdeas>
