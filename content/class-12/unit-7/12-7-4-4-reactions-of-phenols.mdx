<Hook>
Plain benzene ignores liquid bromine until a Lewis acid such as $\ce{FeBr3}$ polarises the halogen. Pour bromine water into an aqueous solution of phenol at room temperature, however, and a dense white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol forms almost at once. What gives the phenolic ring such extraordinary reactivity, and how can a chemist dial that reactivity up or down to isolate a single isomer or even capture carbon dioxide?
</Hook>

## Why the phenolic ring seeks electrophiles

In phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$), the oxygen atom of the $-\ce{OH}$ group carries two lone pairs adjacent to the $\pi$-electron system of the benzene ring. Through resonance (as seen when explaining phenol's acidity earlier in Section 7.4.4), electron density shifts from oxygen into the ring and concentrates specifically at the *ortho* (C-2 and C-6) and *para* (C-4) carbons.

Two consequences follow immediately for electrophilic aromatic substitution (Class 12, Part II, Unit 7, Section 7.4.4):

1. **Strong ring activation:** The benzene ring becomes far more electron-rich than unsubstituted benzene, so it reacts rapidly under mild conditions and often needs no Lewis acid catalyst.
2. **Ortho and para direction:** Incoming electrophiles attack the electron-rich *ortho* and *para* positions rather than the *meta* carbons.

## Nitration: temperature, concentration, and hydrogen bonding

Nitrating phenol reveals how reagent strength changes the degree of substitution, and how hydrogen bonding governs physical separation.

### Mononitration with dilute nitric acid

Treating phenol with dilute nitric acid ($\ce{HNO3}$) at low temperature ($298\text{ K}$) produces a mixture of *o*-nitrophenol and *p*-nitrophenol:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH ->[\text{Dilute HNO3},\ 298\text{ K}] o\text{-O2NC6H4OH} + p\text{-O2NC6H4OH}}
$$

The two isomers are separated cleanly by **steam distillation**:

- ***o*-Nitrophenol is steam volatile:** Because the $-\ce{OH}$ and $-\ce{NO2}$ groups sit on adjacent carbons, the phenolic hydrogen forms an **intramolecular hydrogen bond** with an oxygen atom of the neighbouring nitro group. Each molecule remains self-contained with weak attractions to other molecules, so it evaporates readily with steam.
- ***p*-Nitrophenol is less volatile:** With $-\ce{OH}$ and $-\ce{NO2}$ on opposite sides of the ring, molecules cannot hydrogen-bond internally. Instead, they link together through **intermolecular hydrogen bonding**, associating into larger clusters that remain behind in the distillation flask.

### Trinitration to picric acid

With concentrated nitric acid ($\ce{HNO3}$), substitution occurs at both *ortho* positions and the *para* position to give **2,4,6-trinitrophenol**, commonly known as **picric acid**:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH ->[\text{Conc. HNO3}] C6H2(NO2)3OH}
$$

Because three strongly electron-withdrawing $-\ce{NO2}$ groups pull electron density out of the ring and stabilise the conjugate base, 2,4,6-trinitrophenol is a strong acid that readily releases $\ce{H+}$.

Direct nitration of phenol with concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ gives a poor yield because concentrated nitric acid also oxidises part of the activated ring. To avoid this loss, picric acid is prepared today in two steps: phenol is first treated with concentrated sulphuric acid ($\ce{H2SO4}$) to form **phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid**, and this intermediate is then treated with concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ to obtain 2,4,6-trinitrophenol cleanly.

## Halogenation: solvent polarity decides mono- or trisubstitution

Reacting phenol with bromine ($\ce{Br2}$) illustrates both the extreme activation of the ring and the role of the solvent.

### Monobromination in low-polarity solvents

When phenol reacts with $\ce{Br2}$ in a solvent of low polarity such as carbon disulphide ($\ce{CS2}$) or chloroform ($\ce{CHCl3}$) at $273\text{ K}$, monobromophenols form:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH ->[\text{Br2 in CS2},\ 273\text{ K}] o\text{-BrC6H4OH (minor)} + p\text{-BrC6H4OH (major)}}
$$

Unlike benzene, which requires a Lewis acid such as $\ce{FeBr3}$ to polarise $\ce{Br2}$, phenol polarises the bromine molecule on its own because of the strongly activating $-\ce{OH}$ group.

### Trisubstitution in bromine water

When phenol is treated with aqueous **bromine water**, all three activated positions react at once, precipitating **2,4,6-tribromophenol** as a **white solid**:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3OH(s) + 3HBr}
$$

In polar aqueous medium, partial ionisation of phenol to the even more activating phenoxide ion ($\ce{C6H5O-}$) drives rapid substitution at positions 2, 4, and 6.

When a $\ce{Br2}$ molecule approaches, the electron-rich ring polarises the $\ce{Br-Br}$ bond directly without needing an $\ce{FeBr3}$ catalyst.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Dilute nitration at 298 K:** Dilute $\ce{HNO3}$ at $298\text{ K}$ delivers a nitronium electrophile to the activated positions, yielding a mixture of *o*-nitrophenol and *p*-nitrophenol.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Separating isomers by steam distillation:** Inside *o*-nitrophenol, a dashed intramolecular hydrogen bond closes a six-membered loop between the phenolic $-\ce{OH}$ and the adjacent $-\ce{NO2}$ oxygen, so individual molecules rise freely with steam. Meanwhile, *p*-nitrophenol molecules link end-to-end through intermolecular hydrogen bonds, keeping them associated and far less volatile.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Concentrated nitration and the sulphonation route:** Direct reaction with concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ produces 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid) in poor yield because of ring oxidation. Switching to the modern two-step route converts phenol first with concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ into phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid, and then with concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ into 2,4,6-trinitrophenol in high yield.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Solvent control in bromination:** In low-polarity $\ce{CS2}$ or $\ce{CHCl3}$ at $273\text{ K}$, $\ce{Br2}$ gives *p*-bromophenol as the major product alongside minor *o*-bromophenol. In aqueous bromine water, rapid trisubstitution consumes $3\ce{Br2}$ and drops 2,4,6-tribromophenol out of solution as a white precipitate with $3\ce{HBr}$.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Turning phenol into phenoxide: Kolbe's reaction

Treating phenol with sodium hydroxide ($\ce{NaOH}$) removes the phenolic proton and generates the **phenoxide ion** ($\ce{C6H5O-}$ in sodium phenoxide, $\ce{C6H5ONa}$). Carrying a full negative charge on oxygen makes the phenoxide ring even more electron-rich and reactive toward electrophiles than neutral phenol.

In **Kolbe's reaction**, this boosted reactivity enables electrophilic substitution even with carbon dioxide ($\ce{CO2}$), a weak electrophile. Subsequent acidification ($\ce{H+}$) yields **2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid)** as the major product:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH ->[\text{NaOH}] C6H5ONa ->[\text{(i) CO2,\ (ii) H+}] o\text{-HOC6H4COOH}}
$$

## Ortho formylation: Reimer-Tiemann reaction

In the **Reimer-Tiemann reaction**, treating phenol with chloroform ($\ce{CHCl3}$) in the presence of aqueous sodium hydroxide ($\ce{aq. NaOH}$) introduces an aldehyde group ($-\ce{CHO}$) at the *ortho* position.

The reaction proceeds through a distinct substituted benzal chloride intermediate before releasing **salicylaldehyde** (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde):

1. Phenol reacts with $\ce{CHCl3}$ and aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$ to form the sodium phenoxide intermediate carrying a $-\ce{CHCl2}$ group at the *ortho* carbon (substituted benzal chloride).
2. Alkaline hydrolysis with $\ce{NaOH}$ converts the $-\ce{CHCl2}$ group into an *ortho* $-\ce{CHO}$ group while the phenolic group remains as $-\ce{O-Na+}$.
3. Acidification with $\ce{H+}$ protonates the phenoxide oxygen to give **salicylaldehyde**:

$$
\ce{C6H5OH ->[\text{CHCl3 + aq. NaOH}] o\text{-(CHCl2)C6H4ONa} ->[\text{NaOH}] o\text{-(CHO)C6H4ONa} ->[\text{H+}] o\text{-HOC6H4CHO}}
$$

## Reduction with zinc dust and oxidation with chromic acid

Beyond ring substitution, phenol undergoes clean deoxygenation on heating with zinc and oxidative ring conversion with chromic acid or air.

### Reaction with zinc dust

Heating phenol with **zinc dust** strips the oxygen atom from the ring, reducing phenol to **benzene** and forming zinc oxide ($\ce{ZnO}$):

$$
\ce{C6H5OH + Zn ->[\Delta] C6H6 + ZnO}
$$

### Oxidation to benzoquinone

Oxidising phenol with **chromic acid** (sodium dichromate, $\ce{Na2Cr2O7}$, in sulphuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$) converts the aromatic ring into a conjugated diketone called **benzoquinone** (*p*-benzoquinone):

$$
\ce{C6H5OH ->[\text{Na2Cr2O7},\ \text{H2SO4}] O=C6H4=O}
$$

Even on simple exposure to air, phenols slowly oxidise into dark-coloured mixtures containing quinones.

<Scrolly animation="phenol-named-redox">
  <Step>
    **Super-activating the ring as phenoxide:** Phenol meets $\ce{NaOH}$ and loses its acidic proton to become sodium phenoxide ($\ce{C6H5O-Na+}$). The full negative charge pushes even stronger electron density into the *ortho* and *para* carbons than neutral $-\ce{OH}$.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Kolbe's reaction captures $\ce{CO2}$:** The electron-rich phenoxide ring attacks carbon dioxide ($\ce{CO2}$), installing a carboxylate group at the *ortho* position. Adding $\ce{H+}$ protonates the salt to yield 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid) as the main product.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Reimer-Tiemann intermediate:** Phenol reacts with $\ce{CHCl3}$ and aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$. A $-\ce{CHCl2}$ group attaches next to $-\ce{O-Na+}$ on the ring, forming the *ortho*-substituted benzal chloride intermediate.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Hydrolysis to salicylaldehyde:** Aqueous $\ce{NaOH}$ hydrolyses the $-\ce{CHCl2}$ group into $-\ce{CHO}$, and a final $\ce{H+}$ wash restores the phenolic $-\ce{OH}$ to release salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Reduction versus oxidation:** Heating phenol with $\ce{Zn}$ dust pulls out the oxygen atom as $\ce{ZnO}$, leaving plain benzene ($\ce{C6H6}$). By contrast, chromic acid ($\ce{Na2Cr2O7}$ + $\ce{H2SO4}$) strips hydrogens and converts the ring into the conjugated diketone *p*-benzoquinone.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- The $-\ce{OH}$ group strongly activates the benzene ring and directs electrophiles to the *ortho* and *para* carbons, enabling bromination without a Lewis acid such as $\ce{FeBr3}$.
- Dilute $\ce{HNO3}$ at $298\text{ K}$ gives *o*-nitrophenol (steam volatile due to intramolecular H-bonding) and *p*-nitrophenol (less volatile due to intermolecular H-bonding); concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ (preferably after $\ce{H2SO4}$ conversion to phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid) yields 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid).
- Solvent polarity controls bromination: $\ce{Br2}$ in $\ce{CS2}$ or $\ce{CHCl3}$ at $273\text{ K}$ yields *p*-bromophenol (major) and *o*-bromophenol (minor), whereas aqueous bromine water precipitates 2,4,6-tribromophenol as a white solid.
- Converting phenol to the more reactive phenoxide ion lets weak electrophiles react: Kolbe's reaction uses $\ce{CO2}$ then $\ce{H+}$ to form 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid), and the Reimer-Tiemann reaction uses $\ce{CHCl3}$ + $\ce{aq. NaOH}$ (via a substituted benzal chloride intermediate, alkaline hydrolysis, and $\ce{H+}$) to form salicylaldehyde.
- Heating with $\ce{Zn}$ dust reduces phenol to benzene ($\ce{C6H6} + \ce{ZnO}$), while oxidation with chromic acid ($\ce{Na2Cr2O7 + H2SO4}$) produces the conjugated diketone benzoquinone.
</KeyIdeas>
