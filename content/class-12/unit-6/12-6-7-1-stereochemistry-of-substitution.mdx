<Hook>
A left shoe held up to a mirror looks like a right shoe, yet the two never pair up on the same foot. Some molecules have that same handedness at their centre. So can a molecule be left-handed or right-handed, and does its handedness survive when one group is swapped for another?
</Hook>

## Light that a molecule can turn

Ordinary light vibrates in every direction at once. Pass it through a polariser and only one plane of vibration gets through, which is plane-polarised light. An instrument called a polarimeter measures what a sample does to that plane. Put a solution of certain compounds in the tube and the plane tilts. If it tilts clockwise, the compound is dextrorotatory, the d-form, marked with a plus sign. If it tilts anticlockwise, it is laevorotatory, the l-form, marked with a minus sign. Compounds that do this are optically active, and their (+) and (−) forms are optical isomers.

## A carbon with four different arms

Look at the carbon that bears the halogen in 2-chloropentane. It is joined to a chlorine, a hydrogen, a methyl group and a propyl group, four different arms pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron. Build the mirror image and try to turn one onto the other: no rotation lines up each arm with its partner. A carbon like this is called a stereocentre (or asymmetric carbon). A molecule that cannot be superimposed on its mirror image is chiral, and the two mirror-image forms are enantiomers.

Now compare 3-chloropentane. Its chlorinated carbon carries two identical ethyl arms, so the mirror image lines up with the original after a turn. This molecule is achiral and has no stereocentre. The test is simple: if two arms match, the molecule is achiral.

Enantiomers have the same melting point, boiling point and refractive index. They differ in one property only: they turn the plane of polarised light by the same angle, in opposite directions. Mix the two in equal amounts and the rotations cancel, so the mixture is optically inactive. That is a racemic mixture, written with a (±) or dl prefix, as in (±)-butan-2-ol. Turning one enantiomer into such a 50:50 mixture is racemisation.

## Three things a reaction can do at the stereocentre

When a bond to the stereocentre itself breaks, the reaction has three possible outcomes. The arrangement can be kept (retention). It can be flipped (inversion). Or the two arrangements can appear in equal amounts (racemisation). Which one you get depends on the mechanism, not on the reactant's sign of rotation.

Retention is the easiest case. Take 2-methylbutan-1-ol, which has a stereocentre one carbon away from the carbon that reacts. Heat it with concentrated $\ce{HCl}$ and the OH on the $\ce{CH2}$ group is replaced by Cl. No bond to the stereocentre breaks, so its arrangement is preserved. The rotation is a separate measurement, and it can come out with the opposite sign even though the arrangement has not moved.

## Why an SN2 reaction flips the arrangement

Consider 2-bromooctane, the (−) form, reacting with hydroxide:

$$
\ce{CH3(CH2)5CH(Br)CH3 + OH- -> CH3(CH2)5CH(OH)CH3 + Br-}
$$

The hydroxide arrives from the side opposite the bromine. In the transition state the carbon is partly bonded to both the incoming and the leaving group, and its other three groups lie in one plane. When the bromine leaves, those three groups flip through that plane, like an umbrella turning inside out in a gust of wind. The product is the (+)-octan-2-ol, with the OH on the far side of where the Br was. Every optically active substrate that goes through this path ends up inverted.

## Why an SN1 reaction scrambles it

An SN1 reaction starts with the halide leaving on its own. The carbon left behind is a carbocation, and it is sp² hybridised and flat. A flat carbon has no preferred face, so the nucleophile can attack from above or below with about equal chance. Optically active 2-bromobutane gives an equal mix of the two arrangements of butan-2-ol, written (±)-butan-2-ol, which is optically inactive. The same thing happens to 1-bromo-1-phenylethane: its benzylic carbocation is stabilised by the ring, so the ionisation is easy, and the product 1-phenylethanol is racemic.

<KeyIdeas>
- A stereocentre is a carbon with four different groups; a molecule that cannot be superimposed on its mirror image is chiral and is optically active, so its two enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light by the same angle in opposite directions.
- A racemic mixture of equal amounts of two enantiomers is optically inactive, written (±); converting one enantiomer into that mixture is racemisation.
- Retention keeps the arrangement when no bond to the stereocentre breaks; the sign of rotation can still change, so sign and arrangement are separate things.
- $\mathrm{S_N2}$ inverts the arrangement, because the nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the leaving group, through a transition state with the other three groups flattened into one plane.
- $\mathrm{S_N1}$ gives racemisation, because the carbocation is sp² and flat, and the nucleophile can attack from either face.
</KeyIdeas>
