## Two atoms that share unevenly

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, but the sharing is rarely fair. Each atom pulls on that pair with a strength called its electronegativity. The halogens are among the most electronegative elements in the periodic table, and carbon is much less so. In $\ce{CH3-Cl}$ the shared pair spends more of its time near chlorine. Chlorine ends up slightly negative, written $\delta^-$, and carbon slightly positive, written $\delta^+$.

These charges are partial. No electron has jumped from carbon to chlorine. The pair is simply pulled unevenly, and that unevenness is one of the most useful facts about every haloalkane.

The halogen is not a fixed size, though. Moving down the group, each halogen is bigger than the one above it, and the bond it forms with carbon changes with it. The scrolly below follows that change one step at a time.

<Scrolly animation="carbon-halogen-bond">
  <Step>
    **A lopsided pair:** Start with a methyl group bonded to chlorine. The shared electron pair is drawn as a cloud pulled toward the chlorine end. Chlorine carries a small negative charge, $\delta^-$, and carbon, which has given up some of its share, carries a small positive charge, $\delta^+$. Nothing has been handed over from one atom to the other. The charges are partial because the sharing is uneven.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Stepping down the group:** Now swap the halogen and keep the carbon and its three hydrogens fixed. Fluorine is the smallest halogen and iodine the largest, and the $\ce{C-X}$ bond stretches to scale as you go: $139\ \text{pm}$ for $\ce{CH3-F}$, $178\ \text{pm}$ for $\ce{CH3-Cl}$, $193\ \text{pm}$ for $\ce{CH3-Br}$ and $214\ \text{pm}$ for $\ce{CH3-I}$. The methyl part does not change, so the whole stretch comes from the larger halogen reaching further out.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A longer bond, a weaker bond:** Bond enthalpy is the energy needed to break one mole of a bond. The bars shrink as the bond lengthens: $452\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ for $\ce{CH3-F}$, $351$ for $\ce{CH3-Cl}$, $293$ for $\ce{CH3-Br}$ and $234$ for $\ce{CH3-I}$. Breaking the C–F bond takes $218\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ more than breaking the C–I bond. The long C–I bond is the weak link, and that is a clue to which haloalkanes react most readily.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Dipoles are not a staircase:** The dipole moment of the whole molecule is given in debye, D. The values are $1.847\ \text{D}$ for $\ce{CH3-F}$, $1.860\ \text{D}$ for $\ce{CH3-Cl}$, $1.830\ \text{D}$ for $\ce{CH3-Br}$ and $1.636\ \text{D}$ for $\ce{CH3-I}$. Fluorine is the most electronegative halogen, yet $\ce{CH3-Cl}$ has the largest dipole. Bond polarity and bond length both feed into the dipole, so do not expect it to climb steadily with electronegativity or with size.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Where the pull leads:** Every idea so far points to one spot. The carbon that carries a partial positive charge is electron-poor, so a reagent with a lone pair is drawn toward it. The later Section on nucleophilic substitution builds the reactions of haloalkanes on this single polar bond.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- The C–X bond is polar because the halogen is more electronegative than carbon: carbon carries $\delta^+$ and the halogen carries $\delta^-$.
- Going down the group from F to I, the halogen gets bigger, so the C–X bond lengthens: $139$, $178$, $193$ and $214\ \text{pm}$ for $\ce{CH3-X}$.
- A longer C–X bond is a weaker one: bond enthalpy falls from $452\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ for C–F to $234\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ for C–I.
- Dipole moments do not follow a simple trend. $\ce{CH3-Cl}$ ($1.860\ \text{D}$) is the largest and $\ce{CH3-I}$ ($1.636\ \text{D}$) the smallest.
- The partly positive carbon is the site that nucleophiles approach, which is the starting point for the reactions in the next Sections.
</KeyIdeas>
