The chloroform bottle in a school lab is brown, and it is filled right up to the cap. That looks strange for a liquid that finds so many uses. Why would anyone keep it that way? The answer lies in something slow and invisible: air and light can change chloroform into a far more dangerous gas, and that is a good way to start thinking about every compound in this section.
</Hook>

## Several halogens on one carbon

Polyhalogen compounds carry more than one halogen atom on their molecules. The simplest route to them starts with methane. Replace its hydrogens with chlorine and you get a family of compounds that share a single carbon atom but do very different jobs in the world. $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ strips paint, $\ce{CHCl3}$ has put patients to sleep, $\ce{CHI3}$ has been an antiseptic, and $\ce{CCl4}$ and its relatives have chilled our fridges and fed our aerosol cans. Each one owes its character to the same few halogen atoms sitting on that carbon.

The uses are only half of the story. The same bonds that make these liquids good solvents also make them hard to clear from the body, hard to break down in nature, and in some cases toxic. In the pages that follow, look at each compound's use first, and then at what it does once it is inside a person, in the air or in the food chain.

## Dichloromethane: a useful solvent with a sharp edge

Open a can of paint stripper and you are likely breathing dichloromethane, $\ce{CH2Cl2}$, also called methylene chloride. It dissolves so many substances that it works as a solvent and lifts paint off surfaces. It also serves as the propellant in aerosol cans, as a process solvent in making drugs, and as a solvent for cleaning and finishing metal.

Dichloromethane mostly harms the nervous system, and the dose decides which symptoms appear. Low levels in air can leave hearing and vision slightly impaired. Higher levels bring dizziness and nausea, plus tingling and numbness in the fingers and toes. On the skin, the liquid causes intense burning and mild redness, and a splash in the eye can burn the cornea. Anyone who handles it should take it seriously.

## Chloroform: from anaesthetic to light-sensitive liquid

Chloroform dissolves fats, alkaloids and iodine, and today most of it goes into making the refrigerant R-22. It was once a general anaesthetic, until safer and less toxic options such as ether took over. Its vapour acts on the central nervous system, so a short exposure can bring dizziness, fatigue and headache. Chronic exposure can damage the kidneys and the liver, where chloroform is converted into phosgene. Some people develop sores when their skin is immersed in it.

Chloroform has a hidden weakness. Air, helped along by light, slowly oxidises it into carbonyl chloride, better known as phosgene, an extremely poisonous gas. That is why the bottles are closed, dark in colour and filled right to the top, which keeps the air out.

$$
\ce{2CHCl3 + O2 ->[\text{air, light}] 2COCl2 + 2HCl}
$$

## Iodoform: an antiseptic that works through iodine

Iodoform, $\ce{CHI3}$ (triiodomethane), once served as an antiseptic, but the molecule is not what kills microbes. The antiseptic work comes from the free iodine it releases. Think of iodoform as a delivery van and iodine as its cargo. Its smell was objectionable, so other formulations that carry iodine have taken its place.

## Carbon tetrachloride and the freons

Tetrachloromethane, $\ce{CCl4}$, is made in very large quantities. Manufacturers use it to make refrigerants and aerosol propellants, to feed the synthesis of chlorofluorocarbons and other chemicals, in pharmaceutical work, and as a general solvent. Until the mid 1960s it was also sold as a cleaning fluid, degreaser, home spot remover and fire extinguisher. Some evidence ties it to liver cancer in humans. Dizziness, light-headedness, nausea and vomiting are the commonest effects, and they can leave nerve cells permanently damaged. In severe cases exposure can quickly lead to stupor, unconsciousness, coma or death. It can also make the heart beat irregularly or stop, and it may irritate the eyes on contact.

Once carbon tetrachloride escapes into the air, it rises to the atmosphere and depletes the ozone layer. A thinner ozone layer is believed to expose people to more ultraviolet rays, which can mean more skin cancer, more eye diseases and disorders, and possibly a disrupted immune system.

Freons are stable and unreactive enough to survive the trip up. They were also popular because they are non-toxic, non-corrosive and easy to liquefy, which suited refrigeration and air conditioning. Freon 12, $\ce{CCl2F2}$, is among the most widely used. It is made from carbon tetrachloride by the Swarts reaction you met earlier in this unit. Even freon used in a fridge usually escapes, drifts up, and passes through the stratosphere unchanged. There it starts radical chain reactions that disturb the ozone balance.

## DDT: an effective insecticide that stays behind

DDT was the first chlorinated organic insecticide, and it was prepared as early as 1873. Its power against insects went unnoticed until 1939, when Paul Müller of Geigy Pharmaceuticals in Switzerland found it effective. That discovery won him the Nobel Prize in Medicine and Physiology in 1948. Its use exploded worldwide after the Second World War, mostly against the mosquito that spreads malaria and the lice that carry typhus.

Trouble followed in the late 1940s. Many insect species became resistant to DDT, and fish proved highly sensitive to it. The deeper problem is chemical: DDT is stable and dissolves in fat. Animals break it down slowly, so it is laid down and stored in their fatty tissues. With a steady intake, the amount inside the animal climbs over time. The United States banned DDT in 1973, though it is still used in some other parts of the world.

<KeyIdeas>
- Polyhalogen compounds put several halogen atoms on one carbon, and the number and kind of halogen decide what the compound is used for and how it behaves.
- Dichloromethane, chloroform, carbon tetrachloride and the freons have real industrial uses, and their health and environmental effects are part of the same story.
- Chloroform is kept dark, closed and full because air and light turn it into phosgene, a very poisonous gas.
- Iodoform is an antiseptic because it releases free iodine, not because of the molecule itself.
- Freons are stable and unreactive enough to reach the stratosphere, where they start radical chain reactions that upset the ozone balance. DDT is stable and fat-soluble, so it is stored in animal fat and builds up over time.
</KeyIdeas>
