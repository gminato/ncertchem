<Hook>
The chloroform bottle in a school lab is brown, and it is filled right up to the cap. That looks strange for a liquid that finds so many uses. Why would anyone keep it that way? The answer lies in something slow and invisible: air and light can change chloroform into a far more dangerous gas, and that is a good way to start thinking about every compound in this section.
</Hook>

## Several halogens on one carbon

Polyhalogen compounds carry more than one halogen atom on their molecules. The simplest route to them starts with methane. Replace its hydrogens with chlorine and you get a family of compounds that share a single carbon atom but do very different jobs in the world. $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ strips paint, $\ce{CHCl3}$ has put patients to sleep, $\ce{CHI3}$ has been an antiseptic, and $\ce{CCl4}$ and its relatives have chilled our fridges and fed our aerosol cans. Each one owes its character to the same few halogen atoms sitting on that carbon.

The uses are only half of the story. The same bonds that make these liquids good solvents also make them hard to clear from the body, hard to break down in nature, and in some cases toxic. In the pages that follow, look at each compound's use first, and then at what it does once it is inside a person, in the air or in the food chain.

## Dichloromethane: a useful solvent with a sharp edge

Dichloromethane, $\ce{CH2Cl2}$, is also called methylene chloride. It dissolves a wide range of substances, which explains its work as a solvent, a paint remover, a propellant in aerosol cans, and a process solvent in the manufacture of drugs. It is also used to clean and finish metals.

The harm it does is mostly to the nervous system. At low levels in air it can leave hearing and vision slightly impaired. At higher levels it causes dizziness, nausea, and a tingling, numb feeling in the fingers and toes. Liquid on the skin causes intense burning and mild redness, and contact with the eyes can burn the cornea. Anyone who works with it needs to treat it with respect.

## Chloroform: from anaesthetic to light-sensitive liquid

$\ce{CHCl3}$ is a good solvent for fats, alkaloids and iodine. Most of the chloroform made today goes into the production of the refrigerant R-22. For a long time it was also a general anaesthetic in surgery, but safer and less toxic anaesthetics, such as ether, have taken its place. Its vapour depresses the central nervous system, so a short exposure can bring on dizziness, fatigue and headache. Long exposure can damage the liver, which converts chloroform into phosgene, and the kidneys. Repeated skin contact can produce sores.

The storage story is the part that surprises most students. Air slowly oxidises chloroform in the presence of light, and the product is carbonyl chloride, usually called phosgene, an extremely poisonous gas. Keep the bottle dark, closed and filled to the top, and the air never gets a chance to start the reaction.

$$
\ce{2CHCl3 + O2 ->[\text{air, light}] 2COCl2 + 2HCl}
$$

## Iodoform: an antiseptic that works through iodine

$\ce{CHI3}$, called triiodomethane or iodoform, was once used as an antiseptic. Its antiseptic power, however, does not come from the iodoform molecule itself. The compound releases free iodine, and it is the iodine that kills microbes. Think of iodoform as a delivery van and iodine as the cargo. Iodoform has an objectionable smell, so it has been replaced by other formulations that deliver iodine without that drawback.

## Carbon tetrachloride and the freons

$\ce{CCl4}$, or tetrachloromethane, is made in large quantities. It goes into refrigerants and propellants for aerosol cans, it is a feedstock for chlorofluorocarbons and other chemicals, and it is used as a general solvent. Until the mid 1960s it was also a common cleaning fluid, a degreaser, a home spot remover and a fire extinguisher. There is some evidence that it causes liver cancer in humans. Its everyday effects include dizziness, nausea and vomiting, and it can damage nerve cells permanently. In severe cases it leads to stupor, coma and death, and it can make the heart beat irregularly or stop.

Released into the air, carbon tetrachloride rises into the atmosphere and depletes the ozone layer. Scientists believe that a thinner ozone layer increases human exposure to ultraviolet rays, which can lead to more skin cancer, more eye disease and possibly a disrupted immune system.

The freons are the chlorofluorocarbons of methane and ethane. They are extremely stable, unreactive, non-toxic, non-corrosive and easy to liquefy, which made them ideal for refrigeration and air conditioning. Freon 12, $\ce{CCl2F2}$, is one of the most common. It is made from carbon tetrachloride by the Swarts reaction that you met earlier in this unit. Most freon, even the kind used in a fridge, eventually drifts into the atmosphere and passes through unchanged into the stratosphere. Up there it starts radical chain reactions that upset the natural balance of ozone.

## DDT: an effective insecticide that stays behind

DDT is the first chlorinated organic insecticide. Its chemistry was known early, but its power against insects was discovered only in 1939, by the Swiss chemist Paul Müller, who won the Nobel Prize for it. Its use grew enormously after the Second World War, chiefly against the mosquito that spreads malaria and the lice that carry typhus.

Problems showed up in the late 1940s. Many insect species developed resistance to DDT, and it proved highly toxic to fish. The bigger issue is its chemistry. DDT is stable, and it dissolves in fat. Animals do not break it down quickly, so it is deposited and stored in their fatty tissues. If an animal keeps taking it in at a steady rate, the amount builds up over time. DDT was banned in the United States in 1973, though it is still used in some other parts of the world.

<KeyIdeas>
- Polyhalogen compounds put several halogen atoms on one carbon, and the number and kind of halogen decide what the compound is used for and how it behaves.
- Dichloromethane, chloroform, carbon tetrachloride and the freons have real industrial uses, and their health and environmental effects are part of the same story.
- Chloroform is kept dark, closed and full because air and light turn it into phosgene, a very poisonous gas.
- Iodoform is an antiseptic because it releases free iodine, not because of the molecule itself.
- Freons are stable and unreactive enough to reach the stratosphere, where they start radical chain reactions that upset the ozone balance. DDT is stable and fat-soluble, so it is stored in animal fat and builds up over time.
</KeyIdeas>
