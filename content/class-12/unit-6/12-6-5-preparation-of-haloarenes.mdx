<Hook>
The white crystals in some mothballs are a benzene ring carrying two chlorine atoms. Benzene barely reacts with chlorine when the two are simply mixed, so how does a chemist attach a chlorine atom to a ring? And once it is attached, what decides which carbon of the ring it lands on?
</Hook>

## A helper for the chlorine

Benzene's ring is stable, and a plain mixture of benzene and chlorine gas does very little. The reaction needs a Lewis acid, a substance that accepts electrons. Iron filings work well, because iron reacts with chlorine to make iron(III) chloride in the flask, and iron(III) chloride can also be added directly.

The Lewis acid grips one chlorine atom of Cl₂ and leaves it short of electrons. The ring's π cloud attacks that chlorine, a hydrogen leaves the ring, and the aromatic character returns. The catalyst is released to act again. The result is an aryl chloride:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + Cl2 ->[FeCl3] C6H5Cl + HCl}
$$

Bromine works the same way, with the same Lewis acid catalysts, iron or iron(III) chloride. Fluorine is different, and we come back to it below.

## Where the second chlorine lands

Put a methyl group on the ring and the choice of position is no longer obvious, so recall from Class 11 that a methyl group directs an incoming electrophile to the ortho and para positions:

$$
\ce{C6H5CH3 + Cl2 ->[FeCl3] CH3C6H4Cl + HCl}
$$

What you get is a mixture: 1-chloro-2-methylbenzene (the ortho isomer) and 1-chloro-4-methylbenzene (the para isomer).

## Pulling the isomers apart

Ortho and para isomers of a disubstituted ring usually differ a great deal in melting point. The para isomer is symmetrical, so its molecules pack neatly into a crystal and melt at a higher temperature. Cool the mixture and the para isomer crystallises first, and it can be filtered off. Separation is therefore a physical job, not a chemical one.

## Iodine that walks back

Iodine does substitute onto benzene, but the reaction is reversible. The hydrogen iodide that forms pushes the equilibrium back towards the starting materials:

$$
\ce{C6H6 + I2 <=> C6H5I + HI}
$$

To keep iodobenzene building up, the HI has to be removed. An oxidising agent does this, for example nitric acid, which oxidises HI to iodine and so takes it out of the mixture:

$$
\ce{6HI + 2HNO3 -> 3I2 + 2NO + 4H2O}
$$

Periodic acid is another oxidiser that can be used in the same role.

## Fluorine is left out

Fluorine is the most reactive halogen. Put it straight onto an arene and the reaction is too violent to control, so fluoroarenes are not prepared by direct substitution. They need other routes, which this Section does not cover.

## The diazonium route

A primary aromatic amine such as aniline gives a different starting point. Start with the amine in cold aqueous mineral acid (a solution or a slurry is fine), then add sodium nitrite. A diazonium salt forms, with the $\text{N}_2^{+}$ group sitting on the ring. Diazonium salts break down when they warm up, so the salt is made cold and used straight away:

$$
\ce{C6H5NH2 + NaNO2 + 2HCl ->[\text{cold}] C6H5N2+Cl- + NaCl + 2H2O}
$$

Now the nitrogen group is replaced. Pour the diazonium salt into cuprous chloride and the group becomes chlorine. Use cuprous bromide with the bromide salt and it becomes bromine:

$$
\ce{C6H5N2+Cl- ->[\ce{Cu2Cl2}] C6H5Cl + N2}
$$

$$
\ce{C6H5N2+Br- ->[\ce{Cu2Br2}] C6H5Br + N2}
$$

Iodine is the easy case. Potassium iodide alone, stirred into the diazonium salt, is enough to give iodobenzene.

$$
\ce{C6H5N2+Cl- + KI -> C6H5I + N2 + KCl}
$$

  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Toluene and two doors:** Now the ring carries a methyl group. Arrows from the CH₃ push electron density into the ring, and the ortho and para carbons glow with extra electrons.   </Step>
  <Step>
    **Pulling them apart:** Cool the mixture in a flask. The symmetrical para isomer, which packs neatly, begins to crystallise and collects as a solid. The ortho isomer stays a liquid. Pour the cold mixture through a filter: the solid para isomer stays on the paper, and the liquid ortho isomer runs through into the filtrate.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Iodine walks back:** Benzene and I₂ meet in a double-headed arrow. Iodobenzene and HI form, and the HI bubbles up and pushes the equilibrium back. Now add nitric acid. The HI is oxidised to I₂, which returns to the mixture, and the arrow tilts towards iodobenzene.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Fluorine is crossed out:** F₂ approaches the benzene ring and a bold cross appears over the route, with a flash of energy at the ring that is too large to control. Direct fluorination of an arene is too violent to control, so the panel shows no fluoroarene product. Fluoroarenes need other routes, outside this Section.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Aniline becomes a diazonium salt:** Aniline sits in a cold flask with HCl and NaNO₂. The NH₂ group turns into an N₂⁺ group on the ring. Next the salt meets Cu₂Cl₂ and the N₂⁺ group leaves as N₂ gas, with chlorobenzene left behind. With Cu₂Br₂ and a bromide salt the product is bromobenzene. With KI alone, and no copper, the product is iodobenzene.
  </Step>

<KeyIdeas>
- A Lewis acid such as FeCl₃ polarises Cl₂ or Br₂ so that the benzene ring can attack, which is how aryl chlorides and bromides are made directly from the arene.
- Ortho and para isomers can be separated because their melting points differ widely. Cooling often crystallises the symmetrical para isomer first.
- Iodination is reversible, so HI has to be removed by an oxidiser such as HNO₃ or HIO₄.
- Fluorine is too reactive for direct substitution, so fluoroarenes are not made this way.
- Sandmeyer's reaction turns ArNH₂ into a diazonium salt in cold mineral acid with NaNO₂. Cu₂Cl₂ or Cu₂Br₂ gives ArCl or ArBr, and KI alone gives ArI.
</KeyIdeas>
