<Hook>
Warm a bromoalkane with potassium hydroxide dissolved in alcohol, and a gas bubbles out. Lead that gas into bromine water and the reddish-brown colour fades within seconds. The bromoalkane had no double bond to begin with, so where did the double bond come from?
</Hook>

## Losing H and X from neighbouring carbons

Start by naming the carbons. The carbon that carries the halogen is the α-carbon. A carbon directly bonded to it is a β-carbon, and the hydrogens sitting on a β-carbon are the β-hydrogens.

Heat the haloalkane with alcoholic potassium hydroxide and two things happen at once. The base pulls off a β-hydrogen, the electrons that held that hydrogen shift into a new π bond between the α- and β-carbons, and the halide leaves from the α-carbon, carrying away the electron pair of the C–X bond. What you are left with is an alkene. Because the hydrogen was removed from the β-carbon, chemists call this β-elimination, or dehydrohalogenation, meaning the loss of HX.

## When two alkenes are possible

Many haloalkanes have β-hydrogens on more than one carbon, so more than one alkene could form. Usually one of them wins out. In 1875 the Russian chemist Alexander Zaitsev, whose name is also spelt Saytzeff, put the pattern into words. The alkene that usually dominates is the one whose double bond carries more alkyl groups.

Test the rule on 2-bromopentane. The bromine sits on C2, so C2 is the α-carbon. Its β-carbons are C1, which has three hydrogens, and C3, which has two. Removing a hydrogen from C3 gives pent-2-ene, with an alkyl group on each carbon of the double bond. Removing one from C1 gives pent-1-ene, with only one. The rule picks pent-2-ene, and the reason is stability: the more substituted alkene is the more stable one. The hydrogen count points the other way, since pent-1-ene has three β-hydrogens to offer against two, yet it still comes second.

$$
\ce{CH3CHBrCH2CH2CH3 + KOH ->[\text{alcoholic}][\Delta] CH3CH=CHCH2CH3 + KBr + H2O}
$$

For this reaction the mixture is 81 % pent-2-ene and 19 % pent-1-ene. Those two figures belong to 2-bromopentane alone; they are not a general table for every bromide.

## Substitution or elimination?

When a haloalkane with β-hydrogens meets a base or nucleophile, it can be substituted (SN1 or SN2) or it can lose HX. Three things decide which path wins: the structure of the halide itself, how strong and how bulky the attacking species is, and the reaction conditions.

Size matters most at the crowded end. A bulky reagent finds it easier to grab a proton on the outside of the molecule than to squeeze in beside a tetravalent carbon, so bulk pushes the reaction towards elimination. Read the class of the halide as a first guide. Primary halides lean towards SN2. Secondary halides sit in the middle, and the strength of the base is what pushes them one way. Tertiary halides go down the SN1 or elimination road, and two things separate those: how stable the carbocation is, and whether a more substituted alkene can form.

2-Bromo-2-methylpropane has nine β-hydrogens, all on three equivalent methyl groups. They can only give one alkene, 2-methylprop-1-ene, so the choice here is between that path and substitution rather than between two alkenes.

## Carbon bonded to a metal

Put bromoethane on magnesium turnings in dry ether and the C–Br bond is replaced by a C–Mg bond. Compounds that carry a carbon–metal bond are organometallic, and most organic chlorides, bromides and iodides can be turned into them this way. The most useful family is the alkylmagnesium halide, RMgX. Victor Grignard found these reagents in 1900 and shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912. For bromoethane the equation is

$$
\ce{CH3CH2Br + Mg ->[\text{dry ether}] CH3CH2MgBr}
$$

Inside the reagent the carbon–magnesium bond is covalent but strongly polar, because carbon draws electrons away from the electropositive metal. The magnesium–halogen bond is essentially ionic. The carbon end is electron-rich and reacts with any proton donor, such as water, an alcohol or an amine:

$$
\ce{CH3CH2MgBr + H2O -> CH3CH3 + Mg(OH)Br}
$$

That sensitivity is why the work is done in dry glassware and dry ether: a trace of moisture destroys the reagent. It is also useful, since it gives a route from a halide to a hydrocarbon.

## Wurtz coupling

Sodium takes a different route. Two alkyl halides that react with it in dry ether couple their alkyl groups together, so the hydrocarbon that comes out carries double the carbons of the starting halide. Bromoethane gives butane:

$$
\ce{2CH3CH2Br + 2Na ->[\text{dry ether}] CH3CH2CH2CH3 + 2NaBr}
$$

This is the Wurtz reaction. Picture two halves of a carbon chain finding each other while the halogens and sodium form the salt.

<KeyIdeas>
- Elimination removes a hydrogen from the β-carbon and the halogen from the α-carbon, and a new π bond forms between them. This is β-elimination, or dehydrohalogenation.
- When more than one alkene is possible, Zaitsev's rule picks the one with more alkyl groups on the double bond. For 2-bromopentane, pent-2-ene (81 %) beats pent-1-ene (19 %).
- A bulky base prefers to take a proton. A primary halide favours $\mathrm{S_N2}$, and a tertiary halide goes by $\mathrm{S_N1}$ or elimination.
- Grignard reagents $\ce{RMgX}$ are made in dry ether. Any proton source turns them into the hydrocarbon $\ce{RH}$, so they must be kept dry.
- The Wurtz reaction joins two alkyl groups with sodium, $\ce{2RX + 2Na -> R–R + 2NaX}$, giving a hydrocarbon with twice the carbons of the halide.
</KeyIdeas>
