<Hook>
Pour chloroform into a glass of water and it sinks to the bottom as a separate, heavy layer, even though a water molecule and a chloroform molecule are both small. Why doesn't it mix, and why does it sink?
</Hook>

## Looks and smell

A pure haloalkane is colourless, whether it is a liquid or a gas. Many haloalkanes develop colour when they are exposed to light. Many of the volatile members have a sweet smell.

## Why the boiling points climb

Put a halogen on a hydrocarbon and two things change. The molecule becomes polar, because the carbon–halogen bond pulls electron density towards the halogen and leaves a permanent dipole. It also becomes heavier, with more electrons to attract its neighbours. Both changes strengthen the attractions between molecules, which are dipole–dipole forces and van der Waals forces. More energy is then needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point rises.

The effect is large. Methane boils at 112 K, and chloromethane boils at 249 K. Ethane boils at 185 K, and chloroethane at 285 K. The rise is not a small correction; it changes which compounds are gases at ordinary temperatures. At 298 K, roughly room temperature, chloromethane, bromomethane and chloroethane are gases, and so are the two fluorides, fluoromethane and fluoroethane. The heavier halides are liquids or solids.

## The heavier halogen boils higher

For one alkyl group, the boiling points fall in the order RI > RBr > RCl > RF. A larger halogen atom carries more electrons, and its electron cloud is easier to distort, so the van der Waals attractions grow as you go down the halogens. The polarity does not rise in step, so size is the main reason for this order. The bars in the animation below show it for methyl, ethyl and n-propyl halides.

## Shape lowers the boiling point

Branching works the other way. A branched molecule is more compact, so less of its surface touches its neighbours, the van der Waals contacts are fewer, and the boiling point drops. Among the three isomers of C₄H₉Br, the straight-chain 1-bromobutane boils at 375 K, the branched 2-bromobutane at 364 K, and the most compact one, 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, at 346 K.

## Isomers on a ring

Dichlorobenzenes give a neat contrast. The ortho, meta and para isomers boil within 7 K of each other, at 446 K to 453 K. Their melting points differ much more. The para isomer melts at 323 K, far above the ortho isomer at 256 K and the meta isomer at 249 K. A para molecule is symmetrical, so its molecules pack into a crystal lattice more tightly, and the solid needs more heat to come apart.

## Density

Bromo, iodo and polychloro compounds are heavier than water. Density rises with the number of carbon atoms, with the number of halogen atoms, and with the atomic mass of the halogen. A single chlorine on a propyl chain is the exception: n-propyl chloride, at 0.89 g mL⁻¹, floats, while n-propyl bromide at 1.335 and n-propyl iodide at 1.747 both sink. Among the chloromethanes, dichloromethane is 1.336 g mL⁻¹, trichloromethane 1.489 and tetrachloromethane 1.595.

## Solubility

Pour a haloalkane into water and it stays apart as its own layer, because very little of it goes into solution. An energy ledger explains why. Making room for a haloalkane molecule in water means breaking hydrogen bonds between water molecules, and that costs energy. When the haloalkane meets water, the new contacts it forms are weaker than the bonds it displaced, so less energy comes back than was spent. The account does not balance, and little dissolves.

Change the solvent to an organic liquid and the ledger balances. The attractions broken in the haloalkane and in the solvent are about as strong as the ones formed between the two, so nothing holds the liquids apart and they mix. Chloroform is the everyday case: it is only very slightly soluble in water, which is why it stays as its own dense layer there.

<KeyIdeas>
- A carbon–halogen bond makes a molecule polar and heavier, which strengthens its dipole–dipole and van der Waals attractions, so haloalkanes boil well above their parent alkanes.
- For one alkyl group the boiling points fall in the order RI > RBr > RCl > RF, because larger halogen atoms give stronger van der Waals forces.
- Branching makes a molecule more compact and lowers its boiling point, so 2-bromo-2-methylpropane boils lowest of the C₄H₉Br isomers.
- Dichlorobenzene isomers boil at nearly the same temperature, but the symmetrical para isomer melts highest because it packs best in a crystal.
- Bromo, iodo and polychloro compounds are denser than water and sink, while they dissolve only very slightly, since the new attractions with water release less energy than the hydrogen bonds they replace.
</KeyIdeas>
