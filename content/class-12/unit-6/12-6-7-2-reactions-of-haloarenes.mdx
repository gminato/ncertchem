<Hook>
Chlorobenzene will not become phenol until you heat it to 623 K under 300 atmospheres. Yet a benzene ring carrying a chlorine and three nitro groups gives up that chlorine to warm water. Which feature of the ring is doing the work, and why does its position matter so much?
</Hook>

## A ring that resists the usual attack

Haloalkanes give way to nucleophiles fairly easily. A halogen on a benzene ring behaves differently, because four features of the ring work against substitution.

First, the lone pairs on the halogen overlap with the π electrons of the ring. Drawn as resonance forms, some electron density moves into the ring and a double bond appears between the ring carbon and the halogen. The C–X bond therefore has some double-bond character, and a bond with that character is harder to break.

Second, the carbon bearing the halogen is sp² hybridised, not sp³. An sp² carbon has more s character, so it pulls the shared pair closer and the bond is shorter. In the comparison NCERT makes, a C–Cl bond is about 169 pm on a ring and about 177 pm on an sp³ carbon.

Third, the SN1 route is closed. Losing the halide from a haloarene would leave a phenyl cation with its charge in the plane of the ring, where no resonance can spread it out. That cation is too unstable to form.

Fourth, the ring is electron-rich. An incoming nucleophile is also electron-rich, so it is pushed away from the π cloud rather than drawn into it.

## Forcing the change: chlorobenzene to phenol

Chlorobenzene can still be converted, but only under harsh conditions. Heating it with aqueous sodium hydroxide at 623 K and 300 atmospheres gives sodium phenoxide, and acid then releases phenol.

$$
\ce{C6H5Cl + 2NaOH ->[\text{623 K, 300 atm}] C6H5ONa + NaCl + H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{C6H5ONa + H+ -> C6H5OH + Na+}
$$

Nitro groups change this picture. Each one placed ortho or para to the chlorine lowers the temperature needed. One nitro group para to the chlorine, as in 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene, is hydrolysed at 443 K. Two groups, one ortho and one para, as in 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, need only 368 K. Three groups, two ortho and one para, as in 2-chloro-1,3,5-trinitrobenzene, are hydrolysed by warm water. The steps that follow the NaOH are the same: the phenoxide is acidified to the phenol.

## Why only ortho and para help

The nucleophile attacks the carbon that carries the halogen. The ring then holds an extra pair of electrons as a carbanion, and in its resonance forms the negative charge lands on carbons ortho and para to the attacked carbon. A nitro group on one of those carbons takes the charge onto its own nitrogen and oxygen atoms, so the carbanion is much more stable.

Put the nitro group at a meta position and the picture changes. None of the resonance forms places the negative charge on the carbon carrying that nitro group, so the group cannot stabilise the intermediate. A meta nitro group leaves the rate unchanged.

## Electrophiles still go to ortho and para

The ring also reacts with electrophiles, as benzene does, but more slowly and under harsher conditions. The halogen does two jobs at once. Its −I effect pulls electron density out of the ring, which is a deactivating effect. Its lone pairs push electron density into the ring by resonance, and that push is strongest at the ortho and para carbons. The new group therefore enters at those positions.

Nitration of bromobenzene shows this clearly. With concentrated nitric acid in concentrated sulphuric acid, the main product is 1-bromo-4-nitrobenzene, with a smaller amount of 1-bromo-2-nitrobenzene, and the meta isomer is not formed to any significant extent.

$$
\ce{C6H5Br + HNO3 ->[\text{conc. H2SO4}] C6H4BrNO2 + H2O}
$$

Halogenation, sulphonation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation and Friedel–Crafts acylation follow the same rule. Chlorobenzene with chlorine and FeCl₃, for example, gives mainly 1,4-dichlorobenzene with some 1,2-dichlorobenzene. In a Friedel–Crafts alkylation with methyl chloride and anhydrous AlCl₃, the methyl group goes ortho and para to the chlorine:

$$
\ce{C6H5Cl + CH3Cl ->[\text{anhyd. AlCl3}] C7H7Cl + HCl}
$$

## Couplings with sodium

Haloarenes also couple with sodium in dry ether. A haloarene and a haloalkane give an alkylarene in the Wurtz–Fittig reaction. Chlorobenzene with chloroethane gives ethylbenzene:

$$
\ce{C6H5Cl + C2H5Cl + 2Na ->[\text{dry ether}] C6H5C2H5 + 2NaCl}
$$

Two aryl halides couple in the Fittig reaction. Two molecules of bromobenzene give a molecule with two benzene rings joined by a single bond, C₁₂H₁₀:

$$
\ce{2C6H5Br + 2Na ->[\text{dry ether}] C12H10 + 2NaBr}
$$

In both, each carbon–halogen bond breaks and a new carbon–carbon bond forms between the two ring or chain carbons.

<KeyIdeas>
- A C–X bond on a benzene ring has partial double-bond character and sits on an sp² carbon, so it is shorter than an sp³ C–X bond and harder to break; SN1 is ruled out because the phenyl cation cannot be stabilised by resonance.
- Chlorobenzene converts to phenol only under extreme conditions (623 K, 300 atmospheres), and nitro groups ortho or para to the halogen lower the temperature step by step.
- A nitro group at an ortho or para position stabilises the carbanion intermediate by taking the negative charge onto its own atoms; at a meta position it does nothing.
- Halogens are deactivating through their −I effect and ortho and para directing through resonance, so electrophiles give mainly ortho and para products.
- Wurtz–Fittig and Fittig reactions couple carbon fragments with sodium in dry ether, forming a new C–C bond from two C–X bonds.
</KeyIdeas>
