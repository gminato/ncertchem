## Two systems, one bottle

Chemists use two naming systems for halides, and they answer different questions. The common system asks which alkyl group carries the halogen. You name the group first and then add the halide word, so the name tells you what the halogen is attached to. The IUPAC system asks where each atom sits on a carbon skeleton. It treats the halogen as a prefix on a hydrocarbon, and it numbers the skeleton so that the substituents take the smallest numbers possible.

The walk-through below starts with a short molecule that carries both names, builds the systematic name of a branched one, and then handles the two special cases: a benzene ring with two halogens, and a chain with two halogens on it.

<Scrolly animation="halide-naming">
  <Step>
  **Two names, one molecule:** Begin with $\ce{CH3CH2CH(Br)CH3}$, a four-carbon chain with the bromine on its second carbon. The common name starts with the group. The butyl group that carries the bromine is the sec-butyl group, so the compound is sec-butyl bromide. The IUPAC name, 2-bromobutane, puts the bromine on the chain as a prefix and numbers the chain. Both names stay beside the same drawing, so you can see which atom each word refers to.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Find the longest chain:** Now move to a larger molecule, $\ce{CH3CH(Br)CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH3}$. The methyl group on the fourth carbon is a branch, not part of the chain. Trace the longest unbroken run of carbons. It has six, so the parent is hexane. The bromine and the methyl are substituents hanging off that hexane, and the rest of the name depends on where they sit.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Number for the lowest locants:** Count from the left and the bromine lands on carbon 2 while the methyl lands on carbon 4, giving the set {2, 4}. Count from the right and the set becomes {3, 5}. The sets are compared number by number, and 2 is lower than 3, so the count from the left wins. Both counts describe the same molecule. Only the winning set goes into the name.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Assemble the name:** Prefixes go in alphabetical order, so bromo comes before methyl. Each locant sits in front of its prefix, and the parent name closes the word. Put together, the name is 2-bromo-4-methylhexane. Read the numbers and letters from left to right and they follow the carbons of the skeleton in order, which is the quickest way to check a name by eye.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **A ring with two halogens:** Put two chlorines on a benzene ring and three arrangements are possible. Adjacent carbons give the ortho pattern, one carbon between them gives meta, and opposite carbons give para. The IUPAC system writes these as 1,2-, 1,3- and 1,4-dichlorobenzene, numbering the ring from carbon 1. A ring with a single halogen needs no number at all, because its common and IUPAC names are the same.
  </Step>
  <Step>
  **Two halogens on one chain:** In $\ce{CH3CHCl2}$ both chlorines sit on the same carbon, so this is a gem dihalide. Its IUPAC name is 1,1-dichloroethane and its common name is ethylidene chloride. In $\ce{ClCH2CH2Cl}$ the chlorines sit on neighbouring carbons, a vic dihalide, named 1,2-dichloroethane or, commonly, ethylene dichloride. Three rows of Table 6.1 confirm the pattern: methylene chloride is dichloromethane, chloroform is trichloromethane, and allyl bromide is 3-bromoprop-1-ene.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- The common name starts with the alkyl group, as in sec-butyl bromide. The IUPAC name treats the halogen as a prefix on the longest chain or on the ring, as in 2-bromobutane.
- Number the chain so that the set of locants is as low as possible. When two numberings tie, the prefix that comes first in the alphabet takes the lower number.
- For a benzene ring, the common prefixes ortho, meta and para become 1,2, 1,3 and 1,4 in the IUPAC system. A monohalobenzene needs no number.
- Put two chlorines on one carbon, as in $\ce{CH3CHCl2}$, and you have a gem dihalide, which the common system calls ethylidene chloride. Put them on neighbouring carbons, as in $\ce{ClCH2CH2Cl}$, and you have a vic dihalide, commonly ethylene dichloride. IUPAC treats both as dihaloalkanes, and the prefix dihalo- or trihalo- gives the number of halogens.
- Chloroform and trichloromethane name the same compound, so the second bottle is not needed. The same holds for 3-bromopropene and 3-bromoprop-1-ene.
</KeyIdeas>
