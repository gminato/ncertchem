<Hook>
The white PVC pipe under your kitchen sink is a chain of carbon atoms with a chlorine on every second one. The thyroid hormone thyroxine carries iodine on the carbon rings of its structure. The anaesthetic halothane carries bromine and chlorine on the same small molecule. Each one depends on a halogen bonded to carbon. Are all those carbon–halogen bonds the same?
</Hook>

Swap one hydrogen of an alkane for a halogen and you get a haloalkane. Do the same to a benzene ring and you get a haloarene. The two families look alike on paper, but the carbon that carries the halogen differs: in a haloalkane it is sp³, while in a haloarene it is sp². That single difference sets how the C–X bond behaves, and most of this Unit follows from it.

These compounds are everywhere in practice. Some occur naturally, made by microorganisms. Others are built on purpose as solvents for substances that do not mix well with water, and as the raw material for a long list of further syntheses. Some of them also persist in soil and water, because the bacteria there cannot easily break the C–X bond apart. You will come back to that point in the last Section of this Unit.

Start with the simplest sorting, which is a count. One halogen atom makes a mono-halo compound, two make a di-halo compound, and three or more make a poly-halo compound. A count tells you only how many halogens there are, though. To place a compound properly, look at the carbon that carries the halogen, and at the atoms next to it.

<Scrolly animation="halide-classes">
  <Step>
    **One skeleton, many positions.** Begin with a chain of four carbons and a methyl group on the second one: the 2-methylbutane frame. Every carbon in it is sp³, and any hydrogen can be swapped for a halogen. Put bromine on the carbon at the end of the chain. That carbon is bonded to only one other carbon, so the bromide is a primary, or 1°, alkyl halide. The formula is C₅H₁₁Br.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Move the bromine along the frame.** Shift the same bromine to the third carbon of the chain and it is bonded to two other carbons: a secondary, or 2°, alkyl halide. Shift it to the second carbon, the one that already carries the methyl group, and three carbons are attached to it: a tertiary, or 3°, alkyl halide. The atoms are the same in all three. Only the position changes, and the class follows the position.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A double bond next door.** Now take 1-bromobut-2-ene. The bromine sits on a CH₂ group that is joined directly to one end of a C=C double bond. That carbon still has four single bonds around it, so it is sp³, but its neighbour is sp². This is an allylic halide. The name comes from the allyl group, CH₂=CH–CH₂–, which has the same arrangement.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **A benzene ring next door.** Put a benzene ring in the neighbour position instead. In bromophenylmethane, the bromine sits on a CH₂ group that is attached to the ring. That is a benzylic halide, and it is still a primary one here. Add carbons to the same carbon and it becomes secondary in 1-chloro-1-phenylethane and tertiary in 2-bromo-2-phenylpropane. The halogen-bearing carbon stays sp³ in all three, and the ring sits beside it.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Now the halogen meets an sp² carbon.** In 2-chloroprop-1-ene the chlorine is on one of the two carbons of a C=C double bond. That is a vinylic halide. In 1-bromo-3-ethylbenzene the bromine is on a carbon of the ring itself: an aryl halide. Both C–X carbons are sp², but a vinylic carbon belongs to a double bond in a chain, while an aryl carbon belongs to the aromatic ring.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Two halogens, two arrangements.** In 2,2-dichlorobutane both chlorines are on the same carbon, so it is a geminal dihalide, also called an alkylidene halide. In 1,2-dibromo-3-methylbutane the two bromines sit on neighbouring carbons, which makes a vicinal dihalide, or alkylene halide. A compound such as 1,1,1-trichloroethane, with three chlorines on one carbon, belongs to the polyhalogen group instead.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<KeyIdeas>
- A haloalkane has its halogen on an sp³ carbon, and a haloarene has its halogen on an sp² carbon of a benzene ring.
- Sort first by the number of halogen atoms (mono, di, poly), then look at the carbon that carries the halogen.
- For an sp³ C–X carbon, the class follows how many other carbons are attached to it: primary (1°), secondary (2°) or tertiary (3°).
- Allylic halides have X on an sp³ carbon next to C=C, and benzylic halides have X on an sp³ carbon next to a benzene ring. Vinylic and aryl halides have X on an sp² carbon, in a double bond or in the ring.
- Geminal dihalides carry both halogens on one carbon, and vicinal dihalides carry one on each of two adjacent carbons.
</KeyIdeas>
