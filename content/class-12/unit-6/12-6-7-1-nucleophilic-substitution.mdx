<Hook>
One bottle of bromoethane can be turned into an alcohol, an ether, an amine or a nitrile just by changing what you add to it. What do all those reagents have in common?
</Hook>

Haloalkanes react in three main ways: nucleophilic substitution, elimination, and reaction with metals. This Section covers the first, and later Sections take up the other two.

## A carbon the halogen has made electron-poor

Bromoethane, $\ce{CH3CH2Br}$, looks like a simple molecule, but its C–Br bond holds a clue. Bromine is more electronegative than carbon, so the shared electrons lean toward it. The bromine end picks up a little negative charge, $\delta^-$, and the carbon it is attached to is left a little positive, $\delta^+$.

That $\delta^+$ carbon is the target. Reagents that carry a lone pair or a full negative charge are electron-rich, and they are drawn to the electron-poor part of a molecule. When one of them reaches the carbon, the bromide ion leaves, taking the bonding pair with it. The halogen is the leaving group, and the reagent is called a nucleophile, which means "nucleus-loving". Because the attack is started by a nucleophile and it replaces a group already on the molecule, the change is a nucleophilic substitution.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2Br + Nu- -> CH3CH2Nu + Br-}
$$

This swap is the main story for haloalkanes whose halogen sits on an $\text{sp}^3$ carbon, which is the case for the alkyl halides of this Section. The rest of the Section shows how far one substrate can be pushed.

## Reading the reagent table

The reagents you can use on a haloalkane fall into a small set of families. Table 6.4 gathers them: each row gives the reagent, the nucleophile that actually does the attacking, the product, and the class of that product. Hydroxide and water give alcohols. An alkoxide gives an ether. Iodide gives an alkyl iodide and ammonia gives a primary amine. A primary amine used as the nucleophile gives a secondary amine, and a secondary amine gives a tertiary one. Cyanide and isocyanide, nitrite and nitroalkane, a carboxylate giving an ester, the hydride from $\ce{LiAlH4}$ giving a hydrocarbon, and an organometallic reagent joining two alkyl groups make up the rest.

Notice the pattern. The halogen is always replaced, and the nature of the product depends entirely on the reagent. The carbon skeleton of the haloalkane is kept, and a single substrate can be turned into a whole family of compounds.

## Ambident nucleophiles: two atoms, one reagent

Some nucleophiles carry two atoms that can each donate a lone pair. These are called ambident nucleophiles, and they are the reason a single reagent can give two different products. Cyanide is the first example. The cyanide ion is a hybrid of two contributing structures, one with the lone pair on C and one with the lone pair on N. The nitrite ion has the same problem with oxygen and nitrogen.

The metal salt you choose decides which end of the ion attacks. Potassium cyanide, KCN, attacks through carbon and gives the nitrile, with a $\text{C–C}$ bond. Silver cyanide, AgCN, attacks through nitrogen and gives the isocyanide. The same pattern holds for nitrite: potassium nitrite, KNO₂, gives an alkyl nitrite through oxygen, and silver nitrite, AgNO₂, gives a nitroalkane through nitrogen.

<KeyIdeas>
- A haloalkane's carbon bonded to halogen is $\delta^+$, so electron-rich nucleophiles are drawn to it.
- In nucleophilic substitution the nucleophile takes the carbon and the halide leaves as X⁻, the leaving group.
- The reagent sets the product: Table 6.4 pairs each nucleophile with its own product class, while the carbon skeleton stays the same.
- Ambident nucleophiles such as cyanide and nitrite can attack through either of two atoms; KCN and AgNO₂ show the two routes.
- Cyanide through carbon gives a nitrile, $\ce{RCN}$, and through nitrogen gives an isocyanide, $\ce{RNC}$. Nitrite through oxygen gives an alkyl nitrite, and through nitrogen a nitroalkane.
</KeyIdeas>
