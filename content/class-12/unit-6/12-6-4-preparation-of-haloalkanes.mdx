<Hook>
Your practical class needs one clean bottle of a chloroalkane. The cheapest-looking route sits on the shelf already: mix an alkane with chlorine and shine light on it. Yet many laboratories send you to an alcohol instead. Why would a careful chemist take the longer road?
</Hook>

## Starting from an alcohol

An alcohol already carries the carbon skeleton you want. You only swap its –OH group for a halogen, and that swap is where the chemistry gets choosy. Hydrogen halides do it with a little help. Dry HCl needs a zinc chloride catalyst for a primary or secondary alcohol, but a tertiary alcohol such as $\ce{(CH3)3COH}$ simply shaken with concentrated HCl at room temperature changes over. For bromides, constant-boiling 48 % HBr heated with the alcohol works well. Warming an alcohol with sodium or potassium iodide in 95 % orthophosphoric acid is a route to iodoalkanes, because the acid supplies the hydrogen iodide the reaction needs.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + H2O}
$$

Whichever acid you use, a tertiary alcohol reacts fastest, a secondary one next and a primary one slowest. Keep that order in mind. It is the same carbocation stability that decides SN1 reactions later in this unit.

Phosphorus halides come next. The tribromide and triiodide are normally prepared on the spot, with red phosphorus combined with bromine or iodine in the same flask. Phosphorus pentachloride also converts an alcohol to a chloroalkane, but it leaves a liquid by-product that must be separated. Thionyl chloride, $\ce{SOCl2}$, is the preferred reagent. Its by-products, $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{HCl}$, are gases that escape, so the chloroalkane left behind is pure.

Alcohol routes therefore cannot convert a phenol into an aryl halide. The oxygen's lone pair feeds into the ring, giving the C–O link some double-bond character that the reagents cannot easily overcome.

## Starting from a hydrocarbon

Alkanes react with chlorine or bromine in light through free radicals, but nothing stops the radicals from hitting every kind of C–H bond. The product is a mixture of isomeric mono- and polyhalogenated compounds. Pulling one isomer out of that blend is laborious, so the usable yield of any single compound is poor. This route is useful only when the mixture does not matter.

Alkenes give cleaner products. Hydrogen halides add across the double bond, and with an unsymmetrical alkene the product follows Markovnikov's rule: the hydrogen goes to the carbon that already carries more hydrogens, and the halogen goes to the other carbon. Bromine adds across the double bond in the same way, giving a vicinal dibromide. Because the reddish-brown bromine is used up, its colour fades. That fading is a quick test for a double bond.

## Swapping one halogen for another

Warming a chloroalkane or a bromoalkane with sodium iodide in dry acetone swaps in iodine; this is the Finkelstein reaction. The sodium chloride or sodium bromide cannot stay dissolved in dry acetone, so it drops out, and removing it keeps the reaction moving forward (Le Chatelier).

Fluorides are made the other way round. The Swarts reaction is the route to fluorides: a chloro- or bromoalkane is warmed with one of the metal fluorides $\ce{AgF}$, $\ce{Hg2F2}$, $\ce{CoF2}$ or $\ce{SbF3}$, and fluorine takes the halogen's place. Fluorine gas itself is far too reactive to use this way.

<Scrolly animation="haloalkane-routes">
  <Step>
    **Alcohol and thionyl chloride:** Begin with butan-1-ol, a primary alcohol. Add thionyl chloride. The –OH group is replaced by chlorine and the product is 1-chlorobutane. The sulphur dioxide and hydrogen chloride formed leave the flask as gases, so the chloroalkane is the only thing left to collect.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Alkane and chlorine in light:** Now start from butane, which has no functional group at all. Add chlorine and shine light on the flask. The products are a family of isomeric chloroalkanes, 1-chlorobutane and 2-chlorobutane among them, together with hydrogen chloride. Separating these isomers is laborious, so any single one you want comes out in a poor yield.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Alkene and hydrogen bromide:** But-1-ene is an unsymmetrical alkene. Hydrogen bromide adds across its double bond. The hydrogen attaches to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and the bromine attaches to the second carbon. The major product is 2-bromobutane, as Markovnikov's rule predicts.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Alkene and bromine in solvent:** Add bromine dissolved in carbon tetrachloride to the same alkene. One bromine atom joins each carbon of the double bond, giving 1,2-dibromobutane, which is colourless. The reddish-brown colour of the bromine disappears as it is used up. That disappearance shows the double bond is present.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Finkelstein exchange:** Take 1-bromobutane and add sodium iodide in dry acetone. Iodide takes the place of bromide and forms 1-iodobutane. Sodium bromide does not dissolve in dry acetone, so it forms a solid that settles at the bottom, and the forward reaction is pulled along.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Swarts exchange:** Heat 1-bromobutane with silver fluoride. Fluoride takes the place of bromide to give 1-fluorobutane. The silver bromide formed is an insoluble solid that stays behind in the flask. Metal fluorides are the practical way to reach a fluoroalkane from a bromoalkane.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- Alcohols give clean haloalkanes with HX, PX₃ or SOCl₂. The reactivity order with HX is tertiary, then secondary, then primary.
- SOCl₂ is preferred for chlorides because its by-products, SO₂ and HCl, are gases that escape.
- Free radical halogenation of an alkane gives a mixture of isomers, so the yield of any single product is low.
- Markovnikov's rule places the halogen on the carbon that carries fewer hydrogens in an unsymmetrical alkene.
- Halogen exchange works by removing a salt: NaCl or NaBr precipitates in dry acetone (Finkelstein), and metal fluorides such as AgF give fluoroalkanes (Swarts).
</KeyIdeas>
