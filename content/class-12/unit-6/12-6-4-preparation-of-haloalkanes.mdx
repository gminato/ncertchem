<Hook>
Your practical class needs one clean bottle of a chloroalkane. The cheapest-looking route sits on the shelf already: mix an alkane with chlorine and shine light on it. Yet most laboratories send you to an alcohol instead. Why would a careful chemist take the longer road?
</Hook>

## Starting from an alcohol

An alcohol already carries the carbon skeleton you want. You only swap its –OH group for a halogen, and that swap is where the chemistry gets choosy. Hydrogen halides do it with a little help. Dry HCl needs a zinc chloride catalyst for a primary or secondary alcohol, but a tertiary alcohol such as $\ce{(CH3)3COH}$ simply shaken with concentrated HCl at room temperature changes over. For bromides, constant-boiling 48 % HBr heated with the alcohol works well. Iodides come from heating an alcohol with sodium or potassium iodide in 95 % orthophosphoric acid.

$$
\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + H2O}
$$

With any one acid, the order of reactivity is tertiary, then secondary, then primary alcohol. Keep that order in mind. It is the same carbocation stability that decides SN1 reactions later in this unit.

Phosphorus halides are the other classic reagents. Phosphorus tribromide and triiodide are usually made in the flask itself, from red phosphorus with bromine or iodine. Phosphorus pentachloride also converts an alcohol to a chloroalkane, but it leaves a liquid by-product that must be separated. Thionyl chloride, $\ce{SOCl2}$, is the preferred reagent. Its by-products, $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{HCl}$, are gases that escape, so the chloroalkane left behind is pure.

These alcohol routes do not work for aryl halides. In a phenol the carbon–oxygen bond has partial double-bond character, and it is much harder to break than a single bond.

## Starting from a hydrocarbon

Alkanes react with chlorine or bromine in light through free radicals, but nothing stops the radicals from hitting every kind of C–H bond. The product is a mixture of isomeric mono- and polyhalogenated compounds. The mixture is hard to separate, so the yield of any one of them is low. This route is useful only when the mixture does not matter.

Alkenes give cleaner products. Hydrogen halides add across the double bond, and with an unsymmetrical alkene the product follows Markovnikov's rule: the hydrogen goes to the carbon that already carries more hydrogens, and the halogen goes to the other carbon. Bromine adds across the double bond in the same way, giving a vicinal dibromide. Because the reddish-brown bromine is used up, its colour fades. That fading is a quick test for a double bond.

## Swapping one halogen for another

A chloride or a bromide can be turned into an iodide by heating it with sodium iodide in dry acetone. This is the Finkelstein reaction. The sodium chloride or sodium bromide formed is insoluble in dry acetone, so it settles out of the solution, and that removal pulls the reaction forward by Le Chatelier's principle.

Fluorides are made the other way round. Heating a chloride or bromide with a metallic fluoride such as $\ce{AgF}$, $\ce{Hg2F2}$, $\ce{CoF2}$ or $\ce{SbF3}$ is the Swarts reaction. Fluorine gas itself is far too reactive to use this way.

<Scrolly animation="haloalkane-routes">
  <Step>
    **Alcohol and thionyl chloride:** Begin with butan-1-ol, a primary alcohol. Add thionyl chloride. The –OH group is replaced by chlorine and the product is 1-chlorobutane. The sulphur dioxide and hydrogen chloride formed leave the flask as gases, so the chloroalkane is the only thing left to collect.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Alkane and bromine in light:** Now start from butane, which has no functional group at all. Add bromine and shine light on the flask. The reaction gives a spread of products, 1-bromobutane and 2-bromobutane among them, with hydrogen bromide. No single product dominates, and the mixture is hard to separate.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Alkene and hydrogen bromide:** But-1-ene is an unsymmetrical alkene. Hydrogen bromide adds across its double bond. The hydrogen attaches to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and the bromine attaches to the middle carbon. The major product is 2-bromobutane, as Markovnikov's rule predicts.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Alkene and bromine in solvent:** Add bromine dissolved in carbon tetrachloride to the same alkene. One bromine atom joins each carbon of the double bond, giving 1,2-dibromobutane, which is colourless. The reddish-brown colour of the bromine disappears as it is used up. That disappearance shows the double bond is present.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Finkelstein exchange:** Take 1-bromobutane and add sodium iodide in dry acetone. Iodide takes the place of bromide and forms 1-iodobutane. Sodium bromide does not dissolve in dry acetone, so it forms a solid that settles at the bottom, and the forward reaction is pulled along.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    **Swarts exchange:** Heat 1-bromobutane with silver fluoride. Fluoride takes the place of bromide to give 1-fluorobutane. The silver bromide formed is an insoluble solid that stays behind in the flask. Metal fluorides are the practical way to reach a fluoroalkane from a bromoalkane.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

## Key ideas

<KeyIdeas>
- Alcohols give clean haloalkanes with HX, PX₃ or SOCl₂. The reactivity order with HX is tertiary, then secondary, then primary.
- SOCl₂ is preferred for chlorides because its by-products, SO₂ and HCl, are gases that escape.
- Free radical halogenation of an alkane gives a mixture of isomers, so the yield of any single product is low.
- Markovnikov's rule places the halogen on the carbon that carries fewer hydrogens in an unsymmetrical alkene.
- Halogen exchange works by removing a salt: NaCl or NaBr precipitates in dry acetone (Finkelstein), and metal fluorides such as AgF give fluoroalkanes (Swarts).
</KeyIdeas>
